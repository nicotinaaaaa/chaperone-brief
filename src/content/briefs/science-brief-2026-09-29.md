This brief covers biology and biotech/pharma news from roughly the past two weeks: new discoveries in the life sciences, clinical trial results and regulatory decisions, and biotech business news. Every disease and technical term is explained the first time it appears — look for the plain-English gloss right after the term.

A few terms come up so often it's worth defining them once, here, instead of every time: a **clinical trial** tests a drug in people, in three main stages before approval. **Phase 1** checks a small group (tens of people) mainly for safety and dosing. **Phase 2** tests a somewhat larger group (dozens to a few hundred) for early signs the drug actually works, alongside more safety data. **Phase 3** is the large, often randomized, placebo-controlled trial (hundreds to thousands of patients) that regulators use to decide whether to approve a drug — this is the stage that carries the most weight. "**Topline results**" means the company has announced the headline outcome before the full, peer-reviewed data have been published or presented in detail. A "**primary endpoint**" is the single main measurement a trial is designed to prove; "meeting" it means the drug beat placebo (or the comparison treatment) by a statistically meaningful margin on that specific measure.

## 1. Clinical Trials, Approvals, and Regulatory Decisions

### New approvals expand treatment options across several diseases

The FDA approved a cluster of new drugs and label expansions in the past two weeks, spanning a mix of common and rare conditions.

**AbbVie's tavapadon wins approval for Parkinson's disease.** Parkinson's disease is a progressive brain disorder in which nerve cells that produce dopamine (a chemical messenger the brain uses to coordinate smooth, controlled movement) gradually die off, causing tremor, stiffness, slowness of movement, and balance problems; it mostly affects people over 60 and worsens over years to decades. Tavapadon is a "D1/D5 dopamine receptor partial agonist" — a drug that partially activates two of the brain's dopamine-receiving proteins, mimicking dopamine's effect without fully replacing it — and it's now approved both on its own and alongside the older standard drug levodopa. It's notable as one of the few genuinely new oral mechanisms for Parkinson's approved in years, rather than another variation on levodopa. *Source: LifeScienceHistory / wire coverage, Sept 25, 2026. Stage: newly market-approved.*

**Mirum Pharmaceuticals' Atebrioz approved for fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP).** FOP is an ultra-rare genetic disease in which the body's normal soft-tissue repair process misfires: muscle, tendon, and connective tissue are gradually and irreversibly replaced by extra bone, often triggered by minor injury, progressively locking joints in place. It's severe and currently has almost no treatment options. This is the first approval in the category and sets up direct competition with a rival program from Regeneron. *Source: Endpoints News / FiercePharma, Sept 25, 2026. Stage: newly market-approved.*

**Merck and Eisai's Welireg–Lenvima combination approved for kidney cancer.** Renal cell carcinoma is the most common form of kidney cancer, arising from the cells that filter blood in the kidney's tubules; when advanced, it's treated with a mix of targeted drugs and immunotherapy. Welireg (belzutifan) is a "HIF-2α inhibitor" — it blocks a protein that many kidney cancers depend on to survive low-oxygen conditions inside a tumor — and it's now approved in combination with the already-established drug Lenvima for certain previously treated patients. Belzutifan is one of Merck's key next-generation growth drugs as its blockbuster immunotherapy Keytruda approaches patent expiration. *Source: Merck press release / FiercePharma, Sept 24, 2026. Stage: market-approved (label expansion).*

**Eli Lilly's Onswik approved as a once-weekly basal insulin for type 2 diabetes.** Type 2 diabetes is a metabolic condition in which the body becomes resistant to insulin (the hormone that moves sugar from the blood into cells) and/or doesn't produce enough of it, leading to chronically high blood sugar that can damage nerves, kidneys, eyes, and blood vessels over time. "Basal insulin" is the slow, steady background dose (as opposed to fast-acting mealtime insulin) that many patients currently have to inject daily; Onswik is designed to replace that with a single weekly injection — roughly 300 fewer injections a year. It's a direct challenge to Novo Nordisk's own once-weekly insulin. *Source: Eli Lilly investor release, Sept 2026. Stage: newly market-approved.*

**Elevar Therapeutics' Lyrfigtu approved for a rare bile duct cancer.** Cholangiocarcinoma is cancer of the bile ducts, the tubes that carry digestive fluid from the liver to the intestine; it's uncommon, often caught late, and historically has few effective drug options. Lyrfigtu treats patients whose tumors carry an "FGFR2 fusion or rearrangement" — a specific DNA scrambling event that fuses the FGFR2 growth-signaling gene to another gene, driving the cancer's growth and making it a target for drugs that block FGFR2 specifically. It's the first FDA approval for Elevar, a South Korea–headquartered biotech. *Source: GlobeNewswire / FiercePharma, Sept 23, 2026. Stage: newly market-approved (basis of approval — accelerated vs. standard — was not fully confirmed in sourcing).*

**An FDA advisory panel backs GRAIL's Galleri blood test for detecting multiple cancers at once.** A "multi-cancer early detection" (MCED) test is a single blood draw designed to screen for signs of many different cancers simultaneously, rather than one test per cancer type (like a mammogram for breast cancer or a colonoscopy for colon cancer), by detecting fragments of tumor DNA circulating in the blood. An FDA advisory committee — outside experts who vote on a recommendation, though the agency doesn't have to follow it — voted in favor of the Galleri test, while cautioning it should supplement, not replace, existing guideline-recommended cancer screenings. A final FDA decision is still pending; if it comes through, it would be the first approval of its kind. *Source: STAT News / NPR, Sept 23, 2026. Stage: regulatory decision pending (advisory committee vote only).*

### Late-stage trial results: two wins for kidney disease, one for an untreatable autoimmune condition, and a safety scare

**Two separate drugs post strong Phase 3 data in IgA nephropathy.** IgA nephropathy is an autoimmune kidney disease in which a specific antibody (immunoglobulin A) forms clumps that deposit in the kidney's filtering units, slowly damaging them; over years it can lead to kidney failure, and it's one of the most common causes of kidney disease in young adults worldwide. Ionis and its partner Roche announced positive interim results from a large Phase 3 trial of sefaxersen, an "antisense" drug — a synthetic snippet of genetic material designed to block a specific piece of RNA and reduce production of the disease-driving antibody at its source, rather than treating symptoms downstream. Separately, Vera Therapeutics released two-year final Phase 3 data showing its drug Trutakna (atacicept, already on the market under a conditional "accelerated approval" — a faster approval track granted on an early biological marker, with confirmatory data required afterward to keep it) stabilized kidney function long-term; Vera plans to file for full approval based on this data. *Sources: Ionis Pharmaceuticals press release, Sept 23, 2026; STAT News, Sept 15, 2026. Stage: Phase 3 (one interim, one final/confirmatory).*

**Amgen's dazodalibep succeeds in the first-ever Phase 3 win for Sjögren's disease.** Sjögren's disease is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the body's moisture-producing glands, most often causing severe dry eyes and dry mouth, along with fatigue and joint pain; it's far more common in women and ranges from mildly annoying to significantly disabling. Until now, there have been no FDA-approved drugs that treat the underlying disease rather than just the dryness symptoms. In a Phase 3 trial, dazodalibep produced a statistically significant improvement on a standard disease-activity scale (ESSDAI, a composite score doctors use to rate how active the disease is across multiple organ systems) versus placebo, with benefit appearing within a month. *Source: Amgen press release, Sept 22, 2026. Stage: Phase 3 (topline).*

**Celldex's chronic hives drug hits its targets — but a rare, serious side effect spooked investors.** Chronic spontaneous urticaria is a condition causing recurring hives (itchy welts on the skin) for six weeks or longer with no identifiable trigger; it can significantly disrupt sleep and daily life and sometimes lasts for years. Celldex's drug barzolvolimab met its goals in two Phase 3 trials, with about half of patients achieving a complete response versus 15–18% on placebo — data one analyst called among the best seen in the category. But two patients out of roughly 2,400 treated experienced anaphylaxis, a sudden, severe, potentially life-threatening allergic reaction that can cause the throat to swell and blood pressure to crash. That safety signal, not the efficacy data, is what moved the stock. *Source: BioPharma Dive, Sept 22, 2026. Stage: Phase 3. Stock: Celldex (CLDX) fell roughly 13% on the announcement.*

**A gene therapy meets its goal in a rare inherited form of blindness.** X-linked retinitis pigmentosa is an inherited eye disease, passed down through a gene on the X chromosome (so it affects boys and men far more often and more severely), in which the light-sensing cells of the retina slowly degenerate, causing progressive vision loss starting in childhood or adolescence. "Gene therapy" here means delivering a working copy of the affected gene directly into the eye's cells to replace or supplement the faulty one. Beacon Therapeutics' gene therapy, laru-zova, met its main goal in the pivotal trial — a milestone in a disease that currently has no approved treatments. *Source: BioPharma Dive, Sept 21, 2026. Stage: pivotal trial (Phase 3–equivalent).*

**A depression trial pause sends a biotech's stock down about 30%.** Xenon Pharmaceuticals voluntarily paused enrollment in Phase 3 trials testing its drug azetukalner for depression after "neuropsychiatric adverse events" emerged — described as mild-to-moderate and reversible episodes affecting mental state or behavior — that hadn't shown up in earlier, smaller trials. The same drug is separately under FDA review for epilepsy (seizure disorders), and it's unclear whether that review will be affected. *Source: BioPharma Dive / FierceBiotech, Sept 18, 2026. Stage: Phase 3 (paused). Stock: Xenon (XENE) fell roughly 29–31%.*

**Viking Therapeutics' obesity drug shows people can keep weight off with far less frequent dosing.** Most current obesity drugs in this class (GLP-1 receptor agonists — drugs that mimic a gut hormone which signals fullness to the brain and slows digestion) require weekly injections indefinitely. In a Phase 2 trial, patients who switched from weekly injections to dosing every two weeks or once a month retained most of their weight loss (about 90% and 85% retention, respectively) over a further 12 weeks — addressing a real-world adherence problem in a hot, competitive drug category. *Source: BioPharma Dive, Sept 22, 2026. Stage: Phase 2. Stock: Viking (VKTX) rose as much as 36% in morning trading.*

## 2. New Scientific Discoveries

### Brain wiring, aging, and disease

**A protein "workspace" solves the mystery of how neurons manage genes that stretch for millions of DNA letters.** Some genes essential for building brain circuits are unusually long — up to two million DNA base pairs, versus a typical human gene's tens of thousands — and reading (transcribing) them accurately is a major logistical challenge for the cell. Researchers found that a protein called SFPQ forms "liquid condensates" — droplet-like clusters of proteins and RNA that behave a bit like oil separating in water, concentrating specific molecular machinery in one spot without a surrounding membrane — that co-locate the machinery needed to transcribe these extra-long genes correctly. Problems with SFPQ have been linked to ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a progressive disease that destroys the nerve cells controlling voluntary muscles, eventually causing paralysis) and autism spectrum disorder, a developmental condition affecting communication, social interaction, and behavior, so understanding this mechanism could shed light on both. *Source: News-Medical.net, Sept 15, 2026, covering a study in Cell Chemical Biology. Peer-reviewed.*

**Scientists show the brain actually develops as two separate organs fused together, not one.** By comparing embryonic development across mice, chickens, zebrafish (a small fish widely used as a lab model because its embryos develop outside the mother and are transparent), acorn worms, and jellyfish, researchers found that the front part of the brain (forebrain/midbrain) and the back part (hindbrain, which controls basic functions like breathing and connects to the spinal cord) arise from two genetically distinct groups of starter cells very early in development, each with a different chemical "packaging" of their DNA (chromatin — the combination of DNA and the proteins that organize and control access to it). Using this insight, the team grew human hindbrain nerve cells from stem cells in the lab for the first time, which could improve disease models for ALS and spinal muscular atrophy, a genetic disease that damages the nerve cells controlling muscle movement, most severely in infants. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 21, 2026, covering a study in Nature Neuroscience. Peer-reviewed.*

**The 3D shape of DNA inside brain cells becomes disorganized in Alzheimer's disease.** Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia, a progressive brain disease in which nerve cells die off over years, gradually destroying memory, thinking, and eventually the ability to carry out daily tasks; it's most associated with two hallmark proteins, amyloid-beta and tau, that clump abnormally in the brain. Using new single-cell mapping and an AI model, researchers found that in Alzheimer's-affected brain tissue, the normal 3D folding pattern of DNA inside the cell nucleus — which keeps active and inactive genome regions properly separated, like keeping different filing cabinets closed — becomes blurred, disrupting how genes are switched on and off. This suggests DNA's physical packaging problems may be a disease driver in their own right, separate from (and possibly upstream of) the amyloid and tau buildup. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 13, 2026, covering a study in Science. Peer-reviewed.*

**A distinct "remodeling" phase of brain aging may explain why Alzheimer's risk jumps between ages 50 and 75.** Looking at human brain tissue across ages, researchers found that in this specific window, the brain's resident immune cells (microglia, which are normally seeded once before birth and typically last a lifetime) sharply decline and get replaced by more inflammation-prone immune cells arriving from the blood, while cells that maintain the blood-brain barrier (the tightly sealed layer of blood vessels that normally keeps harmful substances and immune cells out of the brain) also decline. The 3D genome disorganization described above happens alongside this. Together, the findings suggest brain aging isn't a smooth, gradual decline but a coordinated, multi-system shift concentrated in one age band — which may help explain the timing of rising Alzheimer's risk. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 15, 2026, covering a study in Science. Peer-reviewed.*

### New tools for watching biology happen

**A new imaging system tracks nearly every cell in a live animal at once.** A team built a platform called WHOLISTIC that combines fast 3D fluorescence microscopy with machine-learning cell-tracking and genetically engineered sensors that light up when calcium levels change (a common signal of cell activity) to record activity across essentially the whole body of a living zebrafish larva simultaneously — brain, muscle, and internal organs together. This revealed unexpectedly widespread coordination between the brain and body's activity, and gives researchers a new way to study physiology, behavior, and disease at both the single-cell and whole-organism level in the same animal. *Source: AZoLifeSciences / UCL, Sept 10, 2026, covering a study in Nature. Peer-reviewed.*

**Scientists directly photograph two strands of DNA recognizing and "zipping" toward each other.** Using atomic force microscopy (a technique that "feels" a surface with an ultra-fine physical probe to build an image at near-atomic resolution, rather than using light or electrons), combined with computer simulations, researchers directly visualized two DNA double helices lining up groove-to-groove, bridged by positively charged metal ions acting like molecular glue. This confirms a roughly 20-year-old theoretical model of how DNA strands can recognize matching sequences in each other even before they unwind — a process relevant to genetic recombination (how DNA segments get shuffled, including during reproduction) and to mutation-related processes such as cancer. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 14, 2026, covering a study in Nucleic Acids Research. Peer-reviewed.*

### Evolution and genetics

**Genetic testing reveals the "gentoo penguin" is actually four separate species, including a newly named one.** Using whole-genome sequencing (reading out large portions of an organism's complete DNA sequence) of penguins from ten colonies, along with differences in single "letters" of DNA code between individuals (SNPs, single nucleotide polymorphisms — one of the most common ways scientists measure genetic difference between populations), researchers showed what was long treated as one species is genetically four, including a newly described species from the Kerguelen Islands — the first new penguin species named in over a century. The work also reveals how isolated island populations have locally adapted to different climates, which matters for conservation planning. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 21, 2026, covering a study in Communications Biology. Peer-reviewed.*

**A near-complete fossil skeleton overturns a 40-year-old classification of an ancient mammal group.** A well-preserved fossil skeleton of an animal called Tamirkhan balcarceli shows that "zhelestid" mammals — previously classified, based on tooth shape alone, as close relatives of the ancestors of all modern placental mammals (mammals that nourish their young in the womb via a placenta, as opposed to laying eggs or raising tiny, underdeveloped young in a pouch) — actually belong to an entirely separate evolutionary branch. Their tooth shape evolved independently to resemble that of true placental-mammal ancestors, a phenomenon called convergent evolution, in which unrelated species independently evolve similar features because they face similar pressures. This is a caution against classifying extinct species by teeth alone. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 28, 2026, covering a study in Nature. Peer-reviewed.*

**160-million-year-old "resurrected" molecules turn out to fight drug-resistant bacteria better than modern ones.** Using a technique called ancestral sequence reconstruction — essentially running evolution in reverse by computationally inferring what an ancient gene probably looked like based on how its modern descendants differ, then synthesizing that ancient version in the lab — researchers recreated infection-fighting peptides (short chains of amino acids) as they likely existed in mammal ancestors 160 million years ago. Tested against modern drug-resistant "superbug" bacteria (strains that have evolved to survive standard antibiotics, a growing public-health threat), some of these ancestral peptides outperformed their modern human counterparts, suggesting evolutionary history can be mined as a source of new antibiotic designs. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 28, 2026, covering a study published in PLOS Biology. Peer-reviewed.*

### Cell biology and biochemistry

**Cells have a hidden "impossible" backup route to make an essential amino acid.** Cysteine is one of the amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) the body needs, and it's normally produced through enzymes called disulfide reductases, which break a specific type of chemical bond. Using mice genetically engineered to lack these enzymes entirely, researchers discovered a completely different chemical pathway cells can use instead — one previously assumed to be biochemically impossible. The finding overturns a textbook assumption, and there are early signs cancer cells may exploit this backup route to survive chemotherapy and immunotherapy, pointing to a possible new drug target. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 24, 2026, covering a study in Nature Chemical Biology. Peer-reviewed.*

**A single-celled organism contracts faster than any muscle, using a completely different mechanism.** Spirostomum ambiguum is a giant single-celled organism (a ciliate, named for the hair-like structures, cilia, many use to move) that can snap to about a quarter of its length in under five milliseconds — roughly ten times faster than human muscle — without using ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the molecule cells normally burn as their immediate energy currency) during the contraction itself. Researchers identified the mechanism as a calcium-triggered "fishnet" made of two proteins, centrin and Sfi1, completely unrelated to the actin-myosin system that powers ordinary muscle. It's of interest both for basic cell biophysics and for engineers designing ultrafast artificial actuators. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 19, 2026, covering a study in PNAS. Peer-reviewed.*

### Immunology and the microbiome

**A compound made by gut bacteria helps repair the intestinal lining instead of inflaming it.** Urolithin A is a molecule produced when gut bacteria break down compounds found in foods like pomegranates, walnuts, and berries. Using tissue from patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD, a group of chronic conditions — including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — in which the immune system attacks the lining of the digestive tract, causing pain, diarrhea, and long-term damage) along with lab models, researchers showed urolithin A activates two immune-signaling proteins (the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and the NLRP6 inflammasome) in a way that repairs damaged gut lining and strengthens its barrier function, rather than driving more inflammation. That's notable because these same signaling pathways are usually associated with triggering inflammation — showing the same molecular switch can have opposite effects depending on context, which could point toward more targeted IBD treatments. *Source: ScienceDaily, Sept 17, 2026, covering a study in Nature Communications. Peer-reviewed.*

## 3. Biotech and Pharma Business News

### Consolidation and platform deals

**Telix Pharmaceuticals will buy German isotope supplier ITM for up to $2.35 billion.** Radiopharmaceuticals are drugs that carry a small amount of radioactive material directly to cancer cells (for imaging or treatment), and they depend on a steady, specialized supply of radioactive isotopes — a genuine manufacturing bottleneck for the field. Telix's deal for ITM Isotope Technologies Munich, worth up to $2.35 billion including future milestone payments, combines Telix's drug pipeline with ITM's isotope production and its own late-stage programs, aiming to build a vertically integrated radiopharmaceutical company. The deal dilutes existing Telix shareholders by adding roughly 106 million new shares, which weighed on the stock initially. *Source: Bloomberg / RTT News, Sept 20–21, 2026. Stock: Telix (TLX) fell an estimated 3–10% depending on the source, before recovering some ground on an analyst upgrade.*

**Novartis pays $125 million for a platform designed to get large drugs across the blood-brain barrier.** The blood-brain barrier is a tightly sealed layer of blood vessel cells that normally keeps most molecules — including most drugs — out of the brain, which is one of the biggest obstacles in treating neurological disease. Novartis exercised an option to acquire a delivery-technology platform from Sironax specifically designed to ferry large molecules such as antibodies across that barrier, aiming to apply it to diseases including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Huntington's disease (an inherited condition that progressively damages nerve cells in the brain, affecting movement, cognition, and mood), and ALS. *Source: FierceBiotech, Sept 16, 2026.*

### Layoffs continue across big pharma and smaller biotechs

**Novo Nordisk's total 2026 job cuts turn out to be 13,000 — well above what was first disclosed.** At a London investor event, Novo Nordisk's CEO revealed the company is cutting 13,000 positions globally, 4,000 more than the 9,000 originally announced in late 2025, with Danish headcount alone reportedly down more than 7,000 in less than a year. The savings are being redirected into research and development, as Novo faces intensifying competition from Eli Lilly in the obesity-drug market. In the same period, Novo also struck a partnership worth up to $1.4 billion with Orbis Medicines to develop oral "macrocyclic peptide" drugs — large, ring-shaped molecules designed to be taken as a pill rather than injected — for obesity and related metabolic conditions, underscoring that the cuts are about reallocating spending, not retreating from the category. *Sources: The Local Denmark, Sept 21, 2026; BioPharma Dive, ~Sept 21–22, 2026.*

**Bristol Myers Squibb cuts another 265 jobs, and Sionna Therapeutics cuts nearly half its staff after a drug failure.** Bristol Myers Squibb announced 265 more layoffs tied to its New Jersey headquarters, part of a multi-year cost-cutting push as the company manages several of its older drugs losing patent protection. Separately, Sionna Therapeutics cut 46% of its workforce after its lead drug for cystic fibrosis — a genetic disease that causes the body to produce unusually thick, sticky mucus, primarily damaging the lungs and digestive system — failed in a mid-stage trial the previous month; the company's stock had lost roughly $2 billion in value since that failure and it is now trying to extend its remaining cash by pivoting its approach. *Sources: FiercePharma / NJ Biz, Sept 23–24, 2026; Endpoints News, Sept 2026.*

### Reverse mergers as a workaround for a tight IPO market

**Two private biotechs go public via "reverse mergers" with struggling public companies, each backed by fresh financing.** A reverse merger is when a private company merges into an already-public (often financially struggling) company, effectively becoming publicly traded without going through a traditional initial public offering (IPO). Lisata Therapeutics acquired Marea Therapeutics this way, in a roughly $225–240 million deal that pivots Lisata away from cancer drugs and into heart/metabolic disease, backed by a simultaneous $225 million private placement (a direct sale of new shares to selected investors, rather than the public market). Separately, Aethlon Medical merged with North Immunology — developer of an experimental "bispecific antibody" (a lab-engineered antibody built to grab onto two different target proteins at once, rather than the usual single target) for eczema — backed by $180 million in financing. Two such deals landing in the same week point to reverse mergers becoming a real alternative route to public capital while the traditional biotech IPO market stays difficult. *Sources: FierceBiotech, Sept 17, 2026 (both deals).*

### Money keeps flowing into AI-driven drug discovery

Three sizable funding rounds in the past two weeks went to companies using artificial intelligence to design new drugs or therapies — a sign that investor appetite for this category has held up even as biotech funding overall has been tighter. Enveda Biosciences raised $311 million (reportedly at around a $2 billion valuation) to expand its AI platform for discovering medicines from natural chemical compounds, aimed at inflammatory and metabolic diseases. Basecamp Research, backed by AI companies Anthropic and Nvidia rather than typical biotech investors, raised $140 million to advance AI-designed cell therapies (treatments that use living, engineered cells as the drug itself). And BigHat Biosciences raised $75 million to further its AI-based protein-design platform for cancer treatments. *Sources: Businesswire / FierceBiotech, Sept 23–24, 2026 (Enveda); GEN / European Biotechnology Magazine, Sept 23–24, 2026 (Basecamp); BioSpace / Businesswire, Sept 23–24, 2026 (BigHat).*

Two more mid-size rounds rounded out the period: Sling Therapeutics raised $123 million to push an oral pill for thyroid eye disease (a condition, often linked to thyroid autoimmune disease, in which the tissue behind the eyes swells and pushes the eyeballs forward, causing bulging, pain, and sometimes vision problems) toward Phase 3 testing as a potential alternative to the current injectable standard treatment; and Circle Pharma raised $92.5 million to advance an oral cancer drug targeting cyclin D1, a protein that normally helps control when cells divide and is frequently overactive in cancer. *Sources: BioSpace / Businesswire, Sept 16, 2026 (Sling); Businesswire, Sept 15, 2026 (Circle).*

### A policy signal moves an entire sector

**Chinese biopharma stocks jump on signs the US won't restrict drug-licensing deals with Chinese biotechs.** Over the past couple of years, Western pharmaceutical companies have increasingly "in-licensed" (paid for the rights to develop and sell) promising drug candidates originally discovered by Chinese biotech companies, a trend that has powered a wave of large deals. There had been concern that US legislation aimed at restricting business ties with certain Chinese biotech firms (often referred to as the Biosecure Act) could choke off this deal flow. Reports that the US plans to continue permitting these licensing deals eased that fear and sent Chinese biopharma stocks higher. *Source: CNBC, Sept 21, 2026.*

## Top Takeaways

Regulatory approvals this period skewed toward diseases that have had few or no good treatment options — FOP, a rare bone-forming disorder; a rare bile duct cancer; and a possible first-ever systemic therapy for Sjögren's disease — rather than incremental improvements on existing drug classes, suggesting companies are increasingly chasing underserved rare and autoimmune conditions where regulatory and pricing paths can be more favorable.

Kidney disease had an unusually strong stretch, with two different drugs (an RNA-targeted antisense therapy from Ionis/Roche and an already-approved antibody from Vera) both posting positive late-stage data in IgA nephropathy within the same two weeks — a sign the field is maturing fast after years of having almost nothing to offer these patients.

Trial safety signals, not just efficacy, are what moved markets this period: Celldex and Xenon both had strong or positive efficacy data overshadowed by rare but serious side effects (anaphylaxis and neuropsychiatric events, respectively), each triggering double-digit stock swings — a reminder that in this environment, investors are pricing safety risk as heavily as, or more heavily than, whether a drug works at all.

Big pharma is simultaneously cutting and spending: Novo Nordisk revealed deeper-than-disclosed layoffs (13,000 total) while announcing a new $1.4 billion deal for oral obesity-drug technology in the same stretch, and Bristol Myers Squibb kept trimming New Jersey jobs — a pattern of large companies reallocating cash toward next-generation pipeline bets even while shrinking existing headcount.

Reverse mergers are emerging as a real alternative to the traditional IPO for private biotechs trying to reach public markets, with two such deals (Lisata/Marea and Aethlon/North Immunology) landing in the same week, each paired with a sizable private financing — a sign the conventional IPO window has stayed difficult enough that companies are routing around it.

Investors kept funding AI-driven drug discovery at meaningful scale even as overall biotech funding stayed tight, with three separate nine-figure rounds (Enveda, Basecamp Research, BigHat Biosciences) closing within days of each other — and Basecamp's round, backed by AI companies Anthropic and Nvidia rather than typical biotech investors, points to growing crossover interest from the AI industry itself in owning a piece of biology.

On the pure science side, several of the period's most interesting discoveries converged on a single theme — that 3D physical structure, not just DNA sequence, drives biology in ways only now becoming visible: how genes are physically folded inside the nucleus appears to break down in both Alzheimer's disease and normal brain aging, a newly observed protein "workspace" explains how neurons manage abnormally long genes, and a decades-old theoretical model of how two DNA strands physically recognize each other was directly photographed for the first time.
