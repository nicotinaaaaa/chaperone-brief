Every disease and technical term is explained at first use. **Coverage note:** many major trade-press sites block automated access, so this run is shorter than a full sweep. Items from earlier briefs (Emcitate, I-DXd withdrawal, Novartis–Abogen, etc.) are not repeated.

**Terms used throughout.** A *clinical trial* tests a drug in people: **Phase 1** (small group, mainly safety), **Phase 2** (early evidence it works; "2a/2b" are earlier/larger versions), **Phase 3** (large, usually compared with placebo or standard treatment; the main basis for approval). A *primary endpoint* is the single main measurement a trial is designed to prove. *Progression-free survival (PFS)* is how long patients live without their cancer growing. *Statistically significant* means the difference is unlikely to be down to chance. A *PDUFA date* is the deadline by which the FDA has promised a decision on a drug application.

## 1. Clinical trials

### ArriVent's lung-cancer pill furmonertinib fails its Phase 3 trial; stock halves

**What happened.** In the Phase 3 FURVENT trial, furmonertinib (an oral pill) was compared with standard chemotherapy as first treatment for lung cancer carrying a mutation called EGFR exon 20 insertion. It missed its primary endpoint: at the highest dose, median PFS was about 11 months versus 9.5 months on chemotherapy, a gain of roughly 1.5 months that was not statistically significant. A Jefferies analyst had been hoping for about 3 months. ArriVent cited a "trend" toward better overall survival and reported no new safety signals. Shares fell more than 50% pre-market on Oct 6, hitting a 52-week low, and Shanghai Allist (the drug's original Chinese developer, which sells it in China) fell about 20% in sympathy.

**Biology.** Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common lung cancer; cells in the lung grow uncontrollably, causing cough, breathlessness and weight loss, and it is a leading cause of cancer death. *EGFR* is a protein on the cell surface that tells the cell to grow; in some tumors a mutation leaves it stuck "on." Exon 20 insertions are one rare type of EGFR mutation (up to ~10% of NSCLC), and they are hard to block with older EGFR drugs. Furmonertinib is a *tyrosine kinase inhibitor* — a small molecule that blocks the enzyme activity of EGFR so the growth signal stops.

**Why it matters.** The result removes a would-be competitor to Johnson & Johnson's Rybrevant (an injected antibody) in a niche with several rivals, and it is a pointed warning for U.S. companies in-licensing China-originated drugs: Chinese-approved drugs do not always repeat their results in Western trials. Commercially, it wipes out most of ArriVent's value.

**Sources:** BioPharma Dive (Oct 6, 2026); Benzinga; Bloomberg and Investing.com (Oct 7, headlines only). **Stage:** Phase 3 failure; market-relevant.

### Endeavor's taladegib wins a Phase 2b trial in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

**What happened.** On Oct 8, Endeavor BioMedicines announced that taladegib met its main goal in the 213-patient Phase 2b WHISTLE-PF trial: after 24 weeks, patients on the drug showed a statistically significant improvement in lung capacity (percent predicted forced vital capacity, "ppFVC") versus placebo. Lung-scan measures of total lung capacity and fibrosis also improved, and serious side effects were balanced between drug and placebo. Effect sizes and p-values were withheld for a future medical meeting. Endeavor plans to talk to the FDA about a Phase 3 design.

**Biology.** Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a disease in which lung tissue gradually scars and stiffens for no known cause, making breathing harder over time; it mostly affects people over 60, and survival after diagnosis is often only a few years. *Forced vital capacity* is the amount of air a person can blow out after a deep breath — a standard measure of lung function. Taladegib is a *Hedgehog inhibitor*: it blocks a signaling pathway (a chain of molecular messages) that is normally active in embryonic development and is thought to be re-activated in scarring lung tissue, driving fibrosis.

**Why it matters.** The two approved drugs, Boehringer Ingelheim's Ofev and Roche's Esbriet, only slow the decline in lung function. Endeavor's data suggest an *improvement*, which would be a new type of result in IPF — but numbers are not yet public, so caution is warranted.

**Source:** Fierce Biotech (Oct 8, 2026). **Stage:** mid-stage clinical (Phase 2b), positive on topline.

## 2. Business and deals

### Ono pays $80 million for regional rights to three Biohaven IgG degraders
On Oct 8, Ono Pharmaceutical agreed to pay $80 million upfront for local rights to three drugs from Biohaven designed to *degrade IgG*, including one in Phase 3. IgG is the most common type of antibody in blood; in autoimmune diseases, harmful IgG antibodies attack the body's own tissues, so removing them can calm the disease. This is a small-to-mid deal showing continued interest in "antibody-clearing" medicines as an alternative to immune-suppressing drugs. **Source:** Fierce Biotech (Oct 8). Territory and further terms not available. **Stage:** clinical / market-relevant.

### GSK adopts Chai Discovery's AI models
GSK will use AI models from Chai Discovery (software that predicts how drug-like molecules or antibodies fold and bind to targets) after lab experiments validated their predictions (Fierce Biotech, Oct 9). Financial terms were not available. **Stage:** platform/market-relevant.

### IPO window opens: TRex ($117M), Retension ($45M), Iambic (seeking $135M)
Fierce Biotech reported (Oct 8–10) that Lilly-backed TRex raised $117 million in an IPO (selling shares to the public for the first time), Retension disclosed a $45 million listing, AI-driven cancer-drug biotech Iambic is seeking $135 million, and two companies both named Aspen (one focused on Parkinson's disease, one on bispecific antibodies — antibodies that grab two different targets at once) plan IPOs. *Why it matters:* a cluster of listings suggests investor appetite for biotech is recovering after a tough period. Details of each company's science were not available. **Stage:** market-relevant.

### Other items

## 3. Science news and prizes

### 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine: optogenetics

**What happened.** Karl Deisseroth (Stanford), Peter Hegemann (Humboldt University, Berlin) and Georg Nagel (University of Würzburg) shared the prize, announced Oct 5, for light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.

**Background.** An *ion channel* is a pore in a cell's membrane that opens to let charged particles in or out; in neurons, this is what makes them fire electrical signals. Nagel and Hegemann found that certain algae have channels that open when hit by light. Deisseroth and others then used genetic engineering to place such channels into chosen neurons in animals, so researchers can switch those specific cells on or off with a flash of light — *optogenetics*. (This explanation of the discovery's history is general background; the news article I could access gave only the winners and a one-line citation.)

**Why it matters.** It allows cause-and-effect tests of what specific brain circuits do, and biotechs are now trying to use it therapeutically (for example in vision restoration), per a Fierce Biotech feature on Oct 9. **Sources:** WUNC/NPR (Oct 5); Fierce Biotech (Oct 9). **Stage:** basic science/recognition.

### 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry: Kagan and Soai
Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai won on Oct 7 "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis" (Khaleej Times). Plain English: many molecules come in two mirror-image forms ("left- and right-handed"), and drugs often work only in one form. Their work showed that tiny differences in the mix can be amplified, and that a reaction's product can speed up its own formation, so one mirror image can come to dominate. This is relevant to drug manufacturing, where making only the correct mirror image matters. The source gave only the citation; the explanation is my general background. **Stage:** basic science.
