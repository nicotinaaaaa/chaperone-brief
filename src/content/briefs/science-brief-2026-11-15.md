As always: many trade-press sites block automated access and some results were headline-only; I include only details I could confirm and flag where a number or outcome was unavailable. Prices and stock moves are as reported by the cited source and may be intraday. This is not investment advice.

**Terms used throughout.**
- **Phase 1/2/3.** *Phase 1* is a small first-in-human safety study. *Phase 2* looks for early signs a drug works. *Phase 3* is a large, usually randomized trial — the main evidence used for approval.
- **Primary/secondary endpoint.** The *primary endpoint* is the main measurement a trial is designed to win on; *secondary endpoints* are additional measurements that support the story but aren't the main bar.
- **Topline/headline data** are the first results a company announces, ahead of a full presentation or peer review.
- **Placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind.** Patients are split by chance (randomized) between the drug and an inactive placebo, and neither patients nor (in "double-blind" trials) the treating doctors know who got which, to prevent bias.
- **Non-inferiority trial.** Instead of trying to beat a comparator, the drug only needs to be "not meaningfully worse" than a pre-agreed margin — useful when the new option offers convenience or safety benefits rather than bigger effects.
- **Hazard ratio (HR).** A measure of relative risk over time; HR below 1 favors the treatment, above 1 favors the comparator. If the 95% confidence interval (the range the true value plausibly falls in) spans across 1, the result is not statistically convincing either way.
- **MACE (major adverse cardiovascular events).** A combined count of heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths, used to judge whether a drug is heart-safe or heart-protective.
- **Accelerated approval.** An FDA pathway that approves a drug early based on a "surrogate" measurement (like tumor shrinkage) thought likely to predict real benefit, on condition the company later proves that benefit in a confirmatory trial; the FDA can withdraw the approval if it fails.
- **BLA (Biologics License Application)** is the formal request to the FDA to approve a biologic (a drug made from living cells or biological material). *Priority review* shortens the FDA's target decision time to about 6 months.
- **PDUFA date** is the FDA's target deadline for deciding on an application.
- **Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) and siRNA.** Both are short, lab-made genetic sequences designed to stick to a specific piece of RNA and block it from being used to make a protein — a way to "turn down" a disease-causing gene without editing DNA. They work by different chemistry and the two approaches can differ in potency and duration.
- **Bispecific antibody / T-cell engager / ADC.** A *bispecific antibody* has two different binding arms that latch onto two different targets at once; a *T-cell engager* is a bispecific designed specifically to drag a patient's T cells next to a tumor cell; an *antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)* is an antibody with a toxic drug chemically attached, so it delivers chemotherapy-like poison directly to cells carrying its target.
- **Vector / gene therapy / vector integration.** *Gene therapy* delivers genetic material into cells, usually inside a disabled virus (the *vector*), to supply a missing or working gene. *Vector integration* means the delivered DNA has inserted itself into the patient's own chromosomes — normally the vectors used are designed to avoid this, so when it happens near a growth-control gene it can, in principle, contribute to a tumor.
- **Upfront payment, milestones, royalties.** In a licensing deal: money paid at signing, money paid only if goals (trial results, approvals) are met, and a percentage of future sales.

## 1. Regulatory and safety

### Novartis discloses nine tumors in patients treated with the gene therapy Zolgensma; regulators have not yet acted
**What happened.** On Nov 5, Novartis reported nine tumors among more than 6,000 patients treated with Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec) since its 2019 approval. In two of the nine, the company says the virus used to deliver the therapy (the "vector") appears to have inserted itself into the patient's own DNA — a vector integration event. Tumor types, patient ages and timing were not disclosed. UK clinicians have pathology results for three of the nine children: two tumors are described as "specifically related" to the treatment, one as apparently unrelated, and the remaining six cases have no reported causal finding yet. No reliable incidence rate (tumors per number of patients treated) can be calculated from this handful of reports. Novartis says it notified health authorities as each case arose, that a theoretical tumor risk is already noted in the US prescribing information (revised June 2026), and that the two vector-integration patients remain under monitoring. The UK's product label, last revised Oct 5, 2026, still states no malignancies had been reported — that predates this disclosure. The UK's national multidisciplinary SMA team says no change to routine monitoring is needed "at this time," and as of Nov 6 no statement had been issued by the FDA, EMA or MHRA.

**Biology.** Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare inherited disease in which a faulty gene, *SMN1*, fails to supply enough of a protein motor neurons (the nerve cells that control muscles) need to survive. Without treatment, the most severe form causes progressive muscle weakness, loss of the ability to sit, stand or breathe unassisted, and death in infancy. Zolgensma is given as a single one-time infusion to children under two who carry two faulty copies of *SMN1*; it delivers a working copy of the gene inside a modified, non-replicating virus, aiming to let motor neurons start making the missing protein for life.

**Why it matters.** Zolgensma was one of the first one-shot gene therapies to reach the market and remains a flagship product for the field; any credible tumor signal, even a small and unconfirmed one, raises the stakes for how regulators monitor long-term safety of gene therapies built on viral vectors. It's also a reminder that confirmatory, long-term safety data for gene therapies often lags years behind initial approval — a theme that also showed up last week in uniQure's Huntington's filing, which rests on a small, non-placebo-controlled study. No clinical action is advised yet, and parents of treated children are not being told to seek extra monitoring, but the story is one to watch as more case detail (or an FDA/EMA statement) emerges.
**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 6, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/zolgensma-tumour-risk-novartis-nine-reports/). **Stage:** post-market safety signal; market-relevant for the broader gene-therapy sector.

### Two label expansions widen access for rare-disease drugs
**MHRA extends Joenja (leniolisib, Pharming Group) to children 4–11 in the UK.** Announced Nov 6: the UK regulator authorized leniolisib for children aged 4 to 11 weighing at least 27 kg who have activated PI3K delta syndrome (APDS) — an inherited immune disorder in which an overactive enzyme called PI3K-delta causes immune cells to mature abnormally, leading to recurrent infections, enlarged lymph nodes and a higher risk of lymphoma; previously the UK label covered only ages 12 and up. No new pediatric efficacy numbers were released; the extension leans on the adult/adolescent Phase 3 trial. Expected UK availability is February 2027. **Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 9, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/weekly-news-roundup-9-november-2026/).

**NICE extends Imcivree (setmelanotide, Rhythm Pharmaceuticals) to adults with Bardet-Biedl syndrome in England/Wales/Northern Ireland.** Announced Nov 4: England's cost-effectiveness body widened its 2024 guidance (which covered ages 6–17) to genetically confirmed adult patients. Bardet-Biedl syndrome is a rare inherited disorder that, among other features, damages the brain's hunger-signaling pathway, causing intense, uncontrollable hunger and severe obesity from early childhood, alongside vision loss and kidney problems. Setmelanotide restores signaling further down that same pathway. **Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 9, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/weekly-news-roundup-9-november-2026/).

## 2. Clinical trials

### Vir Biotechnology's antibody combination clears hepatitis delta virus in most patients in Phase 3
**What happened.** Reported Nov 9 (with fuller figures in a Nov 13 roundup): in the ECLIPSE 1 trial, 124 patients were randomized 2:1 to monthly injections of elebsiran plus tobevibart, or to a delayed-treatment comparator arm that received nothing until week 12. At week 48, 44% of treated patients had both undetectable virus and a normal liver-enzyme test (ALT) — the primary endpoint — versus 0% of the comparator group (p<0.0001). Looking at virus suppression alone, 79% of treated patients had undetectable virus versus 0%, and ALT normalized in 53% versus 5%. Earlier data (100% viral response by week 24; undetectable virus in 85% of patients with cirrhosis and 73% without) had been reported at the AASLD liver meeting in Denver. Safety was favorable: one grade 3 (serious but not life-threatening) treatment-related side effect, a drop in a type of white blood cell that resolved within two weeks, and no treatment interruptions or discontinuations. Vir shares jumped 22% on the news.

**Biology.** Hepatitis delta is caused by the hepatitis delta virus (HDV), a small, defective virus that can only infect people who are also infected with hepatitis B, because it borrows hepatitis B's outer shell to spread. It's considered the most severe form of viral hepatitis: it accelerates liver scarring (cirrhosis), liver failure and liver cancer far faster than hepatitis B alone, and until now there has been no approved treatment in the US. Elebsiran and tobevibart are both antibody-based drugs: elebsiran is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that silences the viral genes hepatitis B needs to keep producing surface proteins, while tobevibart is a neutralizing antibody that blocks those surface proteins directly, together starving HDV of the hepatitis B "shell" it depends on.

**Why it matters.** Chronic hepatitis delta affects an estimated 12–20 million people worldwide and has had essentially no dedicated therapy; a combination that clears detectable virus in the large majority of patients, with a clean safety profile, is a meaningful first for the field and sets up a likely regulatory filing.
**Source:** [Yahoo Finance/Benzinga (Nov 9, 2026)](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/vir-biotechnology-stock-jumps-22-130631799.html); [Life Science Daily (Nov 13, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/clinical-trial-results-13-november-2026/). **Stage:** Phase 3, positive. **Market-relevant.**

### A major cardiovascular-prevention hypothesis fails: lowering Lp(a) with pelacarsen doesn't cut heart attacks or strokes
**What happened.** Presented at the American Heart Association meeting on Nov 7 and published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (covered Nov 13): the Lp(a)HORIZON trial randomized about 8,350 people with existing cardiovascular disease and high Lp(a) to monthly pelacarsen or placebo, following them for a median of 4.7 years. Pelacarsen lowered Lp(a) levels by about 78% — exactly as designed — but this did not translate into fewer heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths: the main combined endpoint occurred in 12.3% of the drug group versus 12.1% of the placebo group, a non-result. The trial's chair called it "stone cold neutral." Possible explanations offered (none confirmed) include starting treatment too late in already-established disease, strong background care in both arms, an Lp(a) entry threshold that was too low, the antisense chemistry being less potent than newer siRNA-based rivals, and more side effects (including some anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction) in the treated group. Novartis has ended its pelacarsen cardiovascular program; Ionis, which discovered the drug, keeps royalty rights elsewhere.

**Biology.** Lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is a cholesterol-carrying particle in the blood whose level is mostly set by a person's genes rather than diet or exercise. High Lp(a) is strongly linked, in observational studies, to heart attack, stroke and aortic valve disease, and for decades doctors have lacked any drug that specifically lowers it — making this the first large outcomes trial to actually test whether lowering Lp(a) helps. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide (see glossary) that blocks the liver from making the Lp(a) particle's signature protein.

**Why it matters.** The result doesn't disprove the Lp(a) hypothesis — it leaves it unproven, with multiple confounders — but it's a setback for the first drug to reach a definitive test. Two rivals with different designs are still running: Amgen's olpasiran and Lilly's lepodisiran/muvalaplin, both using the newer siRNA or small-molecule approaches, in trials that start earlier (before an event) and may use a higher entry threshold. Guidance on testing Lp(a) hasn't changed — it remains a recognized risk marker — but the field's hopes now rest on those later trials.
**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 13, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/why-did-pelacarsen-fail-lpa-horizon/). **Stage:** Phase 3, negative (primary endpoint not met). **Market-relevant.**

### An experimental antibody for early Alzheimer's disease fails a Phase 2 trial; developer halts the program
**What happened.** Reported Nov 3: Acumen Pharmaceuticals' ALTITUDE-AD trial enrolled 542 people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease, randomizing them to one of two doses of sabirnetug (ACU193) or placebo, given by IV infusion every four weeks, with cognitive/functional decline measured at 18 months. Neither dose beat placebo on the primary endpoint or on key secondary measures. A brain-imaging measure of amyloid plaque did fall significantly and dose-dependently — showing the drug was doing something biologically — but that didn't translate into any slowing of symptoms. About 10% of treated patients had ARIA-E, a drug-related brain swelling that shows up on MRI scans and is a known risk of this class of antibody. Acumen is stopping all clinical development of sabirnetug, ending the trial's open-label extension, and reviewing its options, including conserving cash.

**Biology.** Alzheimer's disease is a progressive brain disorder, the most common cause of dementia, in which abnormal protein clumps — amyloid plaques outside neurons and tau tangles inside them — build up over years and are associated with the death of brain cells, causing worsening memory loss, confusion and eventual loss of independence. Most approved and late-stage antibody drugs target amyloid plaques directly; sabirnetug instead targeted soluble amyloid-beta oligomers — smaller, loose clusters of the same protein that float freely rather than clumping into plaques, which some researchers think are the more directly toxic form.

**Why it matters.** The failure despite clearing some amyloid marker reinforces a pattern in Alzheimer's drug development: reducing a biological marker doesn't guarantee clinical benefit, and oligomer-targeting (as opposed to plaque-targeting) hasn't yet proven itself as an alternative strategy. It's a reminder that, even after several hard-won amyloid-plaque antibody approvals in recent years, the disease remains difficult to meaningfully slow.
**Source:** [NeurologyLive (Nov 3, 2026)](https://www.neurologylive.com/view/alzheimer-agent-sabirnetug-misses-primary-endpoint-acumen-halts-development). **Stage:** Phase 2, negative; program discontinued.

### Pharvaris' deucrictibant shows fast relief for hereditary angioedema attacks in pivotal trial
**What happened.** Published in *The Lancet* and covered Nov 2: the RAPIDe-3 trial gave 134 patients (113 of whom treated 201 attacks) either a 20 mg immediate-release capsule of deucrictibant or placebo, in a crossover design where patients served as their own comparison across different attacks. Deucrictibant worked much faster: median time to the start of symptom relief was about 1.3 hours versus more than 12 hours on placebo (p<0.0001), and 83% of deucrictibant-treated attacks reached relief within 4 hours versus 28% on placebo. Complete symptom resolution within 24 hours occurred in 60% of attacks versus 15%, and patients needed rescue medication far less often (9% vs. 44% of attacks within 24 hours). Side effects were mild, with fatigue the most common, and no serious or severe drug-related events occurred. A related extended-release tablet for daily prevention (not on-demand treatment), tested in the separate CHAPTER-3 trial, separately cut the monthly attack rate by 83% versus placebo. The FDA is reviewing deucrictibant's application for the on-demand capsule, with a target decision of April 23, 2027.

**Biology.** Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare inherited disorder in which a missing or malfunctioning blood protein (C1 inhibitor, in the most common form) allows a chain of other proteins to run out of control, causing sudden, unpredictable swelling attacks in the skin, gut and — most dangerously — the throat, which can be life-threatening if it blocks breathing. Deucrictibant blocks the bradykinin B2 receptor, the final signaling step that directly causes the swelling, so blocking it short-circuits an attack regardless of what triggered the chain reaction upstream.

**Why it matters.** Current on-demand HAE treatments are injectable; an effective oral pill with this fast an onset would be a major convenience upgrade for patients managing unpredictable, frightening attacks, and sets up competition with KalVista's recently approved oral option in this space.
**Source:** [Healio (Nov 2, 2026)](https://www.healio.com/news/allergy-asthma/20261102/deucrictibant-capsule-shortens-time-to-symptom-relief-after-hereditary-angioedema-attack). **Stage:** Phase 3, positive; regulatory filing under review.

### GLP-1 drugs keep expanding beyond weight loss — with one neutral cardiovascular result
Two separate trials, both reported Nov 13, illustrate how far the GLP-1 drug class (medicines that mimic a gut hormone to reduce appetite and blood sugar, now the biggest growth story in pharma) is reaching beyond diabetes and obesity:
- **Boehringer Ingelheim's survodutide was heart-neutral, not heart-protective, in a dedicated safety trial (SYNCHRONIZE-CVOT, Phase 3, 5,530 adults, published in *The Lancet*).** The drug met its required bar of not increasing cardiovascular risk (hazard ratio 0.89, 95% CI 0.67–1.18, where an interval crossing 1 means the result isn't statistically convincing), while producing weight loss up to 13.7% at 72 weeks versus 2.0% on placebo. About one in five patients discontinued due to gut-related side effects. This confirms safety but doesn't show the extra heart-protection benefit that some rival drugs in the class have claimed.
- **Eli Lilly's orforglipron improved obstructive sleep apnea (ATTAIN-OSA, Phase 3, 712 adults, published in JAMA).** Obstructive sleep apnea is a common disorder in which the airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, cutting off breathing for seconds at a time, fragmenting sleep and raising the risk of heart disease, high blood pressure and daytime fatigue; obesity is a major risk factor. Patients not using a breathing machine (PAP therapy) saw their hourly rate of breathing interruptions (AHI) fall by 43.8% relative to baseline versus 6.3% on placebo, alongside roughly 11% weight loss. This builds the case for a once-daily pill (orforglipron doesn't require injection) as an alternative or add-on to device-based sleep apnea treatment.

**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 13, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/clinical-trial-results-13-november-2026/). **Stage:** both Phase 3; survodutide stage is safety-confirmatory, orforglipron stage is a new-indication positive readout. **Market-relevant.**

### Other readouts (grouped, from the Nov 6 and Nov 13 Life Science Daily roundups)
- **Zura Bio's tibulizumab missed in hidradenitis suppurativa (Phase 2, 247 adults).** Hidradenitis suppurativa is a chronic, painful inflammatory skin condition causing recurrent boil-like abscesses and tunnels under the skin, usually in skin folds. Reductions in abscess/nodule counts were similar across both drug doses and placebo (56.0%, 58.1% vs. 52.5%), and response-rate secondary endpoints also missed. One unrelated death occurred in the higher-dose group. Zura is discontinuing the drug in this indication. **Stage:** Phase 2, negative.
- **Marea Therapeutics' MAR001 cut triglycerides by up to 45.5% versus placebo (Phase 2b, 235 adults, published in NEJM).** High triglycerides are a type of blood fat linked to pancreatitis and heart disease when severely elevated. This is a biomarker win, not yet a proven outcomes benefit. **Stage:** Phase 2b, positive (biomarker).
- **Aravax's PVX108 raised the dose of peanut children could tolerate before reacting (Phase 2, 71 children aged 4–11).** This is a desensitization approach to peanut allergy — rather than a drug for everyday use, it's a treatment designed to gradually raise a child's reaction threshold so accidental exposure is less dangerous. The treated group's maximum tolerated dose rose 2.4-fold from baseline versus 1.2-fold on placebo, with no treatment-related anaphylaxis. **Stage:** Phase 2, positive (topline, not peer-reviewed).
- **Akebia's three-times-weekly vadadustat (Vafseo) matched an injectable anemia drug for dialysis patients (Phase 3, VOCAL trial, 353 adults).** Vadadustat is a pill that boosts the body's own production of red blood cells by mimicking low-oxygen conditions; this result supports a more convenient dosing schedule for anemia caused by chronic kidney disease. **Stage:** Phase 3, positive (non-inferiority).
- **Cerus' INTERCEPT-treated red blood cells matched standard transfusions (Phase 3, RedeS trial).** INTERCEPT is a pathogen-reduction technology applied to donated blood before transfusion, intended to reduce the risk of transmitting infections; this trial supports it doesn't compromise how well the blood works. **Stage:** Phase 3, positive (non-inferiority).

**Source for this group:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 6 and Nov 13, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/clinical-trial-results-13-november-2026/).

## 3. Business, deals and markets

### Boehringer Ingelheim pays up to $7 billion for Chinese cancer-drug options
**What happened.** Announced Nov 4: Boehringer Ingelheim struck a deal with Sino Biopharmaceutical (via its subsidiaries LaNova Medicines and Beijing Tide Pharmaceutical) for six preclinical cancer programs built on antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) and T-cell engager technology (see glossary). Sino Biopharmaceutical gets $358 million upfront and in near-term payments, plus up to roughly $7 billion in milestone payments and tiered royalties, while running early development itself. Boehringer holds options to later license two programs worldwide and four others everywhere except mainland China, Hong Kong and Macau. Targets and specific cancer types were not disclosed.

**Why it matters.** This is the latest in a long run of Western pharma companies licensing early-stage Chinese biotech assets rather than building them in-house — a trend that has reshaped oncology dealmaking over the past two years, with China now a major source of novel antibody and ADC candidates.
**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 9, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/weekly-news-roundup-9-november-2026/). **Stage:** licensing deal, preclinical assets.

### AbbVie licenses an oral bowel-disease drug from Nxera Pharma for up to $1.47 billion
**What happened.** Announced Nov 4: AbbVie took exclusive worldwide rights to NXE0033744, an oral drug for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD — a group of chronic conditions, principally Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, in which the immune system attacks the lining of the gut, causing pain, diarrhea and bleeding) that activates the EP4 receptor, a signaling pathway involved in gut-lining repair and inflammation control. AbbVie pays $100 million upfront plus up to $1.37 billion in milestones. Phase 1 testing in ulcerative colitis patients is reportedly complete, but no Phase 2 efficacy data have been released, so the drug's effectiveness is still unproven.
**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 9, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/weekly-news-roundup-9-november-2026/). **Stage:** licensing deal, Phase 1 complete/Phase 2 not yet reported.

### Lonza buys five Merck KGaA manufacturing sites in the US
**What happened.** Announced Nov 3: contract manufacturer Lonza is acquiring five US sites from Merck KGaA (the German company, distinct from US-based Merck & Co.) for an undisclosed sum in the low-to-mid hundreds of millions of Swiss francs, closing by mid-2027. The sites specialize in different technologies used to manufacture modern biologic drugs: bioconjugates (St. Louis), ADC payload-linker chemistry (Wisconsin), viral-vector manufacturing for gene therapies (Carlsbad, CA), and lipid-nanoparticle/mRNA-type manufacturing (Indianapolis).
**Why it matters.** It's a sign of continued consolidation in the contract manufacturing industry serving next-generation biologics, cell and gene therapies — infrastructure that drugmakers increasingly outsource rather than build themselves.
**Source:** [Life Science Daily (Nov 9, 2026)](https://lifesciencedaily.news/weekly-news-roundup-9-november-2026/).

### Spyre Therapeutics pivots its anti-TL1A antibody after mixed mid-stage results
**What happened.** Reported Nov 4: Spyre's SPY072, an antibody against the inflammatory signaling protein TL1A (a molecule that drives tissue scarring and chronic inflammation in several autoimmune diseases, also being targeted by Merck's tulisokibart), missed its main goals in a Phase 2 "basket" trial (a single trial testing one drug across several related diseases at once) covering psoriatic arthritis and axial spondyloarthritis — two forms of inflammatory arthritis. In a separate rheumatoid arthritis cohort, the low dose hit its target but the high dose didn't, and the effect size overall fell short of Spyre's internal bar, though the drug was well tolerated throughout. Spyre is now redirecting the program toward hidradenitis suppurativa (the same chronic skin condition described above), after Merck's rival anti-TL1A antibody recently posted encouraging Phase 2b data there; Spyre plans pivotal studies in that indication in 2027.
**Why it matters.** It's a case study in how a crowded, well-funded drug-target class (TL1A inhibition, which attracted several major licensing deals over the past two years) is now sorting out which specific diseases each drug actually works best in, rather than assuming one antibody will work everywhere TL1A is involved.
**Source:** [BioPharm International (Nov 4, 2026)](https://www.biopharminternational.com/view/the-biopharm-brief-a-7-billion-oncology-option-deal-a-tl1a-pivot-and-oral-adalimumab-heads-to-the-clinic). **Stage:** Phase 2, mixed/negative; strategic pivot.

### Biora Therapeutics starts human testing of a swallowable capsule for injectable drugs
**What happened.** Reported Nov 4: Biora received ethics approval in Australia to begin first-in-human testing of its BioJet capsule — an ingestible device that uses a tiny liquid jet to inject a drug directly into the wall of the small intestine, aiming to let patients take by mouth a drug that currently requires injection. The first drug being tested this way is adalimumab, a well-established antibody drug used to dampen the immune system in diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease (it blocks a signaling protein called TNF-alpha that drives inflammation). The study will compare oral capsule dosing against the standard injection for safety and how the drug is absorbed, in healthy volunteers, with dosing planned for December and data expected in early 2027. More than 800 capsules have been tested in animals; no human data exist yet.
**Why it matters.** A reliable way to deliver antibody drugs orally, instead of by injection, has been a long-sought and so-far-elusive goal in biotech, since antibodies are normally destroyed by the digestive system; this is an early but concrete test of one mechanical approach to getting around that problem.
**Source:** [BioPharm International (Nov 4, 2026)](https://www.biopharminternational.com/view/the-biopharm-brief-a-7-billion-oncology-option-deal-a-tl1a-pivot-and-oral-adalimumab-heads-to-the-clinic). **Stage:** first-in-human, not yet dosed in people.

## Top takeaways

1. **Gene-therapy safety is getting a harder look.** Novartis's disclosure of nine tumors (two with suspected vector integration) in Zolgensma-treated children, even without a confirmed causal pattern, lands alongside last week's note that uniQure's Huntington's gene therapy still rests on an externally controlled (not placebo-controlled) study. As more one-shot gene therapies accumulate years of follow-up, expect more of these slow-building safety stories rather than sudden single events.
2. **A major cardiovascular hypothesis just took a hit, but isn't dead.** Pelacarsen's clean miss in the first head-to-head test of Lp(a)-lowering lands as a genuine scientific disappointment, not a design flaw — and leaves the door open for Amgen's and Lilly's differently designed trials to still prove the idea works.
3. **GLP-1 drugs keep annexing new diseases.** This week alone added sleep apnea (orforglipron) to a list that already includes diabetes, obesity, liver disease and cardiovascular risk reduction, while survodutide's neutral (not positive) heart-safety data is a useful reminder that not every entrant in the class will show the same benefits.
4. **Hepatitis delta may finally get a dedicated treatment.** Vir's antibody/siRNA combination clearing virus in the large majority of patients, with a clean safety record, addresses a disease that has had no approved therapy and disproportionately affects the most severely ill hepatitis B patients.
5. **Dealmaking continues to flow toward Chinese biotech licensing and outsourced manufacturing.** Boehringer's up-to-$7B Sino Biopharmaceutical deal and Lonza's acquisition of Merck KGaA manufacturing sites both reflect a pharma industry increasingly buying innovation and capacity rather than building everything in-house.
6. **Several well-funded programs are quietly narrowing their ambitions.** Spyre's TL1A pivot away from arthritis and toward skin disease, and Zura's discontinuation of tibulizumab in hidradenitis suppurativa, show how a single mixed or negative mid-stage readout can reroute years of investment in a crowded drug class.
