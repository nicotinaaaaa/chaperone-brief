This brief covers biology, biotech and pharma news from about Nov 15 to Nov 22, 2026. Items already covered in last week's brief (the Vir hepatitis delta data, the pelacarsen Lp(a) failure, the Pharvaris hereditary angioedema results, the GLP-1/sleep apnea and heart-safety readouts, the Boehringer–Sino Biopharmaceutical and AbbVie–Nxera deals) are not repeated here. As always, many trade-press sites block automated access and some results were headline-only; I include only details I could confirm and flag where a number or outcome was unavailable. Prices and stock moves are as reported by the cited source and may be intraday. This is not investment advice.

**Terms used throughout.**
- **Phase 1/2/3; primary/secondary endpoint; hazard ratio (HR); accelerated approval; BLA; PDUFA date.** See last week's brief for full definitions — in short: Phase 1–3 are the standard stages of human testing culminating in the large Phase 3 trial used for approval; the primary endpoint is the main measurement a trial must win on; HR below 1 favors treatment; accelerated approval lets a drug reach market early on a "surrogate" measurement, pending later proof of real benefit; a BLA is the formal request to approve a biologic; a PDUFA date is the FDA's target decision deadline.
- **Complete response letter (CRL).** The FDA's formal written refusal to approve a drug application as submitted. It lists the specific deficiencies — in trial design, data, manufacturing or safety — that must be resolved before the agency will reconsider.
- **siRNA (small interfering RNA).** A short, lab-made genetic sequence that sticks to a specific piece of messenger RNA and marks it for destruction, silencing a disease-related gene without editing DNA.
- **Bispecific antibody.** An antibody engineered with two different binding arms, so it can grab two different targets (or a target and an immune cell) at once, rather than the one target a normal antibody binds.
- **SERD (selective estrogen receptor degrader).** A drug that attaches to the estrogen receptor on cancer cells and marks it for destruction, cutting off the growth signal that estrogen-driven breast cancers depend on — different from older drugs that just block the receptor without destroying it.
- **ESR1 mutation.** A change in the gene for the estrogen receptor that often arises *after* a breast cancer has been treated with hormone therapy, making the receptor active even without much estrogen around — a common way these cancers develop resistance to earlier treatment.
- **Oncolytic virus.** A virus engineered to preferentially infect and kill cancer cells while triggering the immune system to notice and attack the tumor — a form of immunotherapy delivered as an infection rather than a drug.
- **Cancer vaccine (peptide-based).** Unlike a preventive vaccine, this type is given to someone who already has cancer, to train their own T cells to recognize a protein the tumor makes and attack cells carrying it.
- **Cyclodextrin.** A ring-shaped sugar molecule whose hollow center can trap fat-soluble substances (like cholesterol) while its outer surface dissolves in water — used here as a drug that pulls stuck cholesterol out of cells.
- **In vivo vs. ex vivo cell therapy.** Standard CAR-T and other engineered-cell therapies are made "ex vivo": a patient's cells are removed, genetically reprogrammed in a lab, and infused back in, a process that takes weeks. "In vivo" cell therapy instead delivers the reprogramming instructions (often via a lipid nanoparticle, the same delivery technology used in mRNA vaccines) directly into the body, aiming to reprogram the cells where they already are.
- **Retrotransposon.** A stretch of DNA, originally derived from an ancient virus-like genetic element, that can copy and reinsert itself elsewhere in the genome. Most are inactive, but an insertion in or near a gene can occasionally disrupt how that gene works and cause disease.
- **REMS (Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy).** An FDA-mandated safety program, beyond the normal label, that restricts how a higher-risk drug can be prescribed, dispensed or monitored — for example requiring specific lab tests or specialist certification.
- **Boxed warning.** The strongest warning the FDA can put on a drug label, set off in a bordered box, for risks that are serious or life-threatening.

## 1. Regulatory and safety

### FDA opens a formal investigation into tumors linked to AAV gene therapy, widening last week's Zolgensma story to a second drug
**What happened.** Last week's brief covered Novartis's disclosure of nine tumors in children treated with the gene therapy Zolgensma. This week, the FDA confirmed it is formally investigating the risk and has extended its scope: the agency says the neoplasia (tumor) risk applies not just to Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec, given by IV infusion) but also to Itvisma, a newer version of the same gene therapy given by spinal injection, approved in November 2025, because the two products share the same viral vector and genetic construct. No tumors have been reported with Itvisma so far. Of the tumor cases, three were linked to a cancer-related gene called *GLI1*; in two of those three, lab analysis of the tumor tissue confirmed that the gene therapy's viral DNA had physically inserted itself into the patient's own chromosomes near *GLI1* — turning a theoretical risk, noted only abstractly on the drug's label until now, into a molecularly confirmed mechanism in at least two children. The FDA says it is "expeditiously evaluating" whether the current prescribing information and Novartis's long-term safety monitoring plans are still adequate, which leaves open new labeling requirements or monitoring rules. More than 6,000 patients have been treated with Zolgensma since 2019, and neither the FDA nor Novartis has published an incidence rate against that number. The FDA says it has seen comparable vector-integration tumor events in two other, unrelated gene therapy programs in the past year (one in a program called FBX-101, one after a different delivery route for a therapy called RGX-111), suggesting this may be a mechanism to watch across the broader AAV gene therapy field, not just a Zolgensma-specific problem. The agency says it still judges Zolgensma's overall benefits to outweigh its risks for approved uses, and recommends monitoring treated children and trial participants for tumor formation, without yet specifying a monitoring schedule.

**Biology.** Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare inherited disease in which a faulty gene, *SMN1*, fails to supply enough of a protein that motor neurons (the nerve cells controlling muscles) need to survive; without treatment, the most severe form causes progressive muscle weakness and death in infancy. Zolgensma and Itvisma deliver a working copy of the gene inside a disabled, non-replicating virus (an "AAV vector"). These vectors are designed not to integrate into the patient's DNA, but on rare occasions a copy can insert itself into a chromosome anyway; if that insertion happens to land near a gene that controls cell growth, like *GLI1*, it can in principle help trigger a tumor.

**Why it matters.** This moves the story from "a company disclosed some unexplained tumors" to "the FDA has a confirmed molecular mechanism, has widened the safety question to a second approved product, and is actively deciding whether to change how the drug is labeled or monitored." It also reframes the risk as a class concern for one-shot AAV gene therapies generally — a young and fast-growing part of biotech — rather than an isolated Zolgensma issue, right as one-shot gene therapies for other diseases continue to reach the market.
**Source:** [NeurologyLive (Nov 9, 2026, updated Nov 16)](https://www.neurologylive.com/view/fda-investigates-confirmed-insertional-oncogenesis-sma-gene-therapy). **Stage:** confirmed post-market safety signal under active FDA review; market-relevant for the gene-therapy sector broadly.

### FDA rejects Summit Therapeutics' and Akeso's ivonescimab for lung cancer, citing a trial run mostly in China
**What happened.** The FDA issued a complete response letter, dated Nov 13, refusing to approve ivonescimab (plus chemotherapy) for adults with EGFR-mutated non-squamous lung cancer whose disease progressed after a targeted pill (an EGFR inhibitor) stopped working. The agency's stated reasons: 93% of the key efficacy events (disease progression) in the supporting HARMONi trial occurred in patients enrolled in China, and the FDA said it could not be confident the result — a rise in median progression-free survival from 4.4 months (chemotherapy alone) to 6.8 months (with ivonescimab added) — would apply the same way to a broader, including U.S., population. The trial's overall survival result (whether patients lived longer, not just whether their disease held off longer) also missed statistical significance; Summit's additional analyses limited to Western patients were called "exploratory only" by the agency. The FDA also noted it had raised concerns about the trial's enrollment design back in 2023 and 2025: the global study was effectively a continuation of an earlier China-only trial, so non-Chinese enrollment didn't begin until roughly 1.5 years after the Chinese portion started. The FDA reportedly found no issues with manufacturing or site inspections, and said ivonescimab's safety profile (rates of side effects and treatment-related deaths) looked similar between the drug and chemotherapy-alone arms. Summit's shares fell about 16% the day the rejection became public and roughly another 10% the next day. Akeso's CEO called it the most disappointing moment of her 14-year career but said she sees it as isolated to this specific, narrow indication, with a separate, larger first-line lung cancer trial (HARMONi-3) still due to report data later this year.

**Biology.** Ivonescimab is a bispecific antibody (see glossary) combining two mechanisms in one molecule: it blocks PD-1, an "off switch" some tumors exploit to hide from the immune system, and VEGF, a signal tumors use to grow their own blood supply. EGFR-mutated lung cancer is a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer driven by mutations in a growth-signaling protein, EGFR; patients are typically treated first with EGFR-targeted pills, and this application covered what to do once those pills stop working.

**Why it matters.** This is a high-profile setback for what had been one of the most closely watched new classes of cancer drugs (PD-1/VEGF bispecifics), and a concrete example of the FDA pushing back on trials whose efficacy data is concentrated in a single country's patient population — a scrutiny point that is likely to apply to other China-originated oncology trials now licensed by Western companies (see Section 3 for more on that trend).
**Source:** [Fierce Pharma (Nov 2026)](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/fdas-complete-response-letter-summit-ivonescimab-cites-china-heavy-data-beyond-survival-miss). **Stage:** regulatory rejection (CRL); market-relevant.

### A cluster of rare-disease approvals resolves most of last week's FDA watchlist
Four FDA approvals this week clear several items that were pending as of last week's brief:

- **Livsayis (cemdisiran, Regeneron/Alnylam) approved Nov 18 for generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG).** gMG is an autoimmune disease in which misdirected antibodies block the signal nerves send to muscles, causing fluctuating weakness, including in the eyes, face and breathing muscles. Livsayis is an siRNA (see glossary) given by injection just four times a year — Regeneron's first approved siRNA drug — that lowers a blood protein called C5, blocking a part of the immune system (complement) that drives the nerve-muscle damage. In the Phase 3 NIMBLE trial (published in *The Lancet*), patients on Livsayis improved by 4.5 points on a daily-function scale at 24 weeks versus 2.2 points on placebo (p=.0005), and 76.6% had a clinically meaningful improvement versus 44.1% on placebo. It carries a boxed warning for serious meningococcal infections (a type of bacterial infection the drug's mechanism makes more likely), requiring vaccination before the first dose and ongoing monitoring.
- **Infilbray (venglustat, Sanofi) approved as the first US treatment for neurological Type 3 Gaucher disease.** Gaucher disease is caused by a missing enzyme that normally breaks down a type of fat; in Type 3, the fat also builds up in the brain, causing progressive neurological decline alongside the organ and blood problems seen in milder forms. Infilbray is a once-daily pill (an enzyme-pathway blocker, not a replacement enzyme) that, unlike most Gaucher treatments, can cross into the brain, where it reduces production of the fat the missing enzyme can't clear.
- **Lembaz (adrabetadex, Beren Therapeutics) approved to reduce the risk of death in infantile-onset Niemann-Pick disease type C (NPC).** NPC is a rare inherited disorder in which a broken cholesterol-transport gene (*NPC1* or *NPC2*) causes fatty material to build up in cells, especially in the brain; in the infantile form, neurological symptoms start before age 6, and mean age at death has been as young as 5.6 years for the earliest-onset cases. Lembaz is a modified cyclodextrin (see glossary) given by spinal injection every two weeks, designed to pull cholesterol back out of trapped cells. Based on 36 treated patients compared with matched external controls, median survival was 8.5 years versus 4.9 years — a 55% reduction in the risk of death — making it the first NPC therapy shown to extend survival (two previously approved NPC drugs have not shown this). It carries a boxed warning for hearing loss, which can be permanent, and requires regular hearing tests.
- **Nelkarmi (neladalkib, GSK, licensed from Nuvalent) granted accelerated approval for previously treated ALK-positive lung cancer.** This is GSK's second cancer drug approval stemming from its Nuvalent acquisition, giving patients whose ALK-driven lung cancer has stopped responding to an earlier targeted drug a new option; GSK reports a 49% response rate in the pivotal data.

**Why it matters.** Taken together, these resolve the cemdisiran/Livsayis and neladalkib/Nelkarmi items flagged as pending in last week's watchlist, and continue a pattern (also noted last week) of rare-disease approvals making up an outsized share of the FDA's recent decisions.
**Source:** [RAPS Regulatory Recon (Nov 19, 2026)](https://www.raps.org/resource/recon-fda-approves-drugs-for-four-rare-diseases-pfizer-signs-2-6b-licensing-deal-in-china-for-in-vivo-car-t.html); [PharmExec (Nov 19, 2026)](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/fda-approves-livsayis-first-sirna-therapy-myasthenia-gravis); [MedCity News / pharmaphorum (Nov 2026)](https://pharmaphorum.com/news/fda-clears-sanofis-type-3-gaucher-therapy-infilbray); [NeurologyLive (Nov 17, 2026)](https://www.neurologylive.com/view/fda-approves-adrabetadex-first-therapy-improve-survival-infantile-onset-niemann-pick-disease-type-c); [Fierce Pharma (Nov 18, 2026)](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/gsk-scores-fda-approval-nelkarmi-2nd-lung-cancer-drug-nuvalent-deal). **Stage:** all newly approved; market-relevant.

### Roche's giredestrant approved for ESR1-mutated breast cancer — a different trial and setting from the adjuvant decision still pending Nov 30
**What happened.** The FDA approved giredestrant (brand name Wilserdly, Genentech/Roche), an oral SERD (see glossary), combined with the pill everolimus, for adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative, ESR1-mutated breast cancer that is locally advanced or metastatic and has progressed after hormone therapy, including after a CDK4/6 inhibitor (a different, commonly used class of breast cancer pill). This is based on the Phase 3 evERA trial: in patients with the ESR1 mutation specifically, giredestrant plus everolimus cut the risk of progression or death by 62% versus standard hormone therapy plus everolimus (hazard ratio 0.38), with median progression-free survival of 10.0 versus 5.5 months. Overall survival data are still immature (not yet statistically conclusive either way). Side effects affecting at least a fifth of patients included drops in several blood cell counts, liver enzyme changes, mouth sores, rash, fatigue and diarrhea; the label also warns about slowed heart rate and the need for contraception during treatment.

**Why this is not the same decision flagged in last week's watchlist.** Last week's brief listed "Giredestrant (Roche), Nov 30" as a pending decision for *early* (adjuvant — i.e., after surgery, before any sign the cancer has returned) breast cancer, based on a separate trial called lidERA. That decision is still pending; this week's approval is for a *different* trial (evERA) in a *different*, more advanced setting (ESR1-mutated, locally advanced/metastatic disease, combined with everolimus). If approved, the lidERA adjuvant indication would reportedly be the first new adjuvant endocrine therapy for early breast cancer in more than two decades — that outcome is still unknown as of this writing.
**Source:** [AJMC (Nov 18, 2026)](https://www.ajmc.com/view/fda-approves-oral-giredestrant-regimen-for-esr1-mutated-breast-cancer). **Stage:** Phase 3, approved (evERA/metastatic setting); adjuvant (lidERA) decision still pending Nov 30.

### Cytokinetics' Myqorzo label gains head-to-head data against a beta-blocker
**What happened.** The FDA approved a label update for Myqorzo (aficamten, Cytokinetics) adding results from the Phase 3 MAPLE-HCM trial, which compared aficamten directly against metoprolol (a beta-blocker, long used as a first-line treatment for this condition despite limited rigorous evidence) in 175 adults with symptomatic obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — a condition where the heart muscle thickens and partly blocks blood flow out of the heart, causing breathlessness, chest pain and fainting. Aficamten improved patients' peak oxygen uptake during exercise (a measure of functional capacity) by 1.1 mL/kg/min over 24 weeks, while metoprolol patients' uptake fell by 1.2 mL/kg/min; aficamten also beat metoprolol on 5 of 6 secondary measures, including symptom scores and a cardiac stress biomarker. Hypertension was more common with aficamten (15% vs. 3%); dizziness was more common with metoprolol. The drug still carries a boxed warning about reduced heart pumping function and remains available only through a restricted safety program (REMS).

**Why it matters.** Last week's watchlist flagged this decision (originally due around Nov 14) as unconfirmed; it has now resolved, and Cytokinetics says the label change makes Myqorzo the only drug in its class (cardiac myosin inhibitors) with head-to-head evidence against a beta-blocker on its US label, which the company is positioning as a competitive edge against Bristol Myers Squibb's rival drug Camzyos (mavacamten) in the same disease.
**Source:** [AJMC (Nov 17, 2026)](https://www.ajmc.com/view/fda-adds-head-to-head-maple-hcm-data-to-aficamten-label-in-ohcm). **Stage:** label expansion based on Phase 3 data; approved.

### Still pending: Capricor and Savara decisions due this week
Both Capricor's deramiocel (a cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, an inherited, progressive muscle-wasting disease mainly affecting boys) and Savara's molgramostim (an inhaled therapy for autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, a rare lung disease where protein-like material clogs the air sacs) had FDA decisions expected around Nov 22. As of this writing I found no confirmed outcome for either; both remain on the watchlist below.

## 2. Clinical trials

### CG Oncology's oncolytic virus cuts bladder cancer recurrence risk by 68% in a second pivotal trial
**What happened.** Reported Nov 16: CG Oncology's cretostimogene, given after surgery to patients with intermediate-risk and high-grade non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, cut the relative risk of cancer coming back by 68% compared with standard post-surgery surveillance over 17 months of follow-up in the Phase 3 PIVOT-006 trial. Exact patient counts, the hazard ratio, and p-value were not yet disclosed; full data are due at a medical meeting in December. Side effects were consistent with prior studies, with no severe (grade 3+) drug-related events and no patients stopping treatment due to side effects. Analysts called the result "exceptional," well above the 30–40% reduction investors had been hoping for. CG's shares rose about 12% on the news, pushing the company's market value close to $8 billion; one analyst estimates peak US sales up to $3.2 billion by 2035 if approved in this setting.

**Biology.** Non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) means the tumor is confined to the bladder's inner lining and hasn't grown into the muscle wall. Standard care is surgical removal followed by BCG, an immunotherapy placed directly into the bladder, but BCG doesn't always work and many responders still relapse. Cretostimogene is an oncolytic virus (see glossary) — it infects and kills cancer cells directly while also flagging them to the immune system.

**Why it matters.** This is cretostimogene's second positive Phase 3 readout in a different NMIBC setting (the company is separately pursuing approval in BCG-unresponsive patients, expected to file in early 2027), positioning it as a potential "backbone" therapy usable across multiple stages of this common cancer. A second regulatory filing is planned for 2027.
**Source:** [BioPharma Dive (Nov 16, 2026)](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/cg-oncology-cretostimogene-adjuvant-bladder-cancer-results-pivot/874740/). **Stage:** Phase 3, positive (topline); market-relevant.

### A cancer vaccine cuts the risk of death by 41% in a Phase 3 leukemia maintenance trial
**What happened.** Reported Nov 17–18: Sellas Life Sciences' galinpepimut-S (GPS), an off-the-shelf cancer vaccine, reduced the risk of death by 41% (hazard ratio 0.59, p=.035) compared with best available therapy in the Phase 3 REGAL trial, which enrolled 126 adults with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) who had reached a second or later complete remission after relapse and were not candidates for a stem cell transplant. Median overall survival was 24.0 months with GPS versus 14.7 months on comparator therapy (observation, a hypomethylating drug, venetoclax, and/or low-dose chemotherapy). Side effects were mostly mild-to-moderate injection-site reactions, with no new safety signals. Sellas plans to discuss an accelerated approval pathway with the FDA and submit a formal application in the first half of 2027.

**Biology.** AML is a fast-growing cancer of the blood and bone marrow in which immature white blood cells crowd out healthy ones. "Complete remission" means the disease has been driven down to undetectable levels and blood counts have recovered, but relapse is common, so "maintenance" therapy is given afterward to try to keep the disease from coming back. Unlike a preventive vaccine, galinpepimut-S is a therapeutic cancer vaccine: it contains four lab-made peptide fragments of a protein called WT1, which many AML cells overproduce, designed to train the patient's own T cells to find and kill cells carrying that protein.

**Why it matters.** A clear overall-survival benefit — not just a surrogate marker — in a difficult-to-treat relapsed/maintenance AML population would be a significant result for a treatment class (therapeutic cancer vaccines) that has had a mixed track record; full secondary-endpoint data are still to come.
**Source:** [CancerNetwork (Nov 18, 2026)](https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/galinpepimut-s-improves-overall-survival-in-phase-3-aml-maintenance-trial). **Stage:** Phase 3, positive (topline); market-relevant.

### Lower-dose CagriSema matches full-dose weight loss at ObesityWeek
**What happened.** Presented at ObesityWeek (Nov 14–17) and published simultaneously in *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology*: in Novo Nordisk's REDEFINE 9 trial, 309 adults with obesity were randomized to one of two lower doses of CagriSema (a combined appetite-hormone drug) — 1.7 mg or 1.0 mg of each component — or placebo. At 68 weeks, both lower doses produced essentially identical weight loss: 20.7% and 21%, respectively, versus 2% on placebo (both p<.0001) — and 95–96% of patients lost at least 5% of their body weight, versus a third on placebo. No numerical comparison to CagriSema's standard 2.4 mg dose or to rival drug tirzepatide was given. Side effects were mostly mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal issues, with discontinuation rates of 2.9–4.9% across groups and no deaths. The trial's presenter noted this isn't simply explained by better adherence at lower doses. CagriSema is not yet approved in the US; an FDA obesity-indication decision is expected by the end of 2026.

**Why it matters.** If lower doses work just as well, that could let doctors offer patients a version with a potentially better side-effect profile without sacrificing efficacy — relevant given how often GI side effects cause people to stop GLP-1-class drugs. No stock-reaction figures were reported for this specific data.
**Source:** [Healio (Nov 17, 2026)](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20261117/lowerdose-cagrisema-reduces-body-weight-vs-placebo). **Stage:** Phase 3 (REDEFINE 9), positive; market-relevant (pending FDA decision).

### A small, early human study finds CAR-T cells can get into the brain and reshape immune activity in multiple sclerosis
**What happened.** Published in *Cell* this week: a German/Swiss/US research team treated five people with progressive or relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (MS) with their own re-engineered CAR-T cells (the same general technology used in blood cancer, repurposed here) targeting CD19, a marker on B cells (an immune cell type implicated in MS). This was designed primarily to test safety and whether the cells could reach the central nervous system, not to measure clinical benefit. The CAR-T cells did infiltrate the cerebrospinal fluid (the fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord), reshaped the population of B cells found there, and sustainably reduced antibody-related markers in that fluid; the infiltrating cells also showed distinctive activity changes not seen in blood. The authors call for larger controlled trials to test safety, durability and efficacy.

**Biology.** Multiple sclerosis is a chronic disease in which the immune system attacks the protective coating (myelin) around nerve fibers in the brain and spinal cord, disrupting nerve signaling and causing symptoms from fatigue and numbness to vision loss and mobility problems over time. Current MS drugs control relapses reasonably well but do less for the slow background progression some patients experience — one theory is that disease activity trapped behind the blood-brain barrier is partly to blame, and standard antibody drugs may not fully reach it. CAR-T cells, lab-engineered immune cells carrying an artificial "chimeric antigen receptor," are already approved for several blood cancers; here, the same cell-engineering approach is being tested as a way to clear out disease-driving immune cells specifically within the central nervous system.

**Why it matters.** This is an early, exploratory, five-patient study, not a registrational trial, but it adds mechanistic evidence that engineered T cells can access and act within a compartment of the body that current MS drugs may only partially reach — relevant context for the broader push (see Pfizer/Starna deal in Section 3) to extend CAR-T-style approaches from cancer into autoimmune disease.
**Source:** Fischbach et al., *Cell* (2026), DOI: [10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.044](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.044), via PubMed. **Stage:** first-in-human, exploratory (n=5); not yet a registered efficacy trial.

### A crowded, mostly disappointing week for Alzheimer's disease drug development
Several companies reported Alzheimer's-related data this week, continuing a pattern (also seen in last week's sabirnetug failure) of a field struggling to translate biological activity into clinical benefit:
- **Eli Lilly's mevidalen failed a Phase 2 Alzheimer's study.** No further detail was available on the specific measure it missed.
- **AriBio's repurposed erectile-dysfunction drug produced only "tepid" results** when tested for Alzheimer's disease — the specific drug reuses a medicine originally developed for a different condition.
- **Priavoid's amyloid-detangling pill failed to meaningfully slow disease progression** in a Phase 2 Alzheimer's study. ("Amyloid-detangling" refers to breaking up the sticky protein clumps — amyloid plaques — associated with the disease, rather than simply preventing new ones from forming.)
- **One partial bright spot:** Biogen's low-dose tau-targeting drug "fared better" after nearly two years of follow-up, though specifics were not disclosed. (Tau is the other hallmark abnormal protein in Alzheimer's, forming tangles inside neurons, distinct from amyloid plaques outside them.)
- **A tangential but notable result:** Bristol Myers Squibb's schizophrenia drug showed preliminary benefit for Alzheimer's-related psychosis (hallucinations or delusions that can accompany the disease), a different and narrower target than slowing the underlying disease itself.

**Why it matters.** Even after several hard-won amyloid-plaque antibody approvals in recent years, this week's cluster — like last week's sabirnetug failure — underscores how difficult it remains to move the needle on Alzheimer's progression itself, even as companies find narrower wins (like psychosis symptom control) around the edges of the disease.
**Source:** [RAPS Regulatory Recon (Nov 17, 2026)](https://www.raps.org/resource/recon-fda-approves-roche-s-oral-serd-for-late-stage-breast-cancer-lilly-strikes-up-to-5-9b-cancer-deal-with-stipple-bio.html). **Stage:** mixed; mostly Phase 2, negative, with one Phase 2 exception noted as positive.

## 3. Business, deals and markets

### Pfizer bets $2.6 billion on "in vivo" CAR-T technology for autoimmune disease
**What happened.** Announced Nov 18: Pfizer licensed three preclinical programs from Starna Therapeutics, a Jiangsu, China-based biotech founded in 2021, paying $330 million upfront with up to $2.3 billion more in milestones (total potential value $2.6 billion) plus royalties, and gaining rights to the programs for oncology as well. Pfizer gets rights outside Greater China, with an option to add Greater China later; manufacturing for early trials is planned at Pfizer's US sites. The three programs target autoimmune diseases (not yet named) using Starna's lipid-nanoparticle and RNA delivery platform — the same general type of delivery system used in mRNA vaccines — to reprogram T cells into CAR-T cells directly inside the patient's body ("in vivo"), rather than removing cells, engineering them in a lab, and infusing them back ("ex vivo"), which is how all currently approved CAR-T therapies work.

**Why it matters.** This is Pfizer's first deal in this specific niche and a bet that in vivo cell engineering could sidestep the weeks-long, expensive manufacturing process that limits standard CAR-T today — relevant context alongside the small human MS CAR-T study described in Section 2, as the broader industry explores moving engineered-cell approaches from cancer into autoimmune disease. It also continues the pattern, flagged in last week's brief, of major Western pharma companies licensing early Chinese biotech assets.
**Source:** [Fierce Biotech (Nov 2026)](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/pfizer-enters-vivo-car-t-space-3-program-26b-deal-chinas-starna). **Stage:** licensing deal, preclinical assets.

### Eli Lilly pays up to $5.9 billion for a tumor-specific target-discovery platform
**What happened.** Announced around Nov 16: Lilly signed a collaboration potentially worth $5.9 billion with Stipple Bio, a startup co-founded by researchers from Fred Hutch Cancer Center and UCSF, built around a discovery platform called "Pointillist" that identifies protein markers (epitopes) found specifically on cancer cells but not on healthy tissue. The upfront payment was not disclosed; Lilly is also making an equity investment in Stipple and will pay tiered royalties on anything that reaches the market. Lilly gets exclusive worldwide rights to develop and sell products from the collaboration, which will apply the platform across multiple cancer drug types (including antibody-drug conjugates and T-cell engagers, see last week's glossary) and multiple, not-yet-determined targets.

**Why it matters.** Most cancer drugs target proteins that are merely overexpressed on tumors but still present at low levels on healthy cells, which causes "on-target, off-tumor" side effects that limit how much drug can safely be given. A platform built specifically to find truly tumor-exclusive targets addresses a core bottleneck in making cancer drugs both more effective and more tolerable — one reason large pharma companies keep paying premiums for novel target-discovery technology rather than building it in-house (see also the Boehringer–Sino Biopharmaceutical deal covered last week).
**Source:** [Fierce Biotech (Nov 2026)](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/eli-lilly-dots-59b-deal-stipple-unlock-oncology-targets). **Stage:** platform collaboration, pre-clinical/early-stage.

### Johnson & Johnson buys an eye-disease biotech for up to $3.3 billion
**What happened.** Announced Nov 16: J&J agreed to acquire Ollin Biosciences for $2.2 billion upfront plus up to roughly $1.125 billion in milestone payments (combined value up to about $3.3 billion). The deal brings J&J OLN324, a Phase 3 bispecific antibody — built in a smaller "minibody" format intended to allow more drug to reach the eye — that blocks two signals at once: VEGF (which drives abnormal, leaky blood vessel growth) and Ang2 (angiopoietin-2, a separate pathway involved in vessel stability). It targets wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema, two leading causes of vision loss that together affect more than 40 million people worldwide. Separately, Blackstone Life Sciences agreed to pay up to $750 million over several years to fund OLN324's ongoing Phase 3 development, in exchange for future milestone payments and royalties. The deal is expected to close by the end of 2026.

**Why it matters.** It's another entrant in the crowded and lucrative retinal-disease drug market, competing with existing VEGF-blocking drugs by adding a second mechanism (Ang2) in hopes of controlling fluid buildup in the eye more durably — and another example of private capital (via Blackstone) directly co-funding late-stage pharma trials rather than big companies bearing the full cost alone.
**Source:** [Johnson & Johnson press release (Nov 16, 2026)](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/johnson-johnson-to-acquire-ollin-biosciences-and-late-stage-bispecific-antibody-with-best-in-class-potential-in-ophthalmology). **Stage:** acquisition, Phase 3 asset.

## 4. Basic science

### Two companion studies finally explain the molecular cause of a devastating, geographically concentrated movement disorder
**What happened.** Published this week in *Cell*: a team at NYU Langone Health, working with collaborators at Massachusetts General Hospital and a patient advocacy center in the Philippines, built the first good animal model of X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism (XDP) by literally rewriting part of the mouse genome to carry the exact 78,000-base-pair human mutation that causes the disease — a technique the authors call "genome writing," going beyond simpler gene-editing tools like CRISPR, which typically change only a few DNA letters at a time. XDP is an adult-onset movement disorder that almost exclusively affects men of Filipino descent, caused by an extra, misplaced stretch of repetitive DNA (a "retrotransposon," see glossary) that lands inside a gene called *TAF1*, one of the general machinery genes cells use to switch other genes on and off. Using their new mouse model, the researchers found that this intruding DNA segment doesn't just reduce the amount of a hybrid, partly broken version of the *TAF1* protein that gets made — the intruding segment is itself being actively read by the cell's transcription machinery, and in doing so it physically blocks normal transcription from completing, like a roadblock partway through the gene. Mice engineered to carry the mutation in their nervous system developed severe muscle control problems and shrinkage (atrophy) in the same brain region (the striatum) affected in human patients, and died within two months if male. In a companion paper, the same broader team found, through both the humanized mice and autopsy tissue from real XDP patients, that oligodendrocytes — the brain's cells responsible for making myelin, the fatty insulation around nerve fibers — are reduced and dysfunctional in XDP, and that myelin itself breaks down in affected brain regions (including the prefrontal cortex), identifying this as a previously unrecognized, major contributor to the disease. Separately, the first paper found that artificially boosting activity of the broken hybrid gene using a gene-activation technique (CRISPRa, a variant of CRISPR that turns a gene up rather than editing its sequence) restored normal cell survival in lab-grown cells, hinting at a possible therapeutic strategy.

**Why it matters.** XDP has lacked a good animal model for years, which has been a major obstacle to developing any treatment for a disease that is otherwise a death sentence over one to two decades for the men it affects (overwhelmingly concentrated in and around Panay Island in the Philippines, where it's believed a single founder mutation, centuries old, has spread through the population). This pair of studies both explains a genuinely new disease mechanism — a retrotransposon acting as a physical transcription roadblock, rather than simply lowering gene dosage — and newly implicates myelin loss, rather than only direct neuron death, as central to how the disease unfolds; together they open a concrete, testable path (boosting the damaged gene's activity) toward an eventual therapy.
**Source:** Zhang et al. and Prakash et al., *Cell* (2026), via PubMed, DOI: [10.1016/j.cell.2026.07.057](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.07.057) and [10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.046](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.10.046). **Stage:** basic/translational research (mouse models and human autopsy tissue); not a clinical trial.

## Top takeaways

1. **Gene-therapy safety scrutiny has moved from "a company disclosed something" to "the FDA has confirmed a mechanism and is actively investigating."** The widening of the Zolgensma tumor story to include Itvisma, with molecularly confirmed vector integration in two cases and explicit parallels drawn to two other unrelated AAV gene-therapy programs, suggests this is shaping up as a safety theme for the AAV gene-therapy field broadly, not a single-drug issue.
2. **Regulators are scrutinizing where pivotal trial data actually comes from.** The ivonescimab rejection turned heavily on a trial whose efficacy data was 93% Chinese, a concern that is likely to recur as more Western companies license and file for approval of China-originated oncology drugs and trials (a trend also visible in this week's and last week's dealmaking).
3. **A backlog of rare-disease decisions is clearing.** Four FDA approvals this week (for myasthenia gravis, two different ultra-rare metabolic/neurological diseases, and a lung cancer subtype) resolve several items flagged as pending last week, continuing a pattern of rare-disease-heavy approval activity this quarter.
4. **Engineered cell therapy is visibly expanding beyond cancer, and beyond the lab-manufacturing bottleneck.** Pfizer's $2.6B bet on "in vivo" CAR-T for autoimmune disease, alongside a small but genuine first-in-human result showing CD19 CAR-T cells reaching the brain's immune compartment in multiple sclerosis patients, point to the same broader shift: moving engineered T-cell therapy out of cancer-only, lab-manufactured use.
5. **Big pharma keeps paying premiums for novel discovery platforms over building in-house.** Lilly's up-to-$5.9B bet on Stipple's tumor-specific target-discovery platform and Isomorphic Labs' $5B AI-drug-discovery raise both reflect how much capital is still flowing toward companies that promise better ways to find drug targets, rather than new drugs themselves.
6. **A genuinely novel basic-science finding, after two straight weeks without one.** This week's pair of *Cell* papers on X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism combine a technically striking method (rewriting 78,000 base pairs of mouse DNA to match a human patient mutation) with two real mechanistic discoveries — a retrotransposon acting as a transcriptional roadblock, and myelin loss as a previously unrecognized disease driver — plus a plausible therapeutic lead, for a disease that has had essentially no treatment options.
