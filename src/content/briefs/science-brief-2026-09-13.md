## 1. The week's biggest story: two cardiovascular mechanisms collapse in a row

**Novartis/Ionis pelacarsen misses in Lp(a)HORIZON; Novo Nordisk kills two more ziltivekimab heart failure trials**

*Stage: Late clinical / market-relevant*

Novartis disclosed on Sept 4 that pelacarsen — an *antisense* drug that cuts lipoprotein(a) — failed the Phase 3 Lp(a)HORIZON trial in >8,300 high-risk patients followed for roughly six years. The drug lowered Lp(a) as designed but did not significantly reduce cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke or urgent revascularization versus placebo. Three days later, Novo Nordisk terminated two late-stage heart failure trials of ziltivekimab, its anti-IL-6 antibody, after an independent monitoring committee judged them unlikely to differ from the already-failed ZEUS outcomes trial.

**Biological background.** Two long-standing hypotheses were on trial here. Lp(a) is a genetically determined LDL-like particle carrying apolipoprotein(a), and *Mendelian randomization* studies have consistently linked high Lp(a) to heart attack risk. That method exploits the fact that gene variants are dealt out essentially at random at conception — nature running the randomization that a trial would otherwise have to pay for. Comparing people who happened to inherit high-Lp(a) variants against those who didn't therefore approximates a lifelong randomized trial of Lp(a) exposure, without the confounders (diet, wealth, exercise) that make ordinary observational studies untrustworthy. Those studies said Lp(a) causes heart attacks, so lowering it *should* lower events.

Worth holding onto, given how this week went: Mendelian randomization measures a lifetime of elevated exposure starting in the womb, while a drug trial measures a few years of lowering in patients who already have established disease. Those are not the same experiment, which is one live explanation for the miss.

Pelacarsen attacks Lp(a) with *antisense* — a short synthetic strand of DNA-like material designed to base-pair with one specific messenger RNA inside the cell and mark it for destruction. Conventional drugs block a protein after it exists; antisense intercepts the instructions so the protein is never built. Here the target is the *LPA* transcript, so the liver simply makes less apolipoprotein(a).

Separately, the *inflammation hypothesis* holds that the heart attack risk left over after cholesterol is controlled — "residual risk" — comes from chronic inflammation in the artery wall, signalled through the molecule IL-6. That idea was validated in principle by the CANTOS trial a decade ago, which showed that an antibody damping inflammation cut cardiovascular events without touching cholesterol at all. Ziltivekimab was built to block IL-6 directly.

So the two hypotheses on trial this fortnight were: **(1) the Lp(a) hypothesis** — that lowering this inherited particle prevents heart attacks, tested by pelacarsen; and **(2) the inflammation hypothesis** — that damping IL-6 inflammation prevents them, tested by ziltivekimab. Both drugs moved their intended biological target. Neither prevented the events. Both hypotheses have now hit hard clinical data and lost.

**Why it matters.** This is the difference between a *biomarker* and a *cause you can drug*. Pelacarsen's failure does not disprove Lp(a) causality, but it raises the bar for Amgen's olpasiran and Lilly's lepodisiran, both in Phase 3 with the same rationale. Oppenheimer analysts went further on IL-6, calling it "an invalid target" in cardiovascular disease. Expect capital to rotate out of the whole residual-risk category.

**Market reaction.** Ionis fell ~13.1% to $50.51 in premarket trading on Sept 8 (the first session after the Labor Day holiday), near its 52-week low of $50.01 against a 52-week high of $86.74 — and this follows a July sell-off on eplontersen's separate cardiovascular failure. Amgen and Lilly shares also traded lower on read-through.

## 2. Regulatory decisions

### Ionis wins first-ever approval for Alexander disease (Sep 3)

*Stage: Approved / market-relevant*

The FDA approved Zanvastro (zilganersen), an antisense drug injected into the spinal fluid, for pediatric and adult Alexander disease — nearly three weeks ahead of its Sep 22 PDUFA date. Approval rested on functional benefit at 61 weeks: walking speed in patients aged ≥5, and a broader gross motor assessment in ages 2–4.

**Biological background.** *Alexander disease* is an ultra-rare, progressive and often fatal brain disorder. Most cases begin in infancy or childhood; children lose motor milestones, develop seizures and an enlarged head, and deteriorate over years. It is a *leukodystrophy* — a disease of the brain's white matter, the insulated wiring that carries signals between regions.

The cause is dominant gain-of-function mutations in *GFAP*, the intermediate filament gene of astrocytes — the star-shaped support cells that maintain neurons and help build myelin, the fatty insulation around nerve fibers. Mutant GFAP clumps into deposits called Rosenthal fibers, which poison astrocyte function and destroy myelin. Zilganersen doesn't repair the gene — it degrades *GFAP* mRNA so less of the toxic protein is made at all.

**Why it matters.** Scientifically it's a clean proof that knocking down a toxic gain-of-function protein in the CNS changes clinical trajectory. Commercially it's Ionis's first independently launched neurology drug — a milestone in its shift from partner-dependent royalties to a commercial company. The timing was fortunate: it landed days before the pelacarsen news. Shares moved only ~+0.6% premarket on the approval, reflecting the ultra-rare patient population.

### Bayer's sevabertinib expanded to first-line HER2-mutant lung cancer (Sep 9)

*Stage: Accelerated approval / market-relevant*

The FDA granted accelerated approval to sevabertinib (Hyrnuo) for treatment-naïve patients with locally advanced or metastatic non-squamous NSCLC carrying HER2 (*ERBB2*) tyrosine kinase domain mutations, on a 75% objective response rate in the Phase I/II SOHO-01 trial. The drug had accelerated approval in the previously-treated setting since November 2025.

**Biological background.** *Non-small cell lung cancer* is the common form of lung cancer, about 85% of cases. It is sorted by the cell type the tumor grew out of. *Squamous* tumors arise from the flat lining cells of the larger central airways and are tightly linked to smoking. *Adenocarcinoma* arises instead from the mucus-producing glandular cells, usually further out toward the edge of the lung; it is the single most common subtype and the one most often seen in never-smokers and in women.

The two labels in this approval aren't synonyms, which is worth untangling. *Adenocarcinoma* is a specific diagnosis. *Non-squamous* is a category defined by exclusion — everything that isn't squamous, which in practice is adenocarcinoma plus the rarer large-cell tumors. Regulators write labels in the broader "non-squamous" terms because the squamous/non-squamous split is what drives real treatment decisions, but the HER2 mutations this drug targets sit overwhelmingly in the adenocarcinoma group. "Metastatic" means the cancer has spread beyond the lung, at which point surgery is no longer curative.

About 2–4% of lung adenocarcinomas carry activating insertions or point mutations in the HER2 kinase domain — a distinct driver from the HER2 amplification familiar from breast cancer. HER2 is a growth-signal receptor on the cell surface; these mutations jam it permanently on, so the cell is told to divide continuously. Sevabertinib is a reversible small molecule tuned to hit mutant HER2 while largely sparing wild-type EGFR, which is what limits the tolerability of older pan-HER inhibitors.

**Why it matters.** Moving a targeted agent into first line displaces chemo-immunotherapy for this biomarker group and reinforces the case for universal NGS testing at diagnosis. Confirmatory trials are still outstanding — this remains accelerated approval, not full.

## 3. Clinical readouts

### AbbVie's etentamig hits both endpoints in Phase 3 myeloma (Sep 3)

*Stage: Phase 3 / market-relevant*

In CERVINO (n=393, median three prior lines), the BCMA×CD3 bispecific T-cell engager etentamig delivered a 74% objective response rate versus investigator's choice of standard therapy, and cut the risk of progression or death by 60% (HR 0.40). Both co-primary endpoints were met. AbbVie reported low rates of cytokine release syndrome and fatal infection on a monthly dosing schedule after a single step-up dose.

**Biological background.** *Multiple myeloma* is a cancer of plasma cells, the bone-marrow cells that normally manufacture antibodies. The malignant clone crowds out healthy marrow and eats away at bone, producing the characteristic bone pain, fractures, anemia, kidney damage and infections. It is treatable but not curable: patients cycle through successive drug classes, and "relapsed/refractory, triple-class exposed" means they have already exhausted the three main ones.

BCMA is a surface receptor that plasma cells — and therefore myeloma cells — depend on for survival. A bispecific antibody grabs BCMA with one arm and CD3 on a T cell with the other, forcing an immune synapse and killing the tumor cell without engineering the patient's T cells ex vivo, as CAR-T requires.

**Why it matters.** The differentiator is the safety and dosing profile, not the response rate. A single step-up dose and monthly administration would make this far more deliverable in community oncology than existing BCMA bispecifics, which need multiple step-ups and inpatient monitoring. Full data at the International Myeloma Society meeting, Glasgow, Sep 23–26.

Sources: [AbbVie](https://news.abbvie.com/2026-09-03-AbbVie-Announces-Positive-Topline-Results-from-the-Phase-3-CERVINO-Trial-Showing-Etentamig-Significantly-Improved-Response-Rate-and-Progression-Free-Survival-in-Patients-with-Relapsed-Refractory-Multiple-Myeloma) · [The Pharma Letter](https://www.thepharmaletter.com/biotech-news/abbvie-s-etentamig-hits-dual-primary-endpoints-in-phase-iii-relapsed-refractory-multiple-myeloma-trial)

### Inhibrx posts first convincing win for OX40 agonism (Sep 8)

*Stage: Phase 2 / early-to-mid clinical*

In the randomized Phase 2 HexAgon study, INBRX-106 plus pembrolizumab produced a 48.3% confirmed response rate versus 26.5% for pembrolizumab alone in first-line PD-L1-high (CPS ≥20) head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Six-month PFS was 72.4% vs 42.8%; complete responses 13.8% vs 0%. In HPV-positive patients the gap was dramatic: 80.0% vs 33.3% response, 30% vs 0% complete responses, with median PFS not yet reached versus 4.6 months.

**Biological background.** *Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma* arises in the flat lining cells of the mouth, throat and voice box. Historically it was a smoking-and-drinking disease, but a growing share is now caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection of the throat. HPV-positive tumors behave as a biologically distinct disease — they occur in younger patients and respond better to treatment, because the viral proteins driving them are foreign flags the immune system can recognize.

OX40 (CD134) is a costimulatory receptor that appears on T cells after activation; engaging it is supposed to amplify and sustain an anti-tumor response. The target has a long graveyard of failed conventional agonist antibodies, which couldn't cluster the receptor tightly enough to signal. INBRX-106 is *hexavalent* — six binding domains — specifically to force that clustering.

**Why it matters.** This is the field's first real signal that OX40 can work, and it validates valency engineering as the fix for a whole class of costimulatory targets. The HPV-positive subgroup is the standout, biologically plausible because viral neoantigens give T cells something clear to attack. Caveats: Phase 2, unconfirmed, small subgroups. Inhibrx is adding ~50 more HPV+ oropharyngeal patients to support a possible accelerated approval path. Market reaction was notably muted.

Sources: [STAT, Sep 8](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/08/inhibrx-inbrx-106-ox40-immunotherapy-head-and-neck-cancer-hpv-positive/) · [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/inhibrxs-inbrx-106-nearly-doubles-response-rate-and-achieves-interim-median-pfs-of-9-6-months-in-phase-2-hnscc-study)

## 4. Discovery science

### Semaglutide extends lifespan in aged mice — and looks like it's doing more than weight loss (Nature, Sep 2)

*Stage: Preclinical*

Starting semaglutide in 20-month-old female C57BL/6 mice — roughly late middle age — for three months raised median lifespan to 834 days versus 742 days in controls, about 12% or ~100 extra days. Treated animals also showed better motor coordination, muscle function, locomotor activity, cognition, glycemic control and insulin sensitivity, and attenuation of several molecular hallmarks of aging.

**Biological background.** GLP-1 receptor agonists were built to drive insulin secretion and satiety. But the paper reports that semaglutide modulates conserved nutrient-sensing pathways — the same mTOR/AMPK-adjacent machinery that caloric restriction acts through, the oldest known lifespan intervention in mammals. On some measures (memory retention, glycemic control) semaglutide outperformed calorie restriction directly.

**Why it matters.** If GLP-1 agonists partially phenocopy caloric restriction rather than merely removing the metabolic burden of excess fat, the geroscience implications are large and the commercial implications larger still. Sharp caveats: mice are not people, only female mice were reported, and a 12% median lifespan gain is modest by geroscience standards. No human longevity claim is supported by this.

Sources: [Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10940-7) · [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02757-1) · [NIH](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/glp-1-treatment-late-life-extends-lifespan-animal-model)

### The largest personality genetics study ever: 1,260 variants across the Big Five (Nature, Sep 2)

*Stage: Basic research*

A meta-analysis of 46 cohorts covering 611,000–1.14 million people identified 1,260 common variants associated with at least one Big Five personality trait — more than 800 of them new. Associations held up across countries, age groups and different personality instruments. Together the variants explain only ~5–13% of trait variance.

**Biological background.** Twin studies have long put personality heritability near 40–50%, but individual genes had stubbornly refused to show up. This confirms why: personality is extraordinarily polygenic, thousands of variants each contributing a sliver — the same architecture found for height and educational attainment.

**Why it matters.** The value is mechanistic, not predictive. These loci give a handle on how biology links temperament to health outcomes like depression risk and substance use. The authors explicitly warn against using the findings for employment, education, insurance or healthcare screening — and the 5–13% variance explained is the reason that warning has teeth.

Sources: [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02761-5) · [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/huge-genetic-study-finds-strong-links-between-dna-and-personality-traits-290861) · [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/part-of-your-ersonality-is-encoded-in-your-genes-huge-analysis-links-characteristics-to-more-than-1200-common-genetic-variants-180989450/)

### 2026 Lasker Awards announced (Sep 8)

*Stage: Recognition / basic + clinical*

The Basic Medical Research award went to Emmanuel Mignot (Stanford) and Masashi Yanagisawa (Tsukuba) for identifying orexin/hypocretin as the brain peptide that maintains wakefulness, and for showing that loss of orexin signaling causes type 1 narcolepsy. The Clinical Research award went to Kunihiro Hattori, Tomoyuki Igawa and Takehisa Kitazawa for the bispecific antibody treatment of hemophilia A (emicizumab). Michael J. Fox received the public service award for Parkinson's disease research advocacy.

**The diseases.** *Type 1 narcolepsy* is a lifelong neurological disorder in which the boundary between sleep and waking breaks down: sudden daytime sleep attacks, and cataplexy — abrupt loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, so a laugh can buckle the knees. The cause is destruction of the small population of brain cells that make orexin, which is what Mignot and Yanagisawa's work established. *Hemophilia A* is an inherited bleeding disorder, almost always in males, caused by a missing or defective clotting factor VIII; without it the clotting cascade stalls and patients bleed into joints and muscles. *Parkinson's disease* is a progressive disorder caused by loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the midbrain, producing tremor, rigidity and slowed movement.

**Why it matters.** Both scientific awards are unusually clean examples of mechanism → medicine. Orexin biology produced an entire class of approved insomnia drugs (the orexin receptor antagonists) and, running the other direction, orexin-2 receptor *agonists* for narcolepsy are now in late-stage development. Emicizumab did something conceptually elegant: rather than replacing missing factor VIII, the bispecific antibody physically bridges factor IXa and factor X, substituting for factor VIII's function entirely.

Sources: [Lasker Foundation](https://laskerfoundation.org/winners/2026-winners/) · [STAT, Sep 9](https://www.statnews.com/2026/09/09/biotech-news-orexin-discovers-awawrded-prestigious-lasker-award/) · [Drug Discovery News](https://www.drugdiscoverynews.com/2026-lasker-awards-honor-advances-in-narcolepsy-hemophilia-parkinson-s-disease-17510)

## 5. Deals and business

### Lilly to acquire Merida Biosciences for up to $2.875B (Aug 31)

*Stage: Preclinical/early clinical asset · market-relevant*

Lilly is buying Merida, a private biotech whose platform builds biologics that selectively degrade *pathogenic autoantibodies*. Lead candidate MER511 targets Graves' disease and thyroid eye disease. Deal includes upfront plus contingent milestones; expected to close Q4 2026.

**Biological background.** *Graves' disease* is the most common cause of an overactive thyroid: patients get weight loss, racing heartbeat, tremor, heat intolerance and anxiety. In roughly a quarter of them it also inflames the tissue behind the eyes — *thyroid eye disease* — pushing the eyeballs forward and, at worst, threatening vision.

Both are *autoantibody* diseases. Antibodies normally tag foreign invaders; autoantibodies are ones that have turned on the body's own tissue. In Graves' the culprits bind the TSH receptor on the thyroid and jam it permanently on, so the gland ignores the body's off switch and overproduces hormone. Broad immunosuppression or pan-IgG depletion (the FcRn-blocker approach) removes the bad antibodies but also the good ones. Merida's pitch is selectivity: strip out only the disease-causing antibody species.

**Why it matters.** Two things. First, it's a genuinely differentiated mechanism in a crowded autoimmune space. Second, it's Lilly explicitly diversifying beyond obesity — a signal worth watching given how much of the company's valuation currently rests on incretins.

Sources: [Fierce Biotech](https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/eli-lilly-inks-29b-merida-buyout-autoimmune-drugs-against-rogue-antibodies) · [BioPharma Dive](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/lilly-merida-acquire-deal-immune-drugs-graves-disease/829152/) · [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/31/eli-lilly-to-buy-merida-biosciences.html)

### Roche licenses Simcere Zaiming's trispecific T-cell engager for up to $1.53B (Sep 1)

*Stage: Early clinical · market-relevant*

Roche takes exclusive global rights to SIM0660, a first-in-class trispecific antibody hitting CD3, CD79a and CD19, for $75M upfront, up to $1.53B total, plus tiered royalties to low double digits.

**Biological background.** Conventional CD19-directed bispecifics fail when tumors shed CD19 — a well-documented escape route after CAR-T and blinatumomab. Adding a second B-cell antigen, CD79a (part of the B-cell receptor signaling complex), gives the drug a backup grip. The same logic extends to autoimmune disease, where deep B-cell depletion is increasingly the goal.

**Why it matters.** It's the sixth out-licensing deal by Simcere, whose cumulative deal value now exceeds $6.1B — one more data point in the sustained flow of Chinese-originated assets into Western pharma pipelines, which is now a structural feature of biopharma business development rather than a novelty.

## Top takeaways

1. **The residual-risk cardiovascular thesis had a catastrophic fortnight.** Pelacarsen and ziltivekimab failed within days of each other, taking down both the Lp(a)-lowering and the IL-6-inflammation hypotheses in the same news cycle. Amgen's olpasiran and Lilly's lepodisiran now carry substantially more risk, and the entire category's cost of capital just went up. Note the nuance: biomarker modification was achieved in both cases. It's the translation to hard endpoints that broke.

2. **Engineering, not new targets, is where the wins came from.** Inhibrx's hexavalent OX40 agonist succeeded where conventional bivalent antibodies failed; Roche paid $1.53B for a trispecific because bispecifics lose to antigen escape; AbbVie's etentamig is differentiated on dosing schedule and CRS profile rather than potency. The molecular format is now the innovation.

3. **Antisense is having a quiet, good year in the CNS.** Zanvastro's Alexander disease approval — ahead of its PDUFA date, on functional rather than biomarker endpoints — shows that knocking down a toxic gain-of-function protein in the brain produces real clinical benefit. Awkwardly, it came from the same company whose cardiovascular antisense programs failed twice this year.

4. **GLP-1 biology keeps escaping its original indication.** The semaglutide mouse longevity result suggests these drugs engage conserved nutrient-sensing pathways rather than simply removing fat mass. Preclinical, female-only, modest effect size — but it reframes what the class might eventually be for.

5. **Capital is concentrating while headcount contracts.** Multi-billion-dollar deals (Lilly/Merida, Roche/Simcere) are being struck in the same weeks that ~18,800 industry jobs have been cut this year. Money is flowing to de-risked or mechanistically differentiated late-stage assets; early discovery is where the cuts land.
