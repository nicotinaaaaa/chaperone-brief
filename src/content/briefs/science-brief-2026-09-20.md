## 1. Regulatory decisions and approvals

### FDA approves Scholar Rock's Isembyld, the first muscle-targeted drug for spinal muscular atrophy

**Stage: regulatory / market-relevant. Date: 11 September 2026.**

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA, the American agency that decides whether a medicine may be sold) approved **Isembyld** (generic name *apitegromab-mstn*) from Cambridge, Massachusetts-based Scholar Rock. It is cleared for people aged two and older who have spinal muscular atrophy and are already taking an existing SMN2-targeted drug. The list price is about **$310,000 per year**, varying with a patient's body weight.

**What the disease is.** *Spinal muscular atrophy* (SMA) is an inherited disease in which the motor neurons — the nerve cells in the spinal cord that send "contract now" signals to muscles — progressively die. Without those signals the muscles waste away. Children with the severe infant-onset form may never sit or walk and historically died young; milder forms cause slowly worsening weakness through life. It is caused by a broken *SMN1* gene, which normally makes a protein every cell needs to survive.

**What "SMN2-targeted treatment" means.** Humans carry a backup gene, *SMN2*, that makes a small amount of the same protein. Existing SMA drugs (Biogen's Spinraza, Roche's Evrysdi, Novartis's Zolgensma) all work upstream, by boosting that protein so the motor neurons stop dying. They rescue the nerve, but they do not rebuild muscle that has already wasted.

**What is new here.** Isembyld attacks the problem from the muscle end instead. It is a *monoclonal antibody* — a lab-made copy of an immune protein, designed to stick to one specific target — and its target is **myostatin**, a natural brake the body uses to stop muscles growing too large. Blocking myostatin releases the brake. In trials, patients already on an SMN2 drug who added Isembyld gained **2.2 points** on the Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale-Expanded (a 0–66 physiotherapist-scored checklist of movements like rolling over, sitting, standing and walking) compared with placebo.

**Why it matters.** This is the first SMA therapy that works on muscle rather than nerve, meaning it can be layered on top of existing drugs rather than replacing them — an "add-on" market rather than a competitive one. Several analysts had already modeled it as a future blockbuster (industry shorthand for a drug exceeding $1 billion in annual sales).

### FDA approves Ultragenyx's Fayuvi, the first-ever treatment for Sanfilippo syndrome type A — at $3.95 million

**Stage: regulatory / market-relevant. Date: ~17 September 2026.**

Ultragenyx Pharmaceutical (Nasdaq: RARE) won approval for **Fayuvi**, a one-time *gene therapy* for Sanfilippo syndrome type A in patients whose neurological development is still preserved. The list price of **$3.95 million** makes it one of the most expensive medicines ever launched, second only to Orchard's Lenmeldy at $4.25 million.

**What the disease is.** *Sanfilippo syndrome type A* (also called mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIA, or MPS IIIA) is an ultra-rare inherited childhood disease. Children are born with a broken *SGSH* gene, so they cannot make an enzyme called sulfamidase whose job is to chop up a large sugar molecule, **heparan sulfate**, once the body is finished with it. Undigested heparan sulfate piles up inside cells, especially brain cells. Affected children typically develop normally for two to four years, then regress — losing speech, becoming hyperactive and sleepless, then losing the ability to walk and swallow. Most die in their teens. Until now there was no treatment at all.

**What a gene therapy is, and what this one does.** A gene therapy delivers a working copy of a faulty gene into the patient's own cells, so the body makes the missing protein itself. Fayuvi packages a healthy *SGSH* gene inside **AAV9** — adeno-associated virus serotype 9, a harmless virus shell used as a delivery van, chosen because it is unusually good at crossing into the brain. Once inside, the cells begin producing sulfamidase and clearing the heparan sulfate backlog.

**Why it matters, scientifically and commercially.** Scientifically, it is proof that a single intravenous gene therapy can address a *neuronopathic* storage disease (one whose damage is primarily in the brain) — historically the hardest category, because most drugs cannot cross the blood–brain barrier. Commercially, Ultragenyx also received a **Priority Review Voucher** — a transferable coupon the FDA grants for rare pediatric disease approvals that lets the holder (or a buyer) jump the queue on a future application. These sell for roughly $150–200 million on the open market, so the voucher is a meaningful cash event in itself. This is Ultragenyx's second gene therapy launch since August.

### European Commission clears J&J's Tecvayli in combination for multiple myeloma

**Stage: regulatory. Date: early September 2026.**

The European Commission (the EU's executive body, which formally grants EU-wide drug approvals on the European Medicines Agency's advice) approved Johnson & Johnson's **Tecvayli** (teclistamab) *in combination with* daratumumab for multiple myeloma that has come back or stopped responding after at least one prior treatment. Tecvayli was already approved alone in Europe since 2022, but only after three prior treatment lines; this decision moves it much earlier in the treatment sequence. The FDA approved the same combination in March 2026.

**What the disease is.** *Multiple myeloma* is a cancer of plasma cells — the white blood cells that normally manufacture antibodies. Malignant plasma cells crowd out the bone marrow, causing anemia, bone destruction and fractures, kidney failure and high blood calcium. It is generally treatable but not curable; patients cycle through successive regimens as each stops working.

**What the drugs are.** Tecvayli is a *bispecific T-cell engager* — an antibody with two grabbing ends. One end latches onto **CD3**, a marker on the surface of T cells (the immune system's assassin cells); the other latches onto **BCMA** (B-cell maturation antigen), a protein found almost exclusively on plasma cells. By holding the two together, the drug physically forces a T cell into contact with a myeloma cell and triggers the kill. Daratumumab is a conventional monoclonal antibody against **CD38**, another protein abundant on myeloma cells; it flags them for destruction by the immune system. Tecvayli is given on a *step-up dosing schedule* — deliberately small first doses that ramp up — to reduce **cytokine release syndrome**, a dangerous immune overreaction (fever, low blood pressure, organ stress) that happens when too many T cells activate at once.

### FDA launches "Operation Trialblazer" to speed early-stage US drug research

**Stage: regulatory / policy. Date: 15 September 2026.**

The FDA formally announced a pilot program, first teased in June, that pairs drugmakers with research institutions to move candidate medicines into human testing faster. The mechanism is the **IND** — the Investigational New Drug application, the filing a company must clear before it is legally allowed to give an experimental drug to a person.

**Why it matters.** The explicit framing is competition with China, where trial start-up is considerably faster and cheaper. A large share of the licensing deals in this brief involve Western pharma buying Chinese-originated molecules; the FDA is signalling it wants more of that early work to happen domestically. Separately, the FDA named permanent directors for its two main drug centers — **CBER** (biologics: vaccines, cell and gene therapies) and **CDER** (conventional drugs) — after nearly two years of leadership churn, which analysts read as a stabilizing signal.

### Context: Revolution Medicines' Rasonque, the first RAS inhibitor approved for pancreatic cancer

**Stage: regulatory. Date: late August 2026 (widely analyzed through September).**

Slightly before this window but central to September's commentary: the FDA approved **Rasonque** (daraxonrasib) from Revolution Medicines for metastatic pancreatic cancer.

**What the disease is.** *Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma* (PDAC) is the most common form of pancreatic cancer. It usually produces no symptoms until it has spread, so most patients are diagnosed late; five-year survival remains in the low teens, the worst of any major cancer.

**What RAS is.** RAS is a family of proteins that act as on/off switches relaying "grow and divide" instructions from a cell's surface to its nucleus. In healthy cells the switch flips off within seconds. A mutated RAS is jammed permanently on, so the cell divides endlessly. More than 90% of pancreatic cancers carry a RAS mutation, which has made RAS the single most wanted drug target in oncology for forty years — and, because the protein's surface is smooth with no obvious pocket to bind, long considered "undruggable."

**The result.** In a Phase 3 trial of about 500 patients, Rasonque roughly **doubled median overall survival from 6.6 to 13.2 months** versus chemotherapy, and doubled progression-free survival. (*Overall survival* = time until death from any cause; *progression-free survival* = time until the tumor grows measurably or the patient dies. Overall survival is the more trustworthy of the two because it cannot be gamed by how often you scan.) This is the first broad RAS-targeted medicine approved in pancreatic cancer.

## 2. Clinical trial results

### Summit/Akeso's ivonescimab beats Merck's Keytruda on survival in lung cancer — the headline readout of WCLC 2026

**Stage: clinical (Phase 3) / market-moving. Date: presented 15 September 2026, World Conference on Lung Cancer.**

In the **HARMONi-2** trial run by Akeso in China, **ivonescimab** alone produced a median overall survival of **30.8 months** versus **22.6 months** for pembrolizumab (Keytruda) alone, in previously untreated patients with PD-L1-positive advanced non-small cell lung cancer. Data cutoff was 20 August 2026, median follow-up 36 months, 234 deaths recorded. The trial had already met its main goal on progression-free survival in 2024 (11.1 vs 5.8 months). Summit Therapeutics holds Western rights.

**What the disease is.** *Non-small cell lung cancer* (NSCLC) accounts for roughly 85% of lung cancers. "Advanced" or "metastatic" means it has spread beyond the lung and cannot be cured by surgery; treatment aims to extend life. *PD-L1-positive* means the tumor cells display a protein called PD-L1 on their surface, which is the tumor's way of telling approaching T cells to stand down — and which makes the tumor a good candidate for immunotherapy.

**What the drugs are.** Keytruda is a *checkpoint inhibitor*: an antibody that blocks the PD-1 receptor on T cells so the tumor's "stand down" signal cannot be received, releasing the immune attack. It is the single best-selling drug in the world. **Ivonescimab** is a *bispecific antibody* that does two things at once: it blocks PD-1 like Keytruda, **and** it mops up **VEGF** (vascular endothelial growth factor), the signal tumors use to grow their own blood supply. The theory is that starving the tumor of new vessels and un-blinding the immune system simultaneously is better than either alone.

**Why it matters.** This is the first time any drug has beaten Keytruda on overall survival in a head-to-head first-line lung cancer trial. It validates the PD-1/VEGF bispecific class, which dozens of companies have piled into. Two large caveats dominate the commentary: the trial was run entirely in China, and there is an active scientific debate at WCLC about whether results from China-only studies transfer to Western populations (differences in patient genetics, smoking history, and what "standard of care" means locally). Summit also presented updated **HARMONi** data showing consistent survival results across Western and Asian patients, specifically to address that objection. Wall Street reacted positively.

### Skyhawk's oral Huntington's drug holds up at 15 months

**Stage: clinical (Phase 1/2, moving into pivotal). Date: 15 September 2026.**

Fidelity-backed Skyhawk Therapeutics reported final 15-month results for **SKY-0515**. Treated patients *improved* by an average of 0.94 points on the cUHDRS, while a matched untreated comparison group drawn from the Enroll-HD natural history database *declined* by 0.65 — a between-group difference of **1.59 points** (p<0.001).

**What the disease is.** *Huntington's disease* is an inherited, uniformly fatal brain disease. A single gene, *HTT*, contains a stutter — a repeated three-letter DNA sequence, CAG. Everyone has some repeats; above roughly 40, the resulting protein (mutant huntingtin) misfolds and slowly poisons neurons, especially in the striatum, the brain region governing movement and motivation. Symptoms usually begin between 30 and 50: involuntary writhing movements, loss of coordination, personality change, depression and dementia, progressing to death over 10–20 years. Because it is dominantly inherited, each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance. There is no disease-modifying treatment.

**What the drug is and what the numbers mean.** SKY-0515 is a *small molecule* (a conventional swallowable pill, as opposed to an injected biologic) that acts as an **RNA splicing modifier**. Splicing is the cell's editing step: a freshly copied gene transcript contains unwanted stretches that get cut out before the protein is built. SKY-0515 nudges that editing so the mutant huntingtin message gets miscut and discarded, cutting mutant protein production by **over 60%** at the 9 mg dose. The *cUHDRS* (composite Unified Huntington's Disease Rating Scale) bundles motor, cognitive and functional measures into one score that normally falls about 1 point a year. A 1.59-point advantage is therefore roughly 18 months of held-back decline — genuinely striking for an early trial.

**Caveats worth stating plainly.** The comparison group was not randomized; it came from a historical database. That is a real weakness, because patients who enroll in a drug trial may differ systematically from those in a registry. The definitive test is the ongoing worldwide Phase 2/3 **FALCON-HD-004-WW** study, now running at more than twenty sites in over ten countries.

### AbbVie's etentamig posts strong Phase 3 myeloma numbers

**Stage: clinical (Phase 3). Date: 3 September 2026.**

AbbVie announced topline results from the Phase 3 **CERVINO** trial of **etentamig** in relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (defined above — cancer of the antibody-producing plasma cells; "relapsed" means it came back, "refractory" means it never responded). Reported figures: a **74% overall response rate**; a **60% reduction** in the risk of the disease progressing or the patient dying versus standard therapies; and roughly **90% of patients alive at one year** versus 72% on standard of care. One patient had a neurotoxicity event, with no grade 2-or-higher events reported.

**Terms.** *Overall response rate* (ORR) is the share of patients whose tumor burden shrank by a predefined amount — an early, easy-to-measure signal that says nothing on its own about how long people live. A "60% reduction in risk" is a *hazard ratio* of 0.40: at any given moment, a treated patient was 40% as likely as a control patient to progress or die. Like Tecvayli above, etentamig is a **BCMA×CD3 bispecific T-cell engager** — it clamps a T cell onto a myeloma cell. AbbVie's specific engineering choice is to make the CD3 grip *deliberately weaker*, on the theory that a gentler T-cell activation still kills the cancer while causing less cytokine release syndrome. The monthly dosing schedule is a practical advantage over weekly rivals. Full data go to the International Myeloma Society meeting in Glasgow.

### Transgene's personalized cancer vaccine: 100% three-year survival in a small head-and-neck study

**Stage: clinical (Phase 1/2, very small). Date: 3 September 2026.**

French biotech Transgene reported that **every** patient in a Phase 1 study of **TG4050** was alive and free of recurrence at three years. All were HPV-negative and had already had surgery.

**What the disease is.** *Head and neck cancer* arises in the mouth, throat, larynx or sinuses. It splits into two very different diseases: HPV-positive cases (caused by human papillomavirus, generally in younger patients, and relatively responsive to treatment) and **HPV-negative** cases, typically driven by tobacco and alcohol, which carry far more scattered mutations and a markedly worse prognosis — recurrence after surgery is common and usually hard to salvage. This study was in the harder group.

**What a neoantigen vaccine is.** As a tumor mutates it produces proteins that exist nowhere else in the body — **neoantigens**, unique to that one patient's cancer. A personalized cancer vaccine sequences the tumor, picks the most promising neoantigens, and trains the immune system to hunt them. TG4050 uses a modified **Ankara virus** (a safe, non-replicating vaccine virus used for smallpox vaccination) engineered to carry instructions for up to 30 neoantigens; NEC's artificial intelligence selects which 30. Injected, the virus enters cells, which manufacture the neoantigens and display them to the immune system.

**How much to believe.** Single-arm (no control group), Phase 1, and small. Patients who have had successful surgery for HPV-negative head and neck cancer do not all recur, so "100%" in a handful of patients is encouraging rather than conclusive. Still, no personalized neoantigen vaccine has yet been approved anywhere, and multi-year recurrence-free data of this kind is exactly what the field needs.

### AstraZeneca/Daiichi's Enhertu: better response, shorter survival, in frontline lung cancer

**Stage: clinical (Phase 3), mixed result. Date: September 2026.**

In **DESTINY-Lung04**, Enhertu was tested as first-line treatment for HER2-positive NSCLC against the standard of Keytruda plus chemotherapy. Enhertu cut the risk of progression or death by **37%**, with a **70% response rate versus 44.5%**, and responses lasting 13.4 versus 9.7 months. But **patients on Keytruda-plus-chemo lived longer**: median overall survival 33.1 months versus 29.3.

**What the drug is.** Enhertu is an *antibody–drug conjugate* (ADC) — a three-part molecule: an antibody that finds a target on cancer cells, a chemotherapy payload, and a chemical **linker** joining them. The idea is a guided missile: the antibody delivers a toxic dose directly to tumor cells, sparing healthy tissue. Enhertu's antibody targets **HER2**, a growth-signal receptor over-produced in about 20% of breast cancers and a smaller slice of lung cancers; its payload is a **topoisomerase I inhibitor**. Topoisomerase is the enzyme that nicks and unwinds tangled DNA so a dividing cell can copy it; blocking it leaves the DNA cut and unrepaired, killing the cell mid-division.

**Why the split result matters.** This is the cleanest illustration in the window of why response rate and progression-free survival can mislead. Enhertu shrank more tumors, faster, for longer — and patients still died sooner. The likely explanation involves toxicity and what treatments patients could receive afterward. It is a meaningful obstacle to FDA approval in this setting, and a caution applicable to every "impressive ORR" headline elsewhere in this brief.

### Hutchmed: two tyrosine kinase inhibitors beat one in Chinese lung cancer trial

**Stage: clinical (Phase 3). Date: September 2026.**

A Phase 3 trial in China found that **Orpathys** (savolitinib) plus AstraZeneca's **Tagrisso** outperformed Tagrisso alone in previously untreated NSCLC patients, with what Hutchmed called "very encouraging clinical benefit in overall survival" in both the MET-overexpressing group and the overall randomized population.

**What a tyrosine kinase inhibitor is.** *Tyrosine kinases* are enzymes that act as relay switches in the signalling chains telling cells to grow and survive. In many cancers one of these switches is stuck on. A *tyrosine kinase inhibitor* (TKI) is a pill that jams the switch. Tagrisso blocks **EGFR** (epidermal growth factor receptor); savolitinib blocks **c-MET**, a different receptor that tumors frequently switch on as an escape route when EGFR is blocked. Hitting both closes the escape hatch. The two companies will now pursue approval for the combination.

## 3. Safety setbacks and failures

### Xenon pauses Phase 3 depression trials after psychiatric side effects; shares drop sharply

**Stage: clinical / market-moving. Date: 17 September 2026.**

Xenon Pharmaceuticals (Nasdaq: XENE) delivered good and bad news in one release. Good: it **submitted its New Drug Application** to the FDA for **azetukalner** in focal onset seizures, on schedule. Bad: it **voluntarily paused new enrollment** in its Phase 3 major depressive disorder and bipolar depression studies after reviewing neuropsychiatric adverse events — confusion, speech difficulty, impaired motor coordination, and a "very small number" of cases described under the broad heading of **psychosis** (a state of losing contact with reality: hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking). Patients already enrolled continue. The pause was made in consultation with the trial's **Data Safety Monitoring Board** — an independent panel of physicians and statisticians that reviews unblinded safety data during a trial and can recommend changes or stoppage.

**Market reaction: shares fell roughly 22–30%** depending on the session and source, with after-hours quotes in the mid-to-high twenties percent.

**What the diseases are.** *Focal onset seizures* are epileptic seizures that start in one localized patch of the brain; depending on the region, they can cause anything from a strange smell or déjà vu to a limb jerking or loss of awareness, and may spread to the whole brain. Roughly a third of epilepsy patients do not achieve control on available drugs. *Major depressive disorder* (MDD) is clinical depression: at least two weeks of persistently low mood or loss of interest, plus changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and self-worth, severe enough to impair functioning; it is common, recurrent, and a leading cause of disability worldwide. *Bipolar depression* is the depressive phase of bipolar disorder, in which depressive episodes alternate with manic or hypomanic ones — and it is harder to treat, because standard antidepressants can tip a patient into mania.

**What the drug is.** Azetukalner is a **KV7 potassium channel opener**. Neurons fire by letting charged ions cross their membranes. Opening potassium channels lets positive potassium ions flow out, making the inside of the neuron more negative — *hyperpolarized* — and therefore harder to fire. Dialling down over-excitable neurons is a well-validated way to stop seizures, and Xenon's bet was that the same mechanism would help depression. The company says the events seen are consistent with the drug's known profile and mechanism, but that they had **not** appeared in the earlier Phase 2 MDD study — which is precisely why this is worrying rather than routine. Xenon is evaluating dose modifications and expects the pause to be temporary. It held $1.2 billion in cash as of mid-2026, with runway into 2029, so this is a pipeline problem rather than a survival problem.

### AstraZeneca's newly approved breast cancer pill fails in first-line testing

**Stage: clinical (Phase 3), failure. Date: 11 September 2026.**

AstraZeneca's **Etcamah** missed in a Phase 3 trial as a first-line treatment for advanced breast cancer. It is the second oral drug of its class to fall short in this setting this year, after a similar Roche setback.

**What the class is.** A **SERD** (selective estrogen receptor degrader) treats hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer — the most common subtype, in which tumor cells carry estrogen receptors and use estrogen as fuel. Rather than merely blocking the receptor (what tamoxifen does), a SERD marks it for destruction, removing it from the cell entirely. The first SERD, fulvestrant, requires painful intramuscular injections; the whole commercial premise of oral SERDs is convenience plus activity against tumors that have mutated their estrogen receptor to resist other drugs. The pattern of these pills working in *later* lines but failing in the *first* line suggests the class's advantage shows up mainly after resistance has already developed.

### Cellectis abandons CAR-T entirely

**Stage: corporate strategy / clinical. Date: September 2026.**

French biotech Cellectis, long a leader in donor-derived cell therapy, is quitting **CAR-T** and pivoting to **in vivo gene editing**, offloading its lead programs.

**What CAR-T is.** *Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy* removes a patient's T cells, genetically engineers them in a factory to carry a new receptor recognizing a cancer marker, multiplies them, and infuses them back. It produces remarkable remissions in some blood cancers but is enormously expensive, takes weeks, and requires the patient to undergo chemotherapy first. Cellectis's specialty was *allogeneic* CAR-T — "off the shelf" cells made from healthy donors rather than the patient, to remove the wait and the cost. **In vivo gene editing** skips cell manufacturing entirely: the editing machinery is injected into the patient and does its work inside the body. Cellectis's move is a sharp read on where the field's economics are heading, and it rhymes with several preclinical results below.

## 4. Deals, money and corporate strategy

### GSK buys a Chinese trispecific T-cell engager for up to $750 million

**Stage: preclinical asset / market. Date: 15 September 2026.**

GSK agreed to acquire full global rights to a **preclinical trispecific T-cell engager** for multiple myeloma from China-based Chimagen Biosciences. Undisclosed upfront payment; total potential value up to $750 million. Phase 1 trials expected in 2027.

**What "trispecific" adds.** The bispecifics described earlier grab a T cell with one arm and one tumor marker with the other. A **trispecific** has three arms: one for the T cell, and **two different tumor-associated antigens**. Requiring two tumor markers rather than one makes the drug more selective — healthy cells that happen to carry one of the markers are less likely to be attacked — which is the stated route to better tolerability. T-cell engagers work extremely well in myeloma but are notorious for cytokine release syndrome and infections.

**Why it matters.** This is **GSK's fifth licensing deal with a China-based drugmaker since the start of 2025**, and one of several in this single window. The structural story of 2026 biotech is that Chinese biotechs now originate a large and growing share of the molecules Western pharma buys.

### Genentech and DualityBio: a billion-dollar ADC platform deal

**Stage: preclinical / market. Date: September 2026.**

Roche's Genentech will use Shanghai-based DualityBio's **DUPAC** platform to build next-generation antibody–drug conjugates. Terms: **$45 million upfront**, over **$1 billion** in aggregate milestones across programs.

**The scientific point.** Almost every successful ADC on the market today (Enhertu included) uses a **topoisomerase inhibitor** payload. That creates a shared failure mode: once a tumor becomes resistant to one such ADC, the others may not work either. DUPAC is explicitly built around *different* payload chemistry, aimed at patients who do not respond to topoisomerase-based ADCs. DualityBio's lead asset, DB-1303, targets HER2 and is in Phase 3 with BioNTech.

### Hutchmed licenses an unusual ADC to GSK for up to $1.295 billion

**Stage: preclinical / market. Date: September 2026.**

GSK paid **$110 million upfront** for rights outside greater China to Hutchmed's **HMPL-A830**, with up to $1.295 billion in milestones. Phase 1 starts later this year.

**Why it is unusual.** Conventional ADCs carry a cell-killing chemotherapy payload. HMPL-A830 instead carries a **KRAS inhibitor** — a targeted drug, not a poison — attached to an antibody that homes in on **EGFR** (the growth-signal receptor on many tumor cells). So the antibody concentrates a precision drug inside tumors rather than concentrating a toxin. KRAS is the most commonly mutated member of the RAS family described earlier: mutated in most pancreatic cancers, 44% of colorectal cancers and 34% of lung cancers.

### Novartis pays $125 million for a blood–brain barrier crossing platform

**Stage: platform / market. Date: ~16 September 2026.**

Novartis acquired global rights to a delivery technology from **Sironax**, funding three candidates the startup calls potentially best-in-class.

**Why it matters.** The **blood–brain barrier** is a tight seal of specialized cells lining the brain's blood vessels, evolved to keep toxins out. It also keeps out nearly every large drug molecule — the central reason neurology has lagged oncology. Technologies that ferry biologics across it are among the most contested assets in pharma; this deal, Evox's vesicle work below, and the UCL kidney-targeting work below are all attacking the same general problem of getting a therapy to the organ you actually want.

### Novo signs two significant deals: Orbis (oral peptides) and Anthropic (AI)

**Stage: platform / market. Dates: 16 September 2026.**

**Novo and Orbis** entered a potential **$1.4 billion** alliance on **macrocyclic peptides** for cardiometabolic disease. *Peptides* are short protein chains — GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide are peptides — and their great weakness is that the digestive system destroys them, which is why most are injected. A **macrocycle** is a peptide chemically stitched into a closed ring, which makes it far more resistant to digestion and therefore a plausible route to obesity and diabetes drugs you can swallow. The entire obesity market's next competitive phase is oral.

**Novo and Anthropic** announced a collaboration in which Novo will use Anthropic's frontier models and test the **Claude Science** workbench in specific R&D workflows, and use the models to accelerate internal software development. Financial terms undisclosed. The deal sits alongside Novo's existing arrangements with OpenAI and Amazon. Note also that Novo **dropped "Nordisk" from its name** during this period.

### Anthropic sets up a physical biology lab

**Stage: platform / market. Date: 18 September 2026 (Reuters exclusive).**

Anthropic has quietly built a **wet lab** — a physical laboratory for bench experiments, as opposed to purely computational work — in the San Francisco Bay Area. Head of life sciences Eric Kauderer-Abrams told Reuters: "We believe that to do biology, the final test is still and will be for a while in real lab work." The company is testing whether Claude can direct robotic systems to run experiments with minimal staffing, while stating that human involvement remains a safety requirement. Stated ambition: rare diseases and targets previously considered **undruggable** (having no binding pocket or mechanism a drug can exploit). Which diseases, and how far along, is not disclosed.

**Why it matters.** AI-for-drug-discovery has to date been overwhelmingly computational. An AI company building its own experimental capability — rather than licensing predictions to pharma — is a different and more vertically integrated bet, and a notable data point about where AI labs think the value sits.

### Roche and Dualitas: computational search of 300,000 bispecific combinations

**Stage: discovery / market. Date: September 2026.**

A research deal worth up to **$1 billion** in which Dualitas will screen more than **300,000 possible bispecific antibody combinations** for immune diseases. The combinatorial problem is real: if you have hundreds of candidate targets, the number of possible two-target pairings explodes, and almost all of them are useless. Systematic screening is an attempt to industrialize a search that has mostly been done by intuition.

### Financing: the IPO window is open

**Stage: market.**

## 5. Basic science and preclinical discoveries

### CRISPR's long-lost ancestor found — in viruses

**Stage: basic science. Published in Science; covered September 2026. Doudna lab, UC Berkeley / Innovative Genomics Institute.**

Two papers describe **VIPR** (Viral Interference Programmable Repeat), an RNA-guided DNA-targeting system that appears to **predate CRISPR** and to have originated in viruses rather than bacteria.

**Background.** *CRISPR* is a bacterial immune system: bacteria store snippets of DNA from viruses that have attacked them, transcribe those snippets into guide RNAs, and use them to direct a cutting enzyme to matching viral DNA on the next infection. Repurposed as a laboratory tool, it became the dominant gene-editing technology. The standard origin story is that bacteria evolved it to defend themselves.

**What was found.** The team identified an RNA-binding protein, VIPR, in both bacterial and viral genomes, paired with a small non-coding guide RNA called **vrRNA**. Critically, VIPR does **not** pair with its DNA target continuously, base by base, as CRISPR guides do. Cryo-electron microscopy (a technique that flash-freezes molecules and images them with electrons, revealing atomic-scale structure) showed VIPR proteins assembling into a **helical filament** along the RNA and **skipping every third DNA base**, forming an unusual three-stranded **triplex** structure. The researchers propose VIPR arose as a weapon viruses used against *each other* while competing for control of a bacterial host, and that bacteria later stole it and turned it against viruses — eventually evolving it into CRISPR.

**Why it matters.** Scientifically, it rewrites CRISPR's evolutionary origin: the tool did not begin as a shield, it began as a sword between rival viruses. Practically, a gapped, non-contiguous recognition code is a genuinely different way of specifying a DNA target, which could expand the gene-editing toolkit — different targeting rules mean access to sequences current editors handle poorly.

### Your gut microbiome may be more contagious than assumed

**Stage: basic science. Published in Nature, 7 September 2026. University of Vienna-led.**

Researchers found that distinct populations within common gut bacterial species can **spread between people and across continents**, with some lineages appearing to expand globally within a few decades. Of 77 identified "sweep clusters" (groups of closely related bacteria that have recently expanded), **54 contained samples from more than one continent**.

**Background and method.** The *gut microbiome* is the community of trillions of bacteria living in the intestine; it helps digest food, trains the immune system, and has been linked to conditions from obesity to depression. The prevailing assumption has been that your microbiome is shaped mostly by your diet, environment and early-life exposures. The team used **reverse ecology** — inferring what environment an organism adapted to by reading its genome, rather than observing the environment directly — and found that a single named bacterial "species" often contains several genetically distinct populations adapted to different gut conditions. Some of these hidden populations were associated with aging, colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (chronic immune-driven inflammation of the intestine — Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis) and type 2 diabetes.

**Why it matters.** If microbiome composition is meaningfully **transmissible** between people rather than purely environmental, that changes how we think about microbiome-linked disease risk — and about whether microbiome-based therapies could take hold and spread, or be disrupted.

---

### Gene editing delivered to the primate brain without a virus

**Stage: preclinical (non-human primate), preprint. Evox Therapeutics with Karolinska Institutet, August 2026 bioRxiv preprint.**

Researchers used engineered **extracellular vesicles** to deliver CRISPR machinery into monkey brains. **Extracellular vesicles** (EVs) are tiny membrane bubbles that cells naturally use to shuttle cargo to one another — a non-viral delivery van. The team engineered both the CRISPR components and the vesicles, reporting a roughly **300-fold increase in editing potency** over their earlier system, then programmed the EVs to disrupt **MSH3**, a DNA-repair gene that paradoxically drives the expansion of the CAG repeats underlying Huntington's disease (the same stutter described in the Skyhawk item above). Injected directly into the caudate and putamen (the two structures making up the striatum) of two cynomolgus monkeys, editing was detected across targeted regions, reaching **60–75%** in some striatal samples four to seven weeks later.

**Caveats, stated plainly.** Not peer-reviewed. Two animals. Direct brain injection, not systemic dosing. No test of whether it slows disease. But it is early evidence that a non-viral system can edit the primate brain — and delivery, not editing, is the field's binding constraint.

### Editing blood stem cells inside the body with targeted nanoparticles

**Stage: preclinical (humanized mice). Published in Nature Biomedical Engineering. Chinese Academy of Sciences and collaborators.**

The team screened 15 **lipid nanoparticle** formulations — tiny fat bubbles that carry genetic cargo into cells, the same delivery technology used in mRNA COVID vaccines — and attached an antibody targeting **CD34**, a marker on hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (the bone-marrow cells that generate all blood cells). Injected into the femur of humanized mice, the particles edited an enhancer (a DNA switch controlling a gene) that regulates **BCL11A**, a gene whose disruption reactivates **fetal hemoglobin** — the oxygen-carrying protein used before birth, which can substitute for defective adult hemoglobin. Fetal hemoglobin stayed elevated over long-term follow-up. In a separate model, the platform edited **ELANE**, restoring some neutrophil development.

**What the diseases are.** *Sickle cell disease* is an inherited blood disorder in which abnormal hemoglobin makes red blood cells rigid and crescent-shaped; they jam in small vessels, causing episodes of excruciating pain, organ damage and strokes. *Beta-thalassemia* is a related inherited disorder in which too little hemoglobin is made, causing severe anemia often requiring lifelong transfusions. *Severe congenital neutropenia* is an inherited shortage of neutrophils — the white blood cells that are first responders to bacterial infection — leaving children dangerously vulnerable to infection.

**Why it matters, and the limit.** Approved CRISPR therapies for sickle cell (such as Casgevy) require removing the patient's stem cells, editing them in a factory, and giving harsh chemotherapy to clear the marrow before reinfusion — a process available at few centers and priced in the millions. Doing it inside the body would transform access. The catch is honest and significant: the particles were injected **directly into bone**, and when given systemically they still mostly ended up in the liver. Making bone-marrow targeting work without direct injection is the unsolved step.

---

### A protective coat for transplanted islets, inspired by the egg cell

**Stage: preclinical (mice). Published in Nature Biomedical Engineering. Penn State.**

Researchers built **BZP** (biomimetic zona pellucida), an ultrathin hydrogel coating about 20 micrometers thick that forms directly around clusters of insulin-producing cells. It is modelled on the *zona pellucida*, the natural protein shell surrounding a mammalian egg cell. The coating assembles through **aptamer**-guided recognition (aptamers are short DNA or RNA strands folded into shapes that bind specific molecules, like antibodies made of nucleic acid). Most diabetic mice receiving coated donor islets — **without any immunosuppressive drugs** — held normal blood glucose for over **100 days**.

**What the disease is.** *Type 1 diabetes* is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system destroys the beta cells in the pancreas that make insulin, the hormone that moves sugar from blood into cells. Without insulin, blood sugar rises to toxic levels. Patients depend on lifelong insulin injections and constant glucose monitoring; complications include blindness, kidney failure, nerve damage and cardiovascular disease.

**Why it matters.** Islet transplantation can restore natural insulin production, but the transplanted cells are attacked by the immune system, so recipients need lifelong immunosuppression — which brings infection and cancer risk and is why the procedure is reserved for the most severe cases. A physical barrier that admits nutrients and lets insulin out while keeping immune cells away would remove that trade-off. The team also reported 100% encapsulation efficiency under physiological conditions, without the droplet processing most encapsulation methods require.

### Targeted mRNA slows an untreatable childhood kidney disease in mice

**Stage: preclinical (mice). Published in Science Translational Medicine, August 2026. UCL Great Ormond Street Institute of Child Health.**

**What the disease is.** Mutations in the *WT1* gene cause severe childhood **glomerular disease**. The *glomeruli* are the kidney's microscopic filters, each a tuft of capillaries wrapped in specialized cells called **podocytes** whose interlocking foot processes form the actual sieve. When they are damaged, protein leaks massively into the urine (*albuminuria*), the filters scar, and kidney function progressively fails. There are no disease-modifying treatments.

**What was done.** The team built lipid nanocomplexes targeting **integrin αvβ3** (a surface protein on podocytes and glomerular blood-vessel cells) carrying mRNA for **angiopoietin-1**, a growth factor that maintains the filtration barrier and is depleted in this disease. Three doses over four weeks, delivered by ultrasound-guided injection into the renal artery of mutant mice, reduced protein loss, preserved podocytes and endothelial cells, and reduced scarring.

**The open question.** Whether repeated kidney-targeted delivery by arterial catheter can ever be practical for a chronic disease of small children.

---

### Recyclable protein degraders that work outside the cell

**Stage: preclinical (mice). Published in Nature Biotechnology. University of Wisconsin-Madison.**

**Background.** *Targeted protein degradation* is a drug strategy that destroys a disease-causing protein rather than merely blocking it — conceptually more thorough, since a blocked protein is still present. Most degraders work only on proteins *inside* cells. **LYTACs** (lysosome-targeting chimeras) extended this to proteins on the cell surface or outside it, but they depend on a specific shuttle receptor being present, and each degrader molecule is generally consumed after one use.

**What is new.** **McR-TACs** pair a target-binding ligand with a *polyzwitterion* (a polymer carrying both positive and negative charges) that triggers **macropinocytosis** — the cell's indiscriminate "big gulp" of surrounding fluid — pulling the target inside without needing any particular shuttle receptor. Once in, the degrader releases its cargo in acidic compartments and is then exported back out via the endoplasmic reticulum–Golgi pathway, ready to go again. The platform degraded **PD-L1** (the immune "stand down" signal from the lung cancer item above) and **MIF** (an inflammatory signalling protein), producing durable degradation and inhibiting tumor growth in mouse models of **triple-negative breast cancer** — the subtype lacking estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors and HER2, which means none of the targeted hormone or HER2 drugs work on it, leaving chemotherapy as the mainstay and giving it the worst prognosis of the common breast cancers.

### A single shot that silences a complement protein for months

**Stage: preclinical (monkeys). Published in Molecular Therapy, August 2026. CSPC Pharmaceutical Group.**

**What the disease is.** *IgA nephropathy* (IgAN, or Berger's disease) is the world's most common primary glomerular disease. Abnormal IgA antibodies clump together and deposit in the kidney's filters, triggering inflammation and scarring. It often first shows up as blood in the urine after a cold; a substantial minority of patients progress to kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant over 20–30 years. It typically strikes young adults.

**What was done.** CSPC built a **siRNA** — small interfering RNA, a short double-stranded RNA that binds a specific messenger RNA and destroys it before any protein is made, effectively switching off one gene — against **complement component 5 (C5)**. The *complement system* is a cascade of blood proteins that punches holes in pathogens and amplifies inflammation; in IgA nephropathy it is inappropriately activated in the kidney and drives much of the damage. The siRNA is conjugated to **GalNAc** (N-acetylgalactosamine), a sugar that liver cells actively grab, which routes the drug to the liver where most circulating complement proteins are manufactured.

**The result.** A single subcutaneous injection in cynomolgus monkeys suppressed C5 by **more than 80% for two months** and **more than 50% for up to four months**. Against Nefecon (an approved targeted-release budesonide formulation) in a monkey IgAN model, both improved kidney function, but the siRNA produced greater improvement in renal pathology and almost completely blocked complement activation in the glomeruli. C5 is already a clinically validated target in this disease; the differentiator here is duration.

### Also noted

**Stage: basic science.** A study in *Nature Neuroscience* reports that the chromatin protein **DAXX** helps preserve the identity and function of **microglia** — the brain's resident immune cells, which prune synapses and clear debris — in the adult brain. This connects to a broader 2026 thread: work published in *Nature* earlier this year showed that immune cells from bone marrow cross into the brain and replace some resident microglia during human aging, and a separate *Nature* paper identified lymph-node-like immune structures in skull bone marrow. The emerging picture is that the brain's immune compartment is far more open to, and shaped by, the rest of the body than the textbook "immune-privileged brain" model allowed.

## 7. Top takeaways

**1. Overall survival remains the only number that settles an argument.** This window produced the cleanest possible illustration: Enhertu shrank far more lung tumors than Keytruda-plus-chemo (70% vs 44.5% response rate) and delayed progression by 37% — and patients on it still died sooner. Meanwhile ivonescimab's claim to have beaten Keytruda rests on survival, which is why it lands so much harder. Whenever a press release leads with response rate or progression-free survival and omits survival, that omission is the story.

**2. Delivery, not editing, is now the binding constraint in genetic medicine.** Four separate items here are really the same item: Evox's vesicles reaching the monkey brain, CD34-targeted nanoparticles reaching blood stem cells, Novartis paying $125 million for a blood–brain-barrier crossing platform, and UCL's kidney-targeted mRNA. The scissors work. Getting them to the right organ, ideally with an ordinary injection, is the problem everyone is spending money on. Cellectis abandoning manufactured cell therapy for in-body editing is the strategic expression of the same judgment.

**3. China is now the source, not the competitor.** GSK signed its fifth China licensing deal since 2025 (Chimagen, up to $750M) and separately took Hutchmed's ADC (up to $1.295B); Genentech built a platform deal with Shanghai's DualityBio; Solstice launched on a Harbour BioMed molecule; ivonescimab is Akeso's. The FDA's "Operation Trialblazer" is an explicit institutional response to this. The open scientific question at WCLC — whether China-only trial results transfer to Western patients — is now a commercial question worth billions, and it is unresolved.

**4. Capital is abundant and brutally selective at the same time.** Electra raised $350 million in an IPO, six companies raised $700 million in a single week, and reverse mergers are so popular that dealmakers worry about a shortage of failed companies to merge into. In the same fortnight, Sionna laid off staff and Karyopharm negotiated with creditors for survival. Good data raises money instantly; everything else does not.

**5. Rare disease pricing has stopped being newsworthy, which is itself the news.** Fayuvi at $3.95 million and Isembyld at $310,000 a year drew analysis but little shock. The Priority Review Voucher Ultragenyx received — worth roughly $200 million on resale — is now a routine and material part of the business case for developing an ultra-rare disease therapy.

**6. AI companies are moving from predicting biology to doing it.** Anthropic building a physical wet lab, alongside its Novo collaboration, marks a shift from selling computational predictions to pharma toward owning the experimental loop. Whether an AI lab can run bench biology better than a pharma company can run AI is a genuinely open question, and the next two years should answer it.

**7. Evolution keeps handing biotechnology better tools than biotechnology designs.** The VIPR finding — that CRISPR descends from a weapon viruses used against each other, and that it recognizes DNA by a gapped code nobody had imagined — is a reminder that the most useful molecular machines so far have been found rather than invented. A different targeting grammar may open sequences current editors cannot reach.
