From placental pathophysiology to biomarker-guided care and placenta-targeted therapeutics

Review article · Reproductive and vascular biology

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Prepared 2 November 2026. All factual claims are referenced to the peer-reviewed literature retrieved from PubMed and to trial records from ClinicalTrials.gov; see References.*

## Abstract

Preeclampsia is a pregnancy-specific hypertensive syndrome that affects roughly 2–5% of pregnancies and remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal death. For most of the twentieth century it was described clinically — new-onset hypertension with proteinuria or maternal end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks of gestation — without a mechanistic account linking the placenta to the maternal illness. That changed when the placenta was shown to shed an excess of the antiangiogenic protein soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1), a truncated splice product of the VEGF receptor FLT1 that sequesters vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and placental growth factor (PlGF) in the maternal circulation. Administering sFlt-1 to pregnant rats reproduces hypertension, proteinuria and the glomerular endotheliosis lesion of the human disease, and in women the rise in sFlt-1 with the fall in free PlGF precedes clinical onset by weeks. A second placental antiangiogenic factor, soluble endoglin, blocks transforming growth factor-β co-receptor signalling and amplifies the phenotype toward HELLP syndrome and fetal growth restriction.

This review sets out the biology of the axis for a scientifically literate non-specialist reader: the anatomy of placentation and spiral artery remodelling; VEGF-family signalling in the maternal endothelium; the alternative splicing events at the FLT1 locus that generate soluble receptor; and the two-stage model in which heterogeneous placental insults converge on syncytiotrophoblast stress before producing the maternal syndrome. It then traces how that mechanism has become clinically actionable — the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio now rules out short-term preeclampsia with a negative predictive value approaching 99–100% and shortens hospitalisation in routine use — and surveys the therapeutic pipeline it has opened: low-dose aspirin prophylaxis, repurposed agents such as metformin, extracorporeal removal of sFlt-1 by apheresis, and fully chemically stabilised small interfering RNAs that silence the placental sFLT1 isoforms selectively and have now entered first-in-human testing. Open questions are examined, including the heterogeneity of late-onset disease, the limits of sFlt-1 as a single target, and the unresolved safety calculus of administering nucleic-acid therapeutics in pregnancy.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 A disease defined by its only cure

Preeclampsia complicates an estimated 2–4% of pregnancies worldwide and contributes substantially to maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality (Lee et al., 2025). It is conventionally diagnosed by new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation accompanied by proteinuria or, in the absence of proteinuria, by evidence of maternal end-organ dysfunction — thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary oedema, neurological features — or by uteroplacental dysfunction. The syndrome is conventionally split by gestational age at onset: early-onset preeclampsia (delivery before 34 weeks), which is most strongly associated with abnormal placentation and fetal growth restriction, and late-onset preeclampsia (≥34 weeks), in which maternal cardiovascular and metabolic predisposition weighs more heavily (Kariori et al., 2025).

What makes preeclampsia unusual among common diseases is that its only definitive treatment is removal of the organ that causes it. Delivery of the placenta reliably resolves the maternal syndrome, so clinical management is in effect a negotiation between maternal risk and fetal prematurity. At 26 weeks that negotiation is brutal: every day of expectant management carries risk of eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, abruption or stroke for the mother, and every day gained reduces neonatal mortality and the burden of prematurity for the fetus. A therapy that could reliably buy even one to two weeks in very preterm disease would change outcomes materially, which is why the mechanistic work described here has unusually direct clinical stakes (Rana et al., 2019).

### 1.2 Scope of this review

This review focuses on the antiangiogenic axis — sFlt-1, soluble endoglin and PlGF — because it is the best-validated causal pathway connecting the placenta to the maternal syndrome, and because it is the pathway from which diagnostics and candidate therapeutics have actually emerged. Sections 2 and 3 establish the developmental and molecular background and the two-stage framework. Section 4 assembles the evidence that the axis is causal rather than merely correlated. Sections 5 and 6 cover biomarker translation and the therapeutic pipeline. Sections 7 and 8 address long-term maternal consequences and unresolved questions.

## 2. Biological background

### 2.1 Placentation and spiral artery remodelling

The human placenta is a fetal organ that invades maternal tissue. After implantation, cytotrophoblast cells of the fetal trophectoderm lineage differentiate along two routes. In the villous route they fuse into the syncytiotrophoblast, a continuous multinucleated epithelium that covers the chorionic villi and forms the entire exchange surface bathed directly in maternal blood — the interface across which oxygen, nutrients, waste and placental secretory products move, and the cell layer whose stress state is central to the argument that follows. In the extravillous route, trophoblast cells migrate out of anchoring villi into the decidua and myometrium, where they remodel the maternal spiral arteries.

Spiral artery remodelling is the critical developmental event. Extravillous trophoblast invades the vessel wall, replaces endothelium and smooth muscle with fibrinoid material, and converts narrow, high-resistance, vasoreactive arterioles into wide, flaccid conduits that deliver blood to the intervillous space at low pressure and low velocity. Where remodelling is incomplete, the arteries retain contractile tone, perfusion becomes intermittent, and the villous tree is exposed to cycles of hypoxia and reoxygenation together with high-velocity jets of blood — a combination that generates oxidative, endoplasmic-reticulum and shear stress in the syncytiotrophoblast (Staff, 2019; Rana et al., 2019). Deficient remodelling is characteristic of early-onset disease and is frequently accompanied by fetal growth restriction.

Remodelling depends on an extracellular matrix that permits controlled invasion. Reduced expression of matrix metalloproteinases, altered fibronectin, increased collagen XVIII–derived endostatin fragments and increased degradation of heparan sulfate chains from proteoglycans have all been described in preeclamptic tissue, and degradation of the endothelial heparan sulfate glycocalyx is itself associated with higher circulating sFlt-1 — so matrix biology sits both upstream of defective invasion and downstream in the maternal endothelial injury (Famá and Pinhal, 2025).

### 2.2 VEGF-family signalling in the maternal endothelium

Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF) and placental growth factor (PlGF) are secreted glycoproteins that signal through two receptor tyrosine kinases. VEGFR-2 (KDR) transduces most of the proliferative and permeability signalling; VEGFR-1, encoded by FLT1, binds VEGF with higher affinity but signals weakly, and binds PlGF, which VEGFR-2 does not. In the quiescent adult vasculature this system is not primarily about growing new vessels. Tonic VEGF signalling maintains endothelial homeostasis: it sustains endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity and nitric oxide production, supports prostacyclin release, and is required for the health of specialised fenestrated endothelia — most conspicuously the glomerular capillary, where podocyte-derived VEGF maintains the fenestrated endothelial phenotype.

This explains why an antiangiogenic insult presents as hypertension and proteinuria rather than as a failure of vessel growth. Withdrawal of endothelial VEGF tone shifts the vessel wall toward vasoconstriction and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, raising systemic vascular resistance; in the kidney it produces glomerular endotheliosis, the swollen, occluded, de-fenestrated glomerular endothelium that is the pathognomonic renal lesion of preeclampsia. The same mechanism accounts for the hypertension and proteinuria seen as class effects of anti-VEGF cancer therapeutics — pharmacological evidence, from an entirely separate clinical context, that blocking this pathway in an adult produces the preeclamptic renal and vascular phenotype (Rana et al., 2019).

Importantly, the same molecules play different roles on the two sides of the placental barrier. Within the placenta, VEGF, PlGF, the angiopoietins and their soluble antagonists regulate villous vascular branching; in the maternal circulation, the soluble antagonists act systemically on maternal endothelium. Conflating the two compartments has been a persistent source of confusion in the literature, and careful treatments distinguish placental vascular development from maternal endothelial signalling (Umapathy et al., 2020).

### 2.3 The FLT1 locus and the manufacture of a decoy receptor

sFlt-1 is not a proteolytic shedding product but an alternatively spliced one. Transcription from FLT1 can terminate early within intron 13, yielding a message encoding the six extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains that carry the ligand-binding function, but lacking the transmembrane and tyrosine kinase domains. The resulting protein is secreted and acts as a soluble decoy: it binds VEGF and PlGF in the circulation with high affinity and prevents their engagement of membrane receptors. The human placenta expresses a family of such variants rather than a single species. Bioinformatic and mass-spectrometric analysis identified four sFlt-1 splice variants differing at their 3′ termini — including variants using a novel exon 15 and an extension of exon 14 — of which at least three are expressed as protein and are globally upregulated in preeclampsia, and three contribute significantly to total sFlt-1 output by cultured cytotrophoblasts (Heydarian et al., 2009).

This isoform structure is therapeutically decisive. Full-length membrane FLT1 has physiological functions in angiogenesis and in monocyte biology, so a therapy that suppressed the whole gene would be unattractive. The placental soluble isoforms, by contrast, share sequence that can be targeted selectively — the design principle behind the RNA interference approach discussed in Section 6.4 (Turanov et al., 2018).

## 3. The two-stage model: many upstream insults, one bottleneck

The modern framework for preeclampsia separates a placental Stage 1 from a maternal Stage 2. In its original formulation, Stage 1 was poor placentation and Stage 2 the maternal syndrome, with late-onset disease relegated to a largely 'maternal' category. The revised model argues instead that all preeclampsia requires a placenta, and that both early- and late-onset disease converge on a common Stage 1 endpoint: syncytiotrophoblast stress (Staff, 2019).

The routes to that endpoint differ. In early-onset disease, failed spiral artery remodelling produces uteroplacental malperfusion. In late-onset disease, remodelling may be adequate but the placenta reaches the limits of its perfusion capacity — a mechanism consistent with what is seen in post-mature and multiple pregnancies, where intervillous malperfusion arises from mechanical and metabolic constraint rather than from defective invasion. Maternal predispositions (obesity, chronic hypertension, pre-gestational diabetes, autoimmune disease) can act on either pathway and additionally prime the maternal cardiovascular system to respond badly to the placental factors it receives.

Single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis of delivered placentas supports this partition at molecular resolution. In a study spanning 46 cell types and approximately 90,000 cells, early-onset preeclampsia showed broad cellular dysregulation across trophoblast, stromal, endothelial, myeloid and lymphoid compartments, with upregulated FLT1 and downregulated PGF in syncytiotrophoblast and extravillous trophoblast, heat-shock and apoptotic stress signatures in endothelium and pericytes, and marked inflammatory activation including SPP1, CXCL2 and CXCL3. Late-onset preeclampsia, by contrast, retained largely adaptive placental features with more localised dysregulation of extracellular matrix remodelling and angiogenic markers (Solt et al., 2025). The authors draw the therapeutic corollary explicitly: early-onset disease argues for interventions that target the angiogenic pathway, including RNA-based therapies directed at placental cells, whereas late-onset disease may be better served by maternal cardiovascular optimisation.

## 4. Mechanism: evidence that angiogenic imbalance is causal

### 4.1 Sufficiency — sFlt-1 reproduces the syndrome in animals

The foundational experiment showed that placental sFlt-1 is upregulated in preeclampsia, that circulating levels fall after delivery, and that the rise is accompanied by reduced free VEGF and free PlGF. Serum from affected women caused endothelial dysfunction in vitro that could be rescued by adding exogenous VEGF and PlGF; VEGF and PlGF relaxed rat renal arterioles and sFlt-1 blocked that relaxation; and administering sFlt-1 to pregnant rats produced hypertension, proteinuria and glomerular endotheliosis — the classic renal lesion (Maynard et al., 2003). Sufficiency in an animal, reversibility in vitro and resolution after removal of the source constitute an unusually complete causal chain for a human syndrome.

### 4.2 Soluble endoglin — a second hit on a parallel pathway

Soluble endoglin (sEng) is a placenta-derived truncated form of the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) co-receptor endoglin. It is elevated in preeclamptic sera, correlates with disease severity and falls after delivery. Functionally it impairs binding of TGF-β1 to its receptors, blunting downstream activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase and TGF-β–mediated vasodilation; it inhibits capillary tube formation in vitro and induces vascular permeability and hypertension in vivo. Crucially, its effects are amplified by co-administration of sFlt-1: together the two factors produce severe preeclampsia in pregnant rats, including HELLP-like haemolysis with elevated liver enzymes and low platelets, and restriction of fetal growth (Venkatesha et al., 2006). The axis is therefore not a single-molecule story but the simultaneous withdrawal of two endothelial survival signals — VEGF/PlGF and TGF-β.

### 4.3 Human longitudinal evidence — the biomarkers move before the disease

In a nested case-control study within the Calcium for Preeclampsia Prevention trial, serial sera from 120 case–control pairs (655 specimens) showed that in normotensive pregnancy sFlt-1 rises and PlGF falls over the last two months of gestation; in women who developed preeclampsia these changes occurred earlier and were more pronounced. Circulating sFlt-1 began rising approximately five weeks before clinical onset, and at onset mean serum sFlt-1 was 4,382 pg/mL in affected women versus 1,643 pg/mL in gestation-matched controls (P \< 0.001). Free PlGF was already significantly lower in future cases from 13–16 weeks of gestation (90 versus 142 pg/mL, P = 0.01). The magnitude of these alterations was greater in women with earlier onset and in those whose infants were small for gestational age (Levine et al., 2004).

The clinical importance of the second panel is easy to understand. A biomarker that becomes abnormal only at the moment of diagnosis adds little; one that diverges in the second trimester and accelerates five weeks before clinical onset defines a window in which risk can be stratified and, potentially, modified.

### 4.4 Genetic evidence — the fetal genome points to the same locus

Maternal genome-wide association studies had long failed to yield replicable loci. The breakthrough came from genotyping the offspring. In 4,380 offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies and 310,238 population controls, a single genome-wide significant locus emerged at rs4769613 (P = 5.4 × 10⁻¹¹), located near FLT1 on chromosome 13; a second nearby variant, rs12050029, was associated independently. The association was strongest for offspring of pregnancies in which preeclampsia developed in late gestation and in which birth weight exceeded the tenth centile (McGinnis et al., 2017). Because the fetal genome is the placental genome, this is evidence that dysregulation at FLT1 in the placenta is a fundamental molecular defect rather than a downstream consequence of maternal illness — an argument developed in detail by Gray et al. (2018). It is also a reminder that the genetic signal was strongest in the late-onset subtype whose pathogenesis is most often described as 'maternal'.

### 4.5 Co-mediators and amplifiers

Angiogenic imbalance does not act alone. Agonistic autoantibodies to the angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1-AA) are detectable in many preeclamptic women; transferring AT1-AA to pregnant rats at concentrations comparable to those in affected women raises arterial pressure, although the quantitative contribution in humans remains incompletely defined (LaMarca et al., 2011). The stressed syncytium also sheds extracellular vesicles, cell-free fetal DNA and inflammatory mediators into maternal blood. Fetal cells themselves cross into the maternal circulation in increased numbers in preeclampsia and can persist as microchimerism, which has been proposed as a mechanistic link between Stage 1 placental dysfunction and Stage 2 maternal inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, and possibly between placental syndromes and later maternal cardiovascular disease (Jacobsen et al., 2023).

## 5. Translation I: the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in clinical practice

Because the two arms of the axis move in opposite directions, their ratio amplifies the signal and partially cancels assay and gestational-age variation. The sFlt-1/PlGF ratio has consequently become the first mechanism-derived biomarker in obstetrics to enter routine use, and the ratio is now approved in the United States for preeclampsia risk assessment (Lee et al., 2025).

Its principal value is negative. In the PROGNOSIS programme a ratio cutoff of ≤38 was established to rule out preeclampsia within one week; in the Japanese cohort of PROGNOSIS Asia, among 180 evaluable women with suspected preeclampsia (prevalence 13.3%), a ratio ≤38 gave a negative predictive value of 100% (95% CI 97.5–100) for the following week, while a ratio \>38 gave a positive predictive value of only 32.4% (95% CI 18.0–49.8) for preeclampsia within four weeks. The area under the curve for predicting preeclampsia or maternal/fetal adverse outcomes within one week was 94.2%, and after adjustment for gestational age and final diagnosis, women with a ratio \>38 had a 2.8-fold greater risk of imminent delivery (Ohkuchi et al., 2021).

That asymmetry — excellent rule-out, mediocre rule-in — is exactly what a stretched maternity service needs: it identifies the large majority of women with suspicious symptoms who can safely be sent home. Real-world data support the resource implication. Comparing the German PROGNOSIS cohort (ratio blinded) with a Leipzig routine-care cohort in which the ratio guided decisions, hospitalisations for confirmed preeclampsia were significantly shorter in routine use (median 1 versus 4 days, P = 0.0093), and all-cause hospitalisations were shorter (median 1 versus 3 days, P \< 0.0001), with comparable preeclampsia incidence and gestational age at delivery (Dathan-Stumpf et al., 2022).

Limitations remain. A single threshold cannot serve all gestational ages or all phenotypes equally, assay platforms are not interchangeable, and the modest positive predictive value means the ratio must not be used alone to justify delivery. Multivariable models that combine angiogenic markers with maternal characteristics, blood pressure and uterine artery Doppler across gestation are the natural next step (Deshpande et al., 2021), and the ratio is now itself being tested as the trigger for an intervention rather than merely as a predictor — most ambitiously in PE37, a trial randomising more than 9,000 women to sFlt-1/PlGF screening at 35–36 weeks with planned delivery versus usual care (NCT04766866).

## 6. Translation II: therapeutics aimed at the axis

### 6.1 Prevention: low-dose aspirin

The only widely implemented preventive intervention is low-dose aspirin, begun in the first trimester in women identified as high-risk. In the ASPRE trial, 1,776 women with singleton pregnancies screened as high risk for preterm preeclampsia were randomised to aspirin 150 mg daily or placebo from 11–14 weeks until 36 weeks. Preterm preeclampsia occurred in 13 of 798 women (1.6%) on aspirin versus 35 of 822 (4.3%) on placebo — an odds ratio of 0.38 (95% CI 0.20–0.74; P = 0.004) — with no significant excess of neonatal or maternal adverse events (Rolnik et al., 2017). Aspirin acts upstream of the angiogenic axis, plausibly by shifting the thromboxane–prostacyclin balance during placentation, and its effect is concentrated in preterm disease; it does not treat established preeclampsia.

### 6.2 Repurposed drugs: metformin and proton pump inhibitors

Both metformin and proton pump inhibitors reduce sFlt-1 and sEng secretion from placental tissue in vitro, which prompted clinical testing. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in Cape Town, 180 women with preterm preeclampsia at 26+0 to 31+6 weeks undergoing expectant management received 3 g daily of extended-release metformin or placebo. Median time from randomisation to delivery was 17.7 days with metformin versus 10.1 days with placebo — a median difference of 7.6 days (geometric mean ratio 1.39, 95% CI 0.99–1.95; P = 0.057 for the primary intention-to-treat comparison). Among women who continued any dose the difference was 9.6 days (GMR 1.67, 95% CI 1.16–2.42), and among those taking the full dose 11.5 days (GMR 1.85, 95% CI 1.14–2.88). Notably, circulating sFlt-1, PlGF and sEng did not differ between arms, so the mechanism of benefit — if real — may not be angiogenic at all. Diarrhoea was more common with metformin; no serious drug-related adverse events occurred (Cluver et al., 2021).

The proton pump inhibitor story is more cautionary. In a randomised study of 20 women with confirmed preeclampsia receiving omeprazole versus 20 who did not, four days of omeprazole did not change circulating sFlt-1, free PlGF or CT-proET-1, and pregnancy prolongation was similar (median 15 versus 14 days). In an ex vivo placental perfusion model both omeprazole and esomeprazole crossed to the fetal compartment (fetal-to-maternal ratios 0.43–0.59), but only esomeprazole inhibited placental sFlt-1 release — a reminder that stereochemistry and placental pharmacokinetics matter, and that an in vitro signal need not survive translation (Neuman et al., 2022).

### 6.3 Extracorporeal removal of sFlt-1

If circulating sFlt-1 is the proximate cause of the maternal syndrome, removing it should help. The first demonstration used negatively charged dextran sulfate cellulose columns, which adsorb sFlt-1 in vitro. In a pilot study, a single apheresis session reduced circulating sFlt-1 dose-dependently in five women with very preterm preeclampsia; three further women treated repeatedly showed lowered sFlt-1, reduced proteinuria and stabilised blood pressure without apparent harm to mother or fetus, with pregnancies continuing 15 and 19 days after two treatments and 23 days after four, and with evidence of continued fetal growth (Thadhani et al., 2011).

Subsequent experience has been mixed and instructive. A retrospective series of six women treated before 28 weeks with dextran-sulfate adsorption or therapeutic plasma exchange achieved median absolute sFlt-1 reductions of 42% and 34% respectively, with pregnancy prolonged a median of 10 days from first apheresis; all procedures were tolerated by the fetuses, but anaphylactoid reactions, often with angioedema and attributed to bradykinin activation, occurred in four of six women undergoing dextran-sulfate adsorption, leading the authors to favour the non-selective but better-tolerated plasma exchange (Gubensek et al., 2021). An open pilot of heparin-mediated extracorporeal LDL precipitation (H.E.L.P.) apheresis in six women at 24+4 to 27+0 weeks prolonged pregnancy to a mean of 15.0 days from admission versus 6.3 days in matched historical controls (P = 0.027), and all infants were discharged healthy — yet baseline sFlt-1 levels and the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio were essentially unchanged across treatments, so the benefit was attributed to lipid removal or to other undefined factors rather than to sFlt-1 clearance (Winkler et al., 2018). A selective Flt-1 adsorption column has also been tested in a completed proof-of-concept trial (NCT02923206, n = 21). Apheresis thus remains a demanding, resource-intensive intervention whose mechanism of benefit is not securely established.

### 6.4 RNA interference: silencing the placental isoforms

The most mechanistically precise approach is to stop the placenta making the protein. The central design problem is selectivity: the therapy must silence the soluble isoforms without knocking down full-length FLT1, and it must reach the placenta, an organ no systemic nucleic-acid therapeutic had previously been shown to engage.

Both problems were addressed in a landmark study. Short interfering RNAs were identified that selectively silence the three sFLT1 mRNA isoforms chiefly responsible for placental overexpression while sparing full-length FLT1 message. Full chemical stabilisation combined with hydrophobic (cholesterol) conjugation enabled productive accumulation in the placenta — up to 7% of the injected dose — and reduced circulating sFLT1 by up to 50% in pregnant mice. In a baboon model of preeclampsia, a single dose suppressed sFLT1 overexpression and the clinical signs of disease. The work also established, as a general matter, that non-formulated siRNAs can modulate gene expression in an extrahepatic tissue in non-human primates (Turanov et al., 2018). Systematic chemical optimisation followed: increased 2′-O-methyl content, 5′ stabilisation, sequence-specific duplex asymmetry and a phosphocholine–docosanoic acid conjugate improved placental accumulation, silencing efficiency and safety (Davis et al., 2022).

This pipeline has now reached patients. CBP-4888, an siRNA therapeutic comprising siRNA-2283 and siRNA-2519, is in an open-label, single-ascending-dose Phase 1 study (NCT07282171) in up to 60 hospitalised women with preterm preeclampsia at 26+0 to 35+6 weeks receiving standard expectant management, across sites in Australia; co-primary endpoints are treatment-emergent adverse events through six weeks postpartum and the pharmacodynamic effect on maternal plasma sFlt-1, with infants followed to 24 months including neurodevelopmental screening. Recruitment began in February 2025, with primary completion expected in 2027. Parallel preclinical work is extending the approach: a dual-target divalent siRNA delivered by a ligand-functionalised membrane-fusogenic liposome designed for invasive extravillous trophoblast simultaneously reduced maternal sFlt-1 and sEng, reactivated PI3K/Akt/eNOS signalling, and improved hypertension, proteinuria and fetal growth restriction in a preeclamptic mouse model (Qian et al., 2026).

## 7. After delivery: preeclampsia as a cardiovascular risk marker

Delivery resolves the syndrome but not the risk. Pooled across 73 studies, women with a history of preeclampsia had roughly double the odds of a composite adverse cardiovascular outcome compared with women with normotensive pregnancies, with markedly elevated odds of subsequent hypertension and metabolic disease; the associations persisted whether outcomes occurred within one year, within one to ten years, or beyond ten years, and were consistently stronger after early-onset than after late-onset disease (Dall'Asta et al., 2021). A separate meta-analysis of 24 studies and more than 10.6 million participants found that preeclampsia was associated with any stroke (adjusted RR 1.75, 95% CI 1.56–1.97), ischaemic stroke (1.74, 1.46–2.06) and, most strikingly, haemorrhagic stroke (2.77, 2.04–3.75) (Brohan et al., 2023).

Whether preeclampsia unmasks a pre-existing cardiovascular predisposition or itself contributes to vascular injury remains unsettled, and both are probably true. Either way, the practical implication is the same and is still poorly implemented: a preeclamptic pregnancy is an early, sex-specific risk marker that identifies young women for long-term cardiovascular surveillance decades before conventional risk scores would flag them (Rana et al., 2019).

## 8. Open questions

### 8.1 Is sFlt-1 the whole story, or the best-measured part of it?

Two observations temper enthusiasm. Metformin prolonged gestation without changing circulating sFlt-1, PlGF or sEng (Cluver et al., 2021), and H.E.L.P. apheresis prolonged pregnancy without lowering baseline sFlt-1 (Winkler et al., 2018). If benefit can be obtained without moving the biomarker, then either the biomarker is an imperfect readout of the pathogenic activity, or there are parallel pathways — lipid, inflammatory, complement, autoantibody-mediated — that are independently addressable. The forthcoming Phase 1 pharmacodynamic data from CBP-4888 will be the cleanest available test of the converse proposition: whether lowering sFlt-1 specifically, by the most selective means yet devised, translates into clinical benefit.

### 8.2 The heterogeneity problem

Single-cell data now show that early- and late-onset preeclampsia are molecularly distinct, the former marked by broad placental dysregulation and the latter by comparatively subtle, localised changes (Solt et al., 2025) — yet the fetal FLT1 genetic signal was strongest in late-onset disease (McGinnis et al., 2017). Reconciling these observations matters for trial design: pooling phenotypes may dilute an effect that is real in one subtype, and a therapy aimed at placental sFLT1 output is most plausible precisely where that output is most deranged.

### 8.3 Models that do not misrepresent the human placenta

Deep interstitial and endovascular trophoblast invasion is a human-dominant trait; rodent models reproduce it poorly, which is why the baboon experiments were so consequential. In vitro systems are improving fast — trophoblast organoids, placenta-on-a-chip devices and three-dimensional bioprinted constructs now recapitulate aspects of the placental microenvironment and permit controlled study of aberrant angiogenesis while reducing dependence on scarce primary tissue; in vivo models remain necessary to connect placental factor release to maternal phenotype, therapeutic response and fetal outcome (Akbarzadeh et al., 2026). Integrating bioengineered human systems with animal work is the most plausible route to the predictive preclinical package that an obstetric therapeutic requires.

### 8.4 The ethics and pharmacology of treating two patients

Any drug given in pregnancy has two recipients. The siRNA strategy is designed around this constraint — isoform selectivity to spare full-length FLT1, placental accumulation to limit systemic exposure, chemical modification to reduce immunostimulation — and the Phase 1 protocol accordingly follows infants to 24 months with developmental screening (NCT07282171). Dosing is a genuine dilemma: sFlt-1 excess must be reduced enough to protect the mother, yet VEGF signalling is required for normal placental vascular development, so excessive suppression could in principle harm the fetus. Establishing the therapeutic window will be the central task of early-phase work.

### 8.5 Equity of access

The global burden of preeclampsia falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income settings, where the conventional diagnostic criteria perform poorly and specialist resources are scarce (Deshpande et al., 2021). Apheresis is unlikely to be deliverable there. A single subcutaneous injection, if it works, might be — which is an argument for prioritising modalities whose delivery requirements match the epidemiology of the disease rather than the infrastructure of the countries where they are developed.

## 9. Conclusion

Preeclampsia has moved, in roughly two decades, from a syndrome defined by signs to a disease defined by a mechanism. The demonstration that placental sFlt-1 is overproduced, that it is sufficient to reproduce hypertension, proteinuria and glomerular endotheliosis in animals, that it rises weeks before clinical onset in women, that a second placental factor amplifies it through a parallel pathway, and that the fetal genome implicates the very locus from which it derives, together make the antiangiogenic axis one of the better-supported causal chains in human disease biology.

That understanding has already changed practice: the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio provides a rule-out test with a negative predictive value approaching 100% at one week, and its routine use shortens hospital stays. It has not yet changed treatment, which still ends in delivery. The most direct attempt to change that — isoform-selective, chemically stabilised siRNA that silences placental sFLT1 without touching the full-length receptor, validated in pregnant mice and in a non-human primate model of the disease and now in first-in-human testing — is also the most faithful translation of the mechanism. Whether it succeeds will test not only a drug but the proposition that preeclampsia is, at its core, a disease of a single secreted decoy receptor. The discordant results from metformin and lipoprotein apheresis are a standing reminder that it may be more than that.
