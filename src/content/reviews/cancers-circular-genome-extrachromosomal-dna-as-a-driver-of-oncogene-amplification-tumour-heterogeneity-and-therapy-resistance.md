*A review of the molecular biology, clinical impact and emerging therapeutic targeting of ecDNA*

Weekly Science Review \| 16 November 2026

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Abstract

For most of the century since the chromosome theory of inheritance was formalised, cancer genomes have been read as damaged chromosomes: deleted, duplicated, translocated, but still chromosomes. Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) breaks that frame. ecDNAs are large, circular, acentric DNA molecules — typically hundreds of kilobases to several megabases — that carry oncogenes together with their regulatory elements and replicate inside the nucleus without being attached to a centromere. Because they lack centromeres, they are not partitioned faithfully at mitosis; daughter cells receive unequal and essentially random numbers of them. The consequences are profound. A tumour can raise or lower the dosage of a driver oncogene within a few cell generations, generate extreme cell-to-cell genetic heterogeneity without waiting for new mutations, and reverse that change when selective pressure is removed.

Whole-genome sequencing of 14,778 patients in the 100,000 Genomes Project detected ecDNA in 17.1% of tumour samples, with frequencies above 45% in liposarcoma, glioblastoma and HER2-positive breast cancer, and ecDNA detection was independently associated with higher stage, metastasis and shorter overall survival. Mechanistically, ecDNA combines unusually accessible chromatin, circular regulatory architecture, and the capacity to cluster into transcriptionally active ‘hubs’ in which enhancers on one molecule drive promoters on another. Recent work has begun to explain how such centromere-free elements are nonetheless retained across generations, through CpG-rich retention elements, BRD4-dependent mitotic clustering and tethering to mitotic chromosomes. The same features create a liability: pervasive transcription on ecDNA collides with its replication machinery, producing replication stress and CHK1 dependence that can be exploited pharmacologically. This review summarises the structural biology, formation mechanisms, gene-regulatory behaviour and inheritance of ecDNA; the clinical epidemiology and resistance phenotypes it produces; the first generation of ecDNA-directed therapeutic candidates, including the two first-in-human trials that have now been terminated; and the open questions that will determine whether ecDNA becomes a tractable therapeutic target or remains a prognostic marker.

**Keywords:** extrachromosomal DNA; ecDNA; oncogene amplification; chromothripsis; intratumoral heterogeneity; transcription–replication conflict; CHK1; targeted therapy resistance.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Gene amplification and the chromosomal assumption

Increasing the dosage of a growth-promoting gene is one of the oldest tricks in the cancer repertoire. Amplification of *MYC*, *EGFR*, *ERBB2* (HER2), *MYCN*, *CDK4*, *MDM2* and *FGFR2* occurs across many tumour lineages and frequently determines both the tumour's behaviour and its response to targeted drugs. The textbook mechanisms by which such amplifications arise — breakage–fusion–bridge (BFB) cycles, tandem duplication, unequal recombination — all produce amplified copies that stay attached to a chromosome. In that model, amplification is heritable in the ordinary Mendelian sense: at mitosis, sister chromatids separate, and each daughter cell inherits the amplified locus at approximately the parental copy number \[8, 9\].

Cytogeneticists have nevertheless been aware since the 1960s of a second kind of amplification. Small paired chromatin bodies without centromeres, called ‘double minutes’, appear in metaphase spreads of some tumours, particularly neuroblastoma and glioma. For decades these were catalogued as a curiosity of unstable karyotypes \[9, 20\]. The rebranding of double minutes as ecDNA, and the recognition that they constitute a major and distinct class of oncogenic lesion, is a development of the past decade, driven by whole-genome sequencing combined with computational amplicon reconstruction and by direct imaging \[2, 20\].

### 1.2 What makes ecDNA different

An ecDNA is a circular, double-stranded, chromatinised DNA molecule, typically larger than about 500 kilobases and often several megabases, that resides in the nucleus and replicates once per cell cycle, but which lacks a centromere and therefore lacks a mechanism for ordered segregation \[14, 20\]. Four properties follow, and together they account for essentially all of ecDNA's clinical significance.

First, **copy number can be very high**. In the pan-cancer 100,000 Genomes Project analysis, in 36 of 37 tumour types in which ecDNA was detected, focal amplifications on ecDNA carried higher copy number than chromosomal focal amplifications in the same tumour type \[14\]. Second, **transcription per copy is also higher**: ecDNA-encoded oncogenes rank among the most highly expressed genes in the tumour transcriptome, with the increase exceeding what copy number alone predicts, because ecDNA chromatin is unusually accessible and lacks the higher-order compaction typical of chromosomes \[3, 4\]. Third, **inheritance is random**, so ecDNA generates intratumoral heterogeneity in gene dosage faster than any mutational process can \[2, 7\]. Fourth, **that heterogeneity is reversible**: a cell population can lose ecDNA copies under drug pressure and recover them after drug withdrawal, which is a qualitatively different form of resistance from a fixed secondary mutation \[1\].

## 2. Molecular anatomy, formation and detection

### 2.1 Structure and chromatin state

The structural description of ecDNA that underlies current thinking came from integrating electron microscopy and structured-illumination imaging, long-range optical mapping and whole-genome sequence reconstruction \[3\]. That work established that ecDNAs are genuinely circular rather than linear fragments, that they are packaged into nucleosomal chromatin with intact local domain structure, and — critically — that they lack the higher-order compaction that characterises chromosomal DNA. Measured by ATAC-seq and related assays, ecDNA chromatin is substantially more accessible than the same sequence in its chromosomal context \[3\].

The circularity itself has regulatory consequences. A circle has no telomeres and no topologically associating domain boundaries of the usual chromosomal kind, so regulatory elements that would be insulated from a promoter on a chromosome can contact it on an ecDNA. Chromosome conformation capture applied to ecDNA-bearing cells showed a marked excess of ultra-long-range contacts between ecDNA and active chromatin \[3\]. In other words, ecDNA is not simply a chromosomal segment present at high copy number; it is a segment placed in a different regulatory environment.

### 2.2 How ecDNA forms

Two broad routes to ecDNA formation are supported by experimental data, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**Chromothripsis.** In a landmark set of experiments using cells selected for chemotherapeutic resistance, longitudinal whole-genome sequencing showed that chromothripsis — a catastrophic shattering and imperfect reassembly of one or a few chromosomal regions — is a major driver of ecDNA formation, in a manner dependent on poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) and the catalytic subunit of DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKcs), the enzymes of non-homologous end joining and related repair pathways \[5\]. The same study showed that ecDNA continues to evolve structurally through further rounds of chromothripsis, with each round associated with greater drug tolerance, and that ecDNAs preferentially tether near chromosome ends in interphase \[5\].

Multi-region sequencing of osteosarcoma has since shown that chromothripsis is not a single catastrophic event confined to tumour initiation but an ongoing, subclonal process, detected in 74% of osteosarcomas, generating unstable derivative chromosomes whose continued evolution drives clonal diversification \[17\]. That study also characterised a distinct mechanism, loss–translocation–amplification chromothripsis, in which a single double-strand break simultaneously inactivates *TP53* and initiates oncogene amplification through BFB cycles \[17\].

**Episome excision and circularisation.** A second route involves excision of a chromosomal segment and its circularisation into a small episome that is subsequently amplified, a model supported by the existence of structurally simple, low-complexity ecDNAs \[9, 14\]. Amplicon complexity analysis across the 100,000 Genomes Project cohort is consistent with both routes operating in different tissue contexts: sarcomas were enriched for high-complexity amplicons associated with high structural-variant burden, whereas colorectal tumours were enriched for low-complexity ecDNA associated with high ploidy and genome instability but not structural-variant burden \[14\].

It matters clinically that ecDNA be distinguished from BFB amplification, because the two produce superficially similar copy-number profiles from short-read sequencing but behave differently. Using optical genome mapping together with a purpose-built algorithm, BFB amplifications were detected in 371 events across 2,557 primary tumours and cell lines, concentrated in cervical, head and neck, lung and oesophageal cancers and rare in brain tumours; unlike ecDNA, BFB amplifications drove high copy number without producing comparable intratumoral heterogeneity or rapid genome change \[12\].

### 2.3 Mutational processes acting before and after ecDNA formation

Because mutations present on every copy of an ecDNA must have arisen before the molecule was amplified, whereas mutations present on only a subset arose afterwards, the timing of mutational processes relative to ecDNA birth can be reconstructed from allele frequencies. Applying this to the 100,000 Genomes Project cohort showed that signatures of tobacco exposure (SBS4, SBS92), ultraviolet light (SBS7a) and clock-like deamination (SBS1) tend to act before ecDNA formation, whereas the homologous-recombination-deficiency signature SBS3 tends to accumulate afterwards \[14\]. ecDNA-positive tumours also showed higher tumour mutational burden than tumours with chromosomal amplification only, but an inverse association with hypermutator phenotypes driven by mismatch-repair and polymerase proofreading deficiency \[14\].

Therapy itself can shape ecDNA evolution. In one glioblastoma treated with temozolomide, an *EGFR* missense mutation was present on each of 67 ecDNA copies, and the ecDNAs carried a temozolomide-associated hypermutation signature with some variants reaching high allele frequencies, consistent with mutagenesis followed by selection acting on the extrachromosomal compartment \[14\].

## 3. Gene regulation on ecDNA: hubs, enhancers and fusions

### 3.1 ecDNA hubs and intermolecular enhancer–promoter contacts

Conventional gene induction is a *cis* process: an enhancer contacts a promoter on the same DNA molecule. ecDNA breaks that constraint. Imaging of ecDNA-bearing cancer cells revealed that ecDNAs are not dispersed randomly through the nucleus but cluster into ‘hubs’ of roughly 10 to 100 molecules, in diverse cancer cell types and in primary tumours \[6\]. Within a hub, an enhancer on one ecDNA can activate a promoter on a different ecDNA molecule — enhancer action in *trans*. Each individual ecDNA is more likely to be transcribing its oncogene when it is spatially clustered with others \[6\].

The hubs are held together by protein. In a *MYC*-amplified colorectal cancer line, hub tethering depended on the bromodomain and extraterminal domain protein BRD4, and the BET inhibitor JQ1 dispersed the hubs and preferentially suppressed transcription of ecDNA-derived oncogenes relative to their chromosomal counterparts \[6\]. Independent live-cell work using CRISPR-based ecDNA tagging found that after mitosis ecDNAs re-cluster into hubs that colocalise with RNA polymerase II, linking hub formation directly to transcriptional output \[7\].

### 3.2 Regulatory-element-only ecDNA

If enhancers can act in *trans* between molecules, an ecDNA carrying only regulatory sequence should still be functional. Annotation of ecDNA sequence content in the 100,000 Genomes Project cohort found exactly that: a class of ecDNA carrying enhancers, promoters and long non-coding RNAs but no coding genes. Compared with gene-bearing ecDNA, these regulatory ecDNAs had a significantly higher density of distal enhancers (27.3 versus 13.7 per megabase) and promoters (13.7 versus 3.6 per megabase), were smaller (median 0.12 versus 3.56 Mb), structurally simpler and present at lower copy number \[14\]. Crucially, enhancer-only ecDNAs were frequently co-amplified in the same tumour as oncogene-bearing ecDNAs (140 samples), and when co-amplified they reached higher copy number than when found alone — the pattern expected if they are selected for their ability to boost oncogene transcription in *trans* \[14\].

### 3.3 Fusion transcripts generated on ecDNA

ecDNA is also a factory for structural variants. Integrated analysis of whole-genome and transcriptome data across tumours and cell lines showed that ecDNAs have the highest rate of oncogene fusion events of any class of copy-number alteration \[26\]. The most common fusion hotspot involves the long non-coding RNA gene *PVT1*: fusion of *PVT1* exon 1 to diverse 3′ partners increased RNA stability, apparently through an SRSF1-dependent mechanism, enhanced MYC-dependent transcription and supported cancer cell survival \[26\]. Earlier pan-cancer work had already shown that ecDNA amplifications more frequently produce transcript fusions than chromosomal amplifications do \[4\].

Taken together, these findings argue that ecDNA should be understood as a gene-regulatory lesion as much as a dosage lesion. The amount of oncoprotein a cell makes reflects copy number, chromatin accessibility, hub membership, the availability of enhancers on neighbouring molecules and the stability of the transcripts produced — all of which differ systematically from the chromosomal case (Table 2).

| **Property** | **Focal chromosomal amplification** | **ecDNA** |
|----|----|----|
| Segregation at mitosis | Centromere-directed; approximately equal partition | No centromere; random and often asymmetric partition between daughter cells \[2, 7\] |
| Copy number attainable | Lower in 36 of 37 tumour types analysed | Higher focal copy number in 36 of 37 tumour types \[14\] |

## 4. Inheritance: how a centromere-free element persists

### 4.1 Random segregation and its evolutionary consequences

The founding observation of modern ecDNA biology is that ecDNA segregation is non-Mendelian. Analysis of 17 cancer types, including scoring of chromosome structure in 2,572 dividing cells, found ecDNA in nearly half of the cancers examined and almost never in normal cells, with driver oncogenes preferentially amplified on ecDNA; mathematical modelling predicted — and cytogenetic data confirmed — that random segregation increases both mean oncogene copy number and intratumoral heterogeneity more effectively than chromosomal amplification \[2\].

Single-cell measurement confirms how extreme the resulting variation is. Across patient tissues and cell lines, cell-to-cell ecDNA copy number varies enormously, and CRISPR-based tagging of ecDNA breakpoints in living cells showed disjointed inheritance patterns that let individual cells accumulate ecDNA rapidly \[7\]. Figure 2 illustrates the practical meaning of this difference for a tumour population.

### 4.2 Coordinated inheritance of cooperating ecDNA species

If segregation were truly independent, a cell carrying two different ecDNA species — say one bearing *MYC* and another bearing a receptor tyrosine kinase gene — would quickly lose the combination. In practice such combinations persist, and the explanation is that distinct ecDNA species are coordinately inherited. Imaging and single-cell analysis showed that multiple ecDNAs encoding different oncogenes co-occur, that their copy numbers are correlated, and that they are co-segregated asymmetrically during mitosis so that one daughter cell simultaneously gains copies of several cooperating species \[15\]. This is a mechanism for maintaining advantageous combinations of amplified genes without physically linking them — a kind of selection-friendly linkage disequilibrium between free-floating molecules.

### 4.3 Retention elements and mitotic tethering

The question of how acentric circles are transmitted at all has recently acquired mechanistic answers. More than forty years after ecDNAs were first observed to ‘hitchhike’ on mitotic chromosomes, a genome-scale assay identified a family of human genomic sequences, termed retention elements, that tether episomes to mitotic chromosomes and thereby increase transmission to daughter cells \[25\]. Retention elements are a select subset of CpG-rich gene promoters and act additively, so an ecDNA carrying more of them is retained more efficiently \[25\].

A complementary line of work showed that accurate nuclear segregation of *MYC*-amplifying ecDNA depends on BRD4-mediated transcriptional co-activation and on mitotic transcription of the long non-coding RNA *PVT1*, which is commonly co-amplified with *MYC*. Disrupting mitotic clustering — by BRD4 inhibition, *PVT1* depletion or transcription inhibition — caused ecDNA to be mis-segregated into micronuclei, protecting against which is evidently part of how ecDNA persists \[22\].

### 4.4 A counterweight: micronuclear ecDNA constrains fitness

Mis-segregation is not only a technical failure mode; it may be a built-in brake. A 2026 study found that ecDNAs are a major source of micronuclei, especially after ecDNA damage and under replication stress. Replication stress promoted mitotic ecDNA clustering, asymmetric inheritance and mis-segregation into micronuclei with reduced transcriptional output; single-micronucleus sequencing showed that several distinct ecDNA species can coalesce within one micronucleus. Cells that inherited micronuclear ecDNA proliferated poorly and were more likely to die, and in patients with *MYCN*-amplified neuroblastoma a higher frequency of micronuclear ecDNA at diagnosis was associated with improved overall survival \[27\]. The therapeutic implication is the mirror image of the mechanisms above: if retention and mitotic tethering are what keep ecDNA useful to the tumour, then deliberately breaking retention should be beneficial to the patient.

## 5. Clinical landscape: prevalence, outcomes and resistance

### 5.1 How common is ecDNA?

Estimates have converged as methods have improved. An early pan-cancer computational analysis of 3,212 patients found ecDNA amplification in most cancer types but not in blood or normal tissue, with oncogenes strongly enriched on ecDNA and ecDNA-amplified oncogenes transcribed more highly than copy-number-matched linear DNA; patients whose tumours carried ecDNA had significantly shorter survival even after controlling for tissue type \[4\]. The largest analysis to date, of 15,832 whole-genome-sequenced samples from 14,778 patients in the 100,000 Genomes Project, detected ecDNA in 17.1% of tumour samples and identified 4,716 unique ecDNAs in 2,532 ecDNA-positive tumours \[14\]. Prevalence varies by lineage over the full possible range (Figure 3).

Two features of these data deserve emphasis. First, ecDNA detection is sensitive to sampling: among 578 patients with multi-region sequencing, the odds of detecting ecDNA were 2.6 times greater when two regions of the same tumour were sampled rather than one, and in more than 60% of multi-region-sequenced tumours ecDNA was found in only a subset of regions \[14\]. Single-biopsy prevalence estimates are therefore lower bounds. Second, ecDNA content tracks tissue lineage: 89.9% of ecDNAs derived from a single chromosomal locus, 46% carried more than one oncogene, and the oncogenes carried differed systematically by tumour type \[14\].

### 5.2 When does ecDNA appear?

ecDNA is not merely a late epiphenomenon of a disintegrating genome. Whole-genome sequencing of 206 biopsies from Barrett's oesophagus surveillance and oesophageal adenocarcinoma cohorts, together with multi-region, two-timepoint sampling from 80 patients in a case-control study, showed that ecDNA frequency increased from early-stage (24%) to late-stage (43%) oesophageal adenocarcinoma, and — importantly — that ecDNA could be detected in biopsies of high-grade dysplasia from patients who subsequently progressed to cancer \[10\]. ecDNA can therefore be an early event in the dysplasia-to-carcinoma transition, which raises the possibility of using it as a progression biomarker in surveillance populations. A prospective multicentre observational study of 1,000 participants is currently examining ecDNA in gastric cancer and precancerous gastric lesions \[33\].

### 5.3 Prognosis, stage and the immune microenvironment

In the 100,000 Genomes Project cohort, after adjustment for tumour type, stage, age, sex and underlying genome instability, ecDNA detection was associated with shorter overall survival (hazard ratio 1.44, 95% CI 1.25–1.66), whereas intrachromosomal amplification without ecDNA carried a smaller hazard (1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.33) \[14\]. ecDNA was more frequent at higher stage, in metastatic samples, and in patients who had received prior cytotoxic or targeted therapy (Figure 4) \[14\].

The immune findings are among the more unexpected results in the field. ecDNA amplifies not only classical drivers but also immunomodulatory genes: 34% of ecDNA-positive tumours carried known immunomodulatory genes on ecDNA, and in 41.5% of those tumours the immunomodulatory ecDNA carried no oncogene at all, arguing for independent selection. Gene-ontology analysis implicated negative regulation of immune effector processes, leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity and negative regulation of lymphocyte activation, and tumours carrying such ecDNA showed significantly reduced estimated T-cell fraction \[14\]. Consistent with this, in small cell lung cancer, amplification of *MYC* paralogues on ecDNA was associated with an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment characterised by spatial exclusion of immune effectors \[21\].

### 5.5 ecDNA as a resistance mechanism

The clinical through-line of these studies is that ecDNA is a resistance device of unusual flexibility. It can delete a drug target (mutant *EGFR* under tyrosine kinase inhibition \[1\]), amplify a bypass oncogene (*MYC* paralogues producing cross-resistance in small cell lung cancer \[13\]), amplify a drug efflux pump (*ABCB1* in bortezomib-resistant gallbladder cancer \[30\]), or simply hold a heterogeneous reservoir of receptor tyrosine kinase dosages so that some subclone is always poorly served by any single inhibitor \[18\]. Because the underlying change is a shift in the distribution of copy number rather than a fixed genetic alteration, it can be reversed when the pressure is lifted — which is precisely why it is not detected by resistance-mutation panels.

## 6. Therapeutic strategies directed at ecDNA

### 6.1 The synthetic lethality of excess: transcription–replication conflict

Until recently, no therapy targeted ecDNA as such. The most developed current strategy exploits a liability created by ecDNA's own transcriptional exuberance \[16\]. Stepwise analysis of ecDNA transcription revealed pervasive RNA synthesis and associated single-stranded DNA, producing excessive collisions between transcription and replication machinery relative to chromosomal loci. Nucleotide incorporation on ecDNA was markedly slower, and replication stress was higher in ecDNA-containing tumours regardless of cancer type or oncogene cargo. The single-stranded-DNA-binding repair mediator pRPA2-S33 accumulated on ecDNA in a transcription-dependent manner, alongside increased double-strand breaks and activation of the S-phase checkpoint kinase CHK1 \[16\].

That checkpoint dependence is druggable. Genetic or pharmacological CHK1 inhibition caused extensive and preferential death of ecDNA-containing tumour cells. An orally bioavailable, selective CHK1 inhibitor, BBI-2779, preferentially killed ecDNA-containing cells; in a gastric cancer model carrying *FGFR2* on ecDNA, it suppressed tumour growth and prevented ecDNA-mediated acquired resistance to the pan-FGFR inhibitor infigratinib, producing sustained regression in mice \[16\]. The authors framed this as exploiting a ‘synthetic lethality of excess’: the same hyper-transcription that makes ecDNA oncogenic makes it fragile.

### 6.2 Other mechanistic entry points

Three further strategies follow directly from the biology reviewed above, though none has yet reached clinical testing as an ecDNA-directed therapy.

**Dispersing hubs.** BET inhibition with JQ1 dispersed BRD4-tethered ecDNA hubs and preferentially suppressed ecDNA-derived oncogene transcription \[6\]. BET inhibitors have a long and difficult clinical history for other reasons, but the result establishes hub integrity as a druggable node.

**Breaking retention.** If CpG-rich retention elements and mitotic tethering are required for ecDNA transmission \[25\], and if ecDNA shunted into micronuclei reduces tumour cell fitness and associates with better survival in *MYCN*-amplified neuroblastoma \[27\], then agents that force mis-segregation rather than killing cells outright become attractive. This is conceptually distinct from cytotoxicity: the aim is to make the tumour lose its amplification.

**Combining with the targeted agent whose resistance ecDNA mediates.** Because ecDNA repeatedly mediates acquired resistance to receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors \[1, 13, 18\], the natural clinical design pairs an ecDNA-directed agent with the targeted therapy whose durability it is meant to protect — the design adopted in the first-in-human study described below \[31\].

### 6.3 The first clinical trials, and their outcome

Two first-in-human trials of the ecDNA-directed concept have been registered, both sponsored by Boundless Bio, and both have now been terminated (Table 4). The POTENTIATE study (NCT05827614) evaluated the oral CHK1 inhibitor BBI-355, described explicitly as an ecDNA-directed therapy, as a single agent and in combination with erlotinib, futibatinib or the ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor BBI-825, in patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumours harbouring oncogene amplifications and no remaining standard options \[31\]. Eligibility required documented oncogene amplification, archival or fresh tumour tissue, measurable disease by RECIST v1.1 and ECOG performance status 0–1; patients previously exposed to CHK1 or WEE1 inhibitors were excluded from the single-agent arm. The study enrolled 85 patients across 16 United States sites between March 2023 and its termination (recorded completion date 17 March 2026), with co-primary endpoints of treatment-emergent adverse events and determination of the maximum tolerated and recommended phase 2 doses \[31\]. A separate phase 1 study of BBI-825 in tumours with resistance gene amplifications (NCT06299761) enrolled 19 patients before terminating \[32\]. Neither registry record posts results.

The termination of both interventional studies is a meaningful datum for the field, and it should be read carefully. Registry records state that the studies were terminated but do not state why; termination of an early-phase oncology study may reflect toxicity, insufficient activity, or a sponsor's strategic or financial decision, and in the absence of posted results or a published report none of these can be distinguished from the registry alone \[31, 32\]. What can be said is that the preclinical rationale — that ecDNA-bearing cells are selectively dependent on CHK1 because of transcription–replication conflict — remains supported by the primary data \[16\], and that the first clinical translation of that rationale did not proceed to a registered phase 2 readout.

## 7. Current research directions and open questions

### 7.1 Models that can actually be manipulated

Much of what is known about ecDNA comes from observational genomics of human tumours and from a small number of cell lines that happen to carry ecDNA. A current priority is building experimental systems in which ecDNA can be engineered *de novo* and studied causally, including genetically engineered mouse models and genome-engineering approaches to generate defined ecDNA in otherwise isogenic backgrounds \[29\]. Without such models it is difficult to separate what ecDNA does from what the cells that tolerate ecDNA are like.

### 7.2 Which ecDNA features are selected, and which are byproducts?

Evidence for selection is strong: oncogenes are significantly enriched on ecDNA relative to permuted backgrounds and relative to chromosomal focal amplifications, and dₙ/dₛ analysis shows that *TP53*-pathway and other driver mutations are under more potent selection when amplified on ecDNA \[14\]. But other observations complicate the picture. High ecDNA copy number imposes a fitness cost on pancreatic cancer cells in the absence of selective pressure \[19\], and ecDNA mis-segregation into micronuclei actively constrains tumour cell fitness and correlates with better survival in neuroblastoma \[27\]. ecDNA is therefore not uniformly advantageous; it is a high-variance strategy whose payoff depends on the selective environment. Quantifying that trade-off is an open problem with direct therapeutic implications.

### 7.3 Detection, standardisation and clinical reporting

ecDNA status is not yet a routine component of clinical genomic reporting, and the methodological heterogeneity summarised in Table 1 is part of the reason. Community infrastructure is emerging — an end-to-end workflow with structural categorisation of ecDNA and a public repository of more than 15,000 analysed samples \[28\] — but questions of analytical validity at clinical sequencing depths, minimum purity thresholds, and the handling of multi-region and longitudinal sampling remain unsettled \[14, 28\]. Plasma-based detection by cell-free nucleosome profiling \[11\] is attractive precisely because ecDNA changes over time under therapy, but requires prospective validation.

### 7.4 Therapeutic questions

Several questions now have clinical weight. Does CHK1 inhibition, or replication-stress targeting more broadly, produce clinically meaningful benefit in a biomarker-selected ecDNA-positive population, and is ‘oncogene amplification’ as used in trial eligibility \[31\] an adequate proxy for ecDNA status given that chromosomal and extrachromosomal amplification differ in exactly the relevant biology \[12, 14\]? Can forcing ecDNA mis-segregation be achieved pharmacologically, and would it be tolerable? Does ecDNA-driven immune suppression \[14, 21\] mean that ecDNA-positive tumours should be triaged away from checkpoint blockade, or that they require combinations designed to restore T-cell infiltration? And given that prior chemotherapy and targeted therapy are both associated with higher ecDNA prevalence \[14\], should ecDNA-directed agents be deployed early, before the extrachromosomal reservoir is established, rather than in the heavily pretreated populations where first-in-human studies necessarily begin?

## 8. Conclusion

ecDNA has moved in roughly a decade from cytogenetic curiosity to one of the better-characterised mechanisms of oncogene amplification, present in about one in six tumours overall and in nearly half of liposarcomas, glioblastomas and HER2-positive breast cancers \[14\]. Its biology is coherent and unusual: a circular, centromere-free, highly accessible chromatin particle that is transcribed intensely, clusters into cooperative hubs where enhancers act between molecules, is inherited randomly but not entirely passively, and thereby lets a tumour tune oncogene dosage up or down within a few cell generations \[3, 6, 15, 25\]. That capacity explains why ecDNA-positive tumours are more heterogeneous, more often metastatic, more often treatment-refractory and associated with shorter survival \[4, 14\].

The field's central problem has shifted from description to intervention. The transcription–replication conflict hypothesis provided the first mechanistic, ecDNA-selective therapeutic rationale, validated in preclinical models with a CHK1 inhibitor \[16\]; the two first-in-human studies built on that programme have been terminated without posted results \[31, 32\]. Whether the concept fails or merely awaits a better molecule, a better biomarker or a better patient population is not yet answerable from the public record. What has clearly changed is that the question is now being asked in the right terms: not how much of an oncogene a tumour has, but on what kind of molecule it is carried, and what that molecule's peculiar physics makes possible — for the tumour, and against it.
