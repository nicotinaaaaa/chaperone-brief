*A mechanistic and therapeutic review of MSH3, PMS1, MLH1, MLH3 and FAN1 as modifiers of repeat instability*

Prepared 29 September 2026 · Literature retrieved from PubMed / PubMed Central, bioRxiv and ClinicalTrials.gov

## Abstract

For three decades Huntington's disease (HD) was understood as a polyglutamine disorder: an inherited CAG expansion in *HTT* encodes an abnormally long glutamine tract, the mutant protein misfolds, and striatal neurons die. Human genetics has now overturned the temporal logic of that model. Genome-wide association studies of age at onset repeatedly implicate DNA maintenance genes — *MSH3*, *MSH2*, *MLH1*, *MLH3*, *PMS1*, *PMS2* and *FAN1* — and not genes governing protein handling; the number of *pure* CAG triplets, rather than the length of the encoded polyglutamine tract, predicts when symptoms begin. The unifying explanation is that the inherited repeat is only a starting point. In post-mitotic neurons the tract continues to expand throughout life, driven by an error-prone mismatch repair (MMR) reaction initiated by the MutSβ (MSH2–MSH3) heterodimer, and it is this somatic expansion that sets the clock. Single-nucleus measurements in human post-mortem brain have since shown that striatal projection neurons inheriting 40–45 CAGs accumulate 100–500 or more, that expansion below roughly 150 CAGs is biologically silent, and that neurons cross a shared toxicity threshold near 150 CAGs after which they lose neuronal identity and die within a comparatively short window. Mouse genetics converts this into a rate problem: modal repeat length in Q140 medium spiny neurons grows linearly at about 8.8 CAGs per month, a rate cut to 2.3 and 0.3 CAGs per month by heterozygous and homozygous *Msh3* deletion respectively, with corresponding rescue of aggregation, transcriptional dysregulation and locomotor deficits. This review sets out the molecular background, the human and model-organism evidence, and the resulting therapeutic programme — antisense oligonucleotides, divalent siRNA, and orally available splice modulators, one of which (SKY-0515, which lowers both *HTT* and *PMS1*) has reached randomised Phase 2/3 testing. It closes with the unresolved questions that determine whether ‘slow the repeat’ becomes a disease-modifying therapy: how early treatment must begin, how expansion is to be measured in a living brain, and how much MMR can safely be removed from a human genome for decades.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The disease and its founding mutation

Huntington's disease is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterised by chorea and other movement abnormalities, progressive cognitive decline, and psychiatric disturbance, with death typically 15–20 years after motor onset. Its cause has been known since 1993: an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in exon 1 of *HTT*, the gene encoding huntingtin. Alleles below about 27 repeats are stable and normal; 36–39 repeats confer reduced penetrance; 40 or more repeats are fully penetrant. Longer inherited repeats produce earlier onset, and the relationship is steep enough that repeat length in blood explains roughly half the variance in age at motor onset.

The remaining variance is the subject of this review. It is substantially heritable and, critically, it is not distributed randomly across the genome. When the Genetic Modifiers of Huntington's Disease (GeM-HD) Consortium and allied groups searched for loci altering onset independently of inherited repeat length, they recovered a conspicuously coherent set of genes: components of the DNA mismatch repair machinery and the interstrand-crosslink nuclease *FAN1* \[3, 5, 7\]. Nothing about a polyglutamine-toxicity model predicts that result.

### 1.2 A change in the unit of analysis

The consequence is a shift from the gene to the cell, and from a fixed genotype to a moving one. If the repeat expands somatically, then the genotype relevant to a neuron's fate is not the one measured in that patient's blood at birth but the one present in that particular neuron at that particular moment. Two neurons in the same putamen, with the same inherited allele, may carry tracts differing by hundreds of repeats. Pathogenesis becomes a race between a slowly lengthening DNA tract and the lifespan of the cell carrying it — and a therapy that slows the tract is, in principle, a therapy that postpones the disease.

This review covers: the molecular biology of the repeat and of mismatch repair (Section 2); the human genetic evidence that somatic expansion is the rate-limiting step (Section 3); the mechanism by which a repair pathway causes mutation rather than preventing it (Section 4); the quantitative link between repeat length and neuronal death (Section 5); the therapeutic programme now in the clinic (Section 6); and the open questions (Section 7). The intended reader is scientifically literate but not a specialist in repeat biology.

## 2. Biological background

### 2.1 The CAG tract is a structural, not merely a coding, element

A (CAG)n·(CTG)n duplex is unusual DNA. Single strands of CAG and CTG both fold into imperfect hairpins stabilised by G·C base pairs with A·A or T·T mismatches at every third position. Whenever the duplex is transiently opened — during transcription, during repair synthesis, or at an R-loop — the displaced strand can fold back on itself and re-anneal out of register. The result is a **slipped-strand intermediate**: a correctly paired duplex flanked on one side by an extrahelical loop of repeat units, known as a slip-out. Slipped intermediates of this kind have been detected in DNA from patient tissues, and the size and junction conformation of the slip-out determine which repair factors engage it \[16\].

Two features of the tract matter clinically. First, interruptions stabilise it. Many *HTT* alleles carry glutamine-encoding CAA codons embedded in or adjacent to the CAG run; because CAA breaks the repeat's structural periodicity without changing the encoded protein, an allele's behaviour is governed by the length of its longest *uninterrupted* CAG run rather than by total polyglutamine \[3, 6\]. Second, length begets length: longer uninterrupted tracts form larger and more persistent slip-outs, so the expansion rate accelerates as the tract grows — a positive feedback that is central to the threshold behaviour described in Section 5.

### 2.2 Mismatch repair: the machinery, in brief

Post-replicative mismatch repair exists to correct base–base mismatches and small insertion–deletion loops left behind by DNA polymerase. It operates in two steps. **Recognition** is performed by MutS homologue heterodimers: MutSα (MSH2–MSH6) handles base–base mismatches and single-nucleotide loops, while **MutSβ (MSH2–MSH3)** specialises in larger insertion–deletion loops — exactly the lesion a CAG slip-out presents. On binding, and on exchanging ADP for ATP, MutSβ converts from a DNA-bending recognition complex into a sliding clamp that can diffuse along the duplex; cryo-electron microscopy of human MutSβ has now resolved the DNA-free, (CAG)2-bound, sliding-clamp and ATP-bound conformations, confirming that nucleotide state and heteroduplex recognition are allosterically coupled \[18\].

**Licensing and incision** are performed by MutL homologue heterodimers, each a latent endonuclease that is activated by the loaded MutS complex: MutLα (MLH1–PMS2), MutLγ (MLH1–MLH3), and MLH1–PMS1, whose canonical repair role in humans has long been obscure. The incision permits excision and resynthesis of the nascent strand. In canonical MMR this restores the correct sequence. At a repeat tract it does not — for reasons taken up in Section 4.

### 2.3 Why neurons are the vulnerable compartment

Repeat expansion is often assumed to be a replication error, but the cells that matter in HD do not divide. Medium spiny neurons of the striatum are post-mitotic for the whole of adult life, and it is precisely in them that the *HTT* tract expands fastest. Expansion in non-dividing cells must therefore be driven by repair-associated, not replication-associated, DNA synthesis — a short resynthesis patch templated on a slipped intermediate. This explains why MMR gene dosage, rather than proliferation rate, governs the process, and why the phenomenon is shared across the CAG·CTG, CGG·CCG and GAA·TTC repeat disorders \[16\].

## 3. The human genetic case for somatic expansion

### 3.1 Modifier loci converge on DNA maintenance

The decisive evidence is human and unbiased. Association studies of HD age at onset, conducted independently of any mechanistic hypothesis, returned DNA repair genes. *FAN1* at chromosome 15 gave a bidirectional signal, with increased expression associated with delayed onset and slower progression \[2\]. The same variants behaved consistently across the polyglutamine spinocerebellar ataxias: in a cohort of 1,462 individuals with HD or polyQ SCA, DNA repair genes as a group associated with onset at *p* = 1.43 × 10⁻⁵, with *FAN1* rs3512 and *PMS2* rs1805323 significant individually and in the same direction as in HD \[1\]. A modifier mechanism shared by diseases whose only common feature is an expanded CAG tract is difficult to explain by protein toxicity and easy to explain by repeat instability.

### 3.2 CAG repeat length, not polyglutamine length

The GeM-HD Consortium tested this directly by exploiting naturally occurring CAA interruptions, which alter the uninterrupted CAG run while leaving the polyglutamine tract unchanged. Onset tracked the DNA sequence, not the protein: the uninterrupted CAG repeat determines the rate at which HD develops, and onset showed no significant association with *HTT* cis-eQTLs, but was influenced — sometimes sex-specifically — by variation at multiple DNA maintenance genes \[3\]. The authors' conclusion was blunt: the finding "question\[s\] the fundamental premise that polyglutamine length determines the rate of pathogenesis in the 'polyglutamine disorders'". Sequencing-based work in TRACK-HD and Enroll-HD reached the same endpoint independently, showing that HD outcomes are best predicted by the number of pure CAGs rather than by total encoded glutamines \[6\].

### 3.3 Measured somatic expansion predicts clinical outcome

If somatic expansion is the mechanism, then individuals who expand faster should do worse. They do. Using ultra-deep sequencing of the glutamine-encoding repeat in blood DNA from 203 TRACK-HD and 531 Enroll-HD participants carrying 40–50 repeats, Ciosi and colleagues derived a per-individual somatic expansion score and found a one-unit increase associated with a Cox hazard ratio for motor onset of 3.05 (95% CI 1.94–4.80) \[6\]. Those same expansion scores were themselves associated with variants in *FAN1* (*p*FDR = 4.8 × 10⁻⁹), *MLH3*, *MLH1* and *MSH3* — closing the loop from genotype, through a measurable molecular intermediate, to clinical outcome.

The *MSH3* signal has an unusually clean molecular explanation. The top progression-associated variant from an earlier GWAS turned out to be an alignment artefact; resequencing showed that the real signal is a polymorphic 9-base-pair tandem repeat in *MSH3* exon 1. The three-repeat allele is associated with a reduced rate of somatic CAG·CTG expansion (*p* = 0.004), delayed onset (*p* = 0.003) and slower progression (*p* = 3.86 × 10⁻⁷) — in both HD and myotonic dystrophy type 1, and with a corresponding effect on *MSH3* expression \[4\]. A common human allele that lowers MSH3 activity is, in effect, a natural experiment in the therapy proposed in Section 6.

### 3.4 Modifiers act with tissue and domain specificity

The picture is not uniform. Predicting disease landmarks from integrated motor and cognitive measures revealed that individual modifiers act preferentially in one clinical domain: common variants at *MSH3* and *FAN1* differ in their relative weight on motor versus cognitive decline, and additional effects were identified at *PMS1* and *PMS2* \[5\]. A 2025 study comparing GWAS of somatic expansion measured in blood with GWAS of expansion-driven clinical phenotypes went further, reporting shared modifiers (*MSH3*, *PMS2*, *FAN1*) alongside cell-type-specific and trans-acting effects, plus two instructive dissociations: a synonymous CAG-adjacent variant in *HTT* that markedly hastens motor onset without increasing somatic expansion, and a cis-acting 5′-UTR variant that promotes blood repeat expansion without influencing clinical HD \[8\]. Somatic expansion is the dominant driver; it is not the only one, and expansion in blood is an imperfect surrogate for expansion in neurons.

## 4. Mechanism: how a repair pathway becomes a mutator

### 4.1 MutSβ initiates rather than prevents expansion

The central irony of repeat biology is that MMR, whose function elsewhere is anti-mutagenic, drives expansion at disease-associated repeats \[16\]. The prevailing model runs as follows. A slip-out forms in the tract. MutSβ recognises it as an insertion–deletion loop and loads. But instead of directing excision of the *extra* repeat units, the repair reaction removes and resynthesises the *opposite* strand, using the looped strand as template — so the loop is not excised but fixed into the duplex. Each completed cycle adds a small number of repeats, typically one to three. Because the substrate regenerates itself, the reaction iterates for the life of the cell (Figure 1).

Two lines of evidence tie this specifically to MutSβ rather than to MMR generally. Structurally, human MutSβ adopts distinct conformations on (CAG)2 DNA, including a sliding-clamp form, indicating genuine substrate engagement rather than incidental binding \[18\]. Functionally, the rate of expansion scales almost exactly with MSH3 protein abundance: in *Hdh*<sup>Q111</sup> mice treated with divalent siRNAs across a dose range, the relationship between prevention of somatic expansion and MSH3 protein level was linear with a proportionality constant of approximately 1 — MSH3 is rate-limiting, with no threshold or reserve \[13\].

### 4.2 MutL heterodimers, and the unexpected importance of PMS1

Which MutL complex licenses the incision is not a detail — it determines which protein can be drugged. A CRISPR-interference platform in HD patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells carrying more than 125 CAG repeats, and in iPSC-derived striatal medium spiny neuron cultures, allowed each MMR factor to be knocked down individually. Reducing MSH2, MSH3 or MLH1 slowed expansion most; reducing PMS1, PMS2 or MLH3 slowed it to a lesser but real degree; and — importantly for translation — lowering expression only to levels achievable by current therapeutic modalities was sufficient to slow expansion measurably \[12\].

Mouse genetics sharpened the ranking and produced a surprise. Nine HD GWAS/MMR genes were knocked out in Q140 *Htt* knock-in mice. Loss of *Msh3* or *Pms1* strongly rescued the triad of striatal phenotypes — somatic expansion, transcriptional dysregulation, and mutant huntingtin aggregation — while *Msh2* and *Mlh1* loss rescued them moderately \[9\]. That *Pms1*, whose canonical MMR function in humans is poorly defined, should rank alongside *Msh3* was unexpected, and it is the direct origin of the clinical compound discussed in Section 6.3.

### 4.3 FAN1 sets the brake

FAN1 is the one modifier that protects. Increased FAN1 expression associates with delayed onset and slower progression in patients; overexpression reduces CAG expansion in human cells, and knockdown increases it in patient-derived stem cells and differentiated medium spiny neurons, in a concentration- and repeat-length-dependent manner. Unexpectedly, FAN1's nuclease activity is dispensable for this protection \[2\].

The mechanism was resolved by identifying a highly conserved SPYF motif at the FAN1 N-terminus that binds MLH1. The current model gives FAN1 two separable functions: it sequesters MLH1, restricting its recruitment by MSH3 and thereby preventing assembly of the functional MMR complex that would otherwise drive expansion, and it independently promotes accurate repair through its nuclease domain \[10\]. FAN1 is thus a competitive inhibitor of complex assembly — which makes stabilising or mimicking the SPYF–MLH1 interaction an attractive, if undeveloped, therapeutic concept.

### 4.4 Context-dependent inputs

Expansion rate is also modulated by the neuronal environment. CRISPR/Cas9 knockdown of TDP-43 in the striatum of HD knock-in mice promoted CAG expansion, accompanied by increased *Msh3* and *Mlh1* expression; suppressing *Msh3* and *Mlh1* in turn diminished the expansion \[17\]. Because nuclear TDP-43 loss is a feature of several neurodegenerative conditions, this suggests a route by which unrelated proteinopathies could accelerate repeat instability, and a possible contribution to the cell-type specificity of expansion rate.

## 5. From an expanding repeat to a dying neuron

### 5.1 Reading repeat length one nucleus at a time

The mechanistic account above leaves the central question open: why do neurons carrying the mutation from conception remain healthy for four decades? The answer came from a method that measures the *HTT* CAG repeat length in a single nucleus alongside that nucleus's genome-wide RNA expression. Applied to human post-mortem striatum, it showed that the repeat expands in striatal projection neurons from an inherited 40–45 to 100–500 or more CAGs. Expansion from 40 to about 150 CAGs had no apparent cell-autonomous effect: those neurons were transcriptionally normal. Neurons carrying 150–500+ CAGs, by contrast, lost first the positive and then the negative features of neuronal identity, de-repressed senescence and apoptosis genes, and were lost \[11\].

The implication reframes the disease. At any one time most neurons in an HD brain carry an innocuous but unstable *HTT* gene; the pathogenic event is rare, abrupt and asynchronous, and HD is for almost all of a neuron's life a DNA process rather than a protein process (Figure 3). A striatum losing neurons steadily over years is therefore not a population declining in unison but a population in which individual cells cross a threshold at different times.

### 5.2 The same threshold in cortex

Striatal and cortical degeneration in HD differ in timing and severity, and cortical loss has often been treated as secondary to striatal loss, or vice versa, given the dense corticostriatal projection. Analysis of more than 130,000 nuclei from 12 cortical areas of HD donors argues otherwise. Cortical and striatal neurodegeneration follow analogous, cell-autonomous sequences, each instructed by somatic expansion of that neuron's own repeat; the ≈150-CAG toxicity threshold is shared across striatal and cortical projection neurons of all types; and cortical area, layer and axonal projection act only as proxies for the true variable, which is an up to 50-fold difference between pyramidal neuron subtypes in the probability of reaching the threshold within a human lifespan \[19\]. This work is currently a preprint and has not completed peer review, but if it holds, containing expansion below the threshold would protect both regions by the same mechanism.

### 5.3 Rate, threshold and phenotype in mouse models

Mouse models convert the threshold model into measurable kinetics. In purified Q140 medium spiny neuron nuclei, modal repeat length increases with striking linearity at 8.8 repeats per month (R = 0.98). Heterozygous *Msh3* deletion reduces this to 2.3 repeats per month and homozygous deletion to 0.3 — a near-complete arrest (Figure 2). *Msh3*- or *Pms1*-deficient animals keep somatic repeat length below 150 and, correspondingly, show no mutant huntingtin aggregation; Q140 mice homozygous for *Msh3* knockout lack visible brain aggregates even at 20 months. *Msh3* deficiency also corrects synaptic marker loss, astrogliosis and locomotor impairment, and ameliorates open-chromatin dysregulation \[9, 14\].

The mouse work also imposes an important caveat on model selection. The widely used zQ175 knock-in line carries approximately 185 CAGs — already above the human toxicity threshold — so somatic expansion in that model operates on a tract that has, in the terms of Section 5.1, already crossed the line. A systematic analysis of the CAG repeat threshold for therapeutics targeting somatic instability concluded that the pathogenic threshold in brain is considerably longer than the (CAG)40 measured in blood and that model choice must reflect this if candidate therapies are to be evaluated meaningfully \[15\].

One result usefully complicates the picture. In *Hdh*<sup>Q111</sup> mice, a 75% reduction in MSH3 protein proportionally reduced somatic expansion yet left striatal nuclear huntingtin aggregates unchanged; the same study detected a nuclear *Msh3* mRNA pool inaccessible to RNA interference, and compensatory upregulation of MSH6 protein following MSH3 knockdown \[13\]. Slowing expansion and reversing established pathology are not the same endpoint, and partial knockdown may not be sufficient for the latter.

## 6. Therapeutic implications

### 6.1 The target rationale, and why MSH3 came first

The therapeutic logic follows directly from Sections 3–5. If onset timing is set by the rate at which a neuron's repeat approaches a toxicity threshold, then reducing that rate should postpone onset, and doing so before the threshold is approached should be more valuable than doing so afterwards. This is a fundamentally different proposition from huntingtin lowering, which addresses the protein consequence; the two are complementary rather than competing.

Among the modifiers, *MSH3* has attracted most attention for a specific safety reason. Suppressing a mismatch repair gene for decades raises an obvious concern about microsatellite instability and cancer risk. But *MSH3* is relatively tolerant of loss-of-function variation in humans, and unlike other MMR genes, nullizygosity for *MSH3* does not cause the malignancies associated with mismatch repair deficiency \[14, 15\]. The clinical genetics literature is consistent with this: heterozygous *MSH3* defects alone do not produce a Lynch syndrome phenotype, although biallelic defects have been reported in adenomatous polyposis and heterozygous variants may aggravate an *MSH6* phenotype in affected families \[20\]. The margin is real but not unlimited, and chronic partial reduction in adults is a different exposure from germline nullizygosity. Reassuringly, divalent siRNA silencing of *Msh3* in two HD mouse models blocked striatal CAG expansion without affecting tumour-associated microsatellite instability or the mRNA expression of other MMR genes \[7\], and bulk RNA sequencing of *MSH3*-targeting antisense oligonucleotide-treated neurons showed a safe transcriptional profile even at greater than 95% knockdown \[14\].

### 6.2 Nucleic acid modalities: antisense oligonucleotides and divalent siRNA

An *MSH3*-targeting antisense oligonucleotide engaged its RNA target in iPSC-derived striatal neurons from a patient carrying 125 CAG repeats, producing dose-dependent MSH3 reduction and stalling CAG expansion. Maximal knockdown also slowed expansion in striatal neurons engineered for an accelerated expansion rate by CRISPR-Cas9 *FAN1* knockout in the same line — a stringent test, since removing the protective brake maximises the rate the drug must overcome. A knock-in mouse expressing the human *MSH3* gene confirmed effective *in vivo* reduction after ASO treatment \[14\].

In parallel, a fully chemically modified siRNA silencing *Msh3*, synthesised in a divalent scaffold that improves distribution in the central nervous system, blocked CAG expansion in the striatum of two HD mouse models \[7\]. Dose–response work with the same chemistry established the near-linear MSH3–expansion relationship described earlier, and flagged the two practical caveats — an RNAi-inaccessible nuclear mRNA pool and MSH6 upregulation — that a clinical programme will need to address \[13\].

### 6.3 Small molecules and the first clinical test of the hypothesis

Nucleic acid therapeutics for the brain require intrathecal or intraparenchymal delivery. An orally available small molecule would be preferable, particularly for a treatment likely to be started in pre-manifest carriers and continued for decades. SKY-0515, an oral small-molecule mRNA splicing modulator, is the first agent to bring the somatic expansion hypothesis into randomised clinical testing. It lowers *HTT* mRNA, and thus both wild-type and mutant huntingtin, and additionally lowers PMS1 — the protein identified as a strong expansion driver in the Q140 knockout series \[9\]. The sponsor reports dose-dependent reduction of *HTT* and *PMS1* mRNA in healthy volunteers with acceptable tolerability. Falcon-HD (NCT06873334), a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2/3 dose-ranging study, enrolled 120 participants aged 25 or older with genetically confirmed HD (CAG ≥ 40) across sites in Australia and New Zealand, with co-primary endpoints of change in blood mutant huntingtin, MRI brain volume, and UHDRS over 12 months; a larger 400-participant study (NCT07378644) began in January 2026.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, because SKY-0515 lowers both *HTT* and *PMS1*, a positive result will not by itself isolate the contribution of expansion suppression. Second, none of the primary endpoints directly measures somatic expansion in brain; the field's central quantity remains, for now, clinically unobservable.

### 6.4 Structure-guided discovery

Direct inhibition of MutSβ — rather than reduction of MSH3 abundance — would allow acute, reversible and dose-titratable control, which is attractive given the chronic exposure envisaged. The cryo-EM structures of human MutSβ in its DNA-free, (CAG)2-bound, sliding-clamp and ATP-bound states provide the necessary foundation, identifying conformational transitions that a small molecule could trap or block \[18\]. An inhibitor that prevented the recognition-to-clamp transition, or that blocked MutL recruitment without disabling MutSα-dependent repair, would be the most selective intervention yet proposed.

## 7. Current research and open questions

Five questions determine whether this mechanism becomes a medicine.

### 7.1 How early must treatment begin?

If the threshold model is right, then a therapy started after motor onset is acting on a population in which a substantial fraction of vulnerable neurons have already crossed 150 CAGs, and in which the surviving neurons are the ones nearest the threshold. The value of expansion suppression should therefore be greatest in pre-manifest carriers and should fall as the disease advances. This makes the pivotal trial expensive, long, and dependent on a surrogate endpoint — and it means a null result in manifest patients would not refute the hypothesis.

### 7.2 How will expansion be measured in a living brain?

Somatic expansion in blood is measurable, predicts outcome \[6\], and is already used as a modifier phenotype in GWAS \[8\]. But the same 2025 analysis identified a variant that promotes blood expansion without influencing clinical HD \[8\], and expansion rates differ markedly between cell types \[19\]. Blood is therefore a pharmacodynamic readout of target engagement, not a biomarker of the process that kills neurons. Whether a cerebrospinal fluid measure — for example, repeat-length distributions in neuron-derived cell-free DNA — can bridge this gap is, on the evidence retrieved here, unresolved.

### 7.3 What is the safe depth and duration of MMR suppression?

The *MSH3* safety argument rests on human loss-of-function tolerance and on mouse data showing no change in tumour-associated microsatellite instability \[7, 14\]. Both are reassuring for *MSH3* specifically. They do not transfer automatically to *PMS1*, *MLH1* or *MSH2*, where MMR deficiency is unambiguously oncogenic, and they say little about decades of continuous exposure beginning in a person's thirties. The compensatory MSH6 upregulation seen after MSH3 knockdown \[13\] is a reminder that the network adapts.

### 7.4 Does slowing expansion reverse anything?

The most sobering datum in this literature is that a 75% MSH3 reduction, which proportionally slowed expansion, left existing striatal huntingtin aggregates unchanged \[13\]. Expansion suppression is plausibly preventive rather than restorative. Whether neurons that have crossed the threshold but not yet died can be rescued, and whether the transcriptional collapse described by single-nucleus analysis \[11\] is reversible, are open.

### 7.5 How far does this generalise?

Mismatch repair drives expansion at CAG·CTG, CGG·CCG and GAA·TTC repeats \[16\]; DNA repair variants modify onset across HD and the polyglutamine spinocerebellar ataxias \[1\]; and the *MSH3* exon-1 variant modifies both HD and myotonic dystrophy type 1 \[4\]. More than forty human disorders are caused by expanded tandem repeats. If expansion rate is the common clock, a single mechanism-based therapy could in principle address a large group of otherwise unrelated diseases — the most consequential implication of this work, and the least tested.

## 8. Conclusion

Huntington's disease has been reinterpreted. The inherited CAG repeat is not the disease genotype but its seed; the genotype that matters is the one a given neuron has reached, and the disease is for most of a neuron's life an ongoing mutational process rather than a proteinopathy. Human genetics pointed to this conclusion first, through the improbable convergence of onset modifiers on mismatch repair and the demonstration that pure CAG length, not polyglutamine length, sets the clock. Single-cell measurement in human brain supplied the missing quantitative link: a shared toxicity threshold near 150 repeats, approached at cell-type-specific rates, crossed asynchronously, and followed by rapid loss of neuronal identity. Mouse genetics supplied the tractable variable: expansion rate, which scales with MSH3 dosage and collapses when *Msh3* or *Pms1* is removed.

What follows is a therapeutic hypothesis of unusual clarity — hold the repeat below the threshold and the neuron survives — attached to a set of unusually difficult trial problems: the treatment should be given before symptoms, the mechanism cannot yet be measured where it matters, and the first compound in randomised testing perturbs two targets at once. The hypothesis is, at last, in the clinic. Whether the brake can be applied early enough, deeply enough and safely enough to change a person's trajectory is the question the next decade will answer.
