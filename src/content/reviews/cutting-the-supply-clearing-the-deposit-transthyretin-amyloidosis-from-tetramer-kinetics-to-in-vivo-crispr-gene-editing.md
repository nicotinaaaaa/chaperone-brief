*A review of molecular pathogenesis, the TTR-directed therapeutic landscape, and the emerging discordance between amyloid depletion and clinical outcome*

Prepared 23 November 2026 · Sources retrieved from PubMed/PubMed Central and ClinicalTrials.gov

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Abstract

Transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis (ATTR) is a systemic protein-misfolding disease in which a normally benign plasma transport protein dissociates, misfolds and deposits as amyloid fibrils, most consequentially in the myocardium and peripheral nerves. Once regarded as rare and untreatable, ATTR has become one of the most instructive case studies in modern molecular medicine: the biophysics of a single tetramer-dissociation step has been translated, within roughly two decades, into four distinct and clinically validated therapeutic strategies — kinetic stabilisation of the native tetramer, RNA-mediated knockdown of hepatic TTR synthesis, permanent CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of the TTR gene in hepatocytes, and antibody-mediated phagocytic clearance of deposited fibrils. This review traces that arc. It begins with TTR structure and physiology and the rate-limiting dissociation step that gates amyloidogenesis, incorporates cryo-electron microscopy structures of patient-derived fibrils that reframed how misfolding is thought to proceed in vivo, and summarises the epidemiology and diagnosis of wild-type and variant disease. It then evaluates the therapeutic landscape trial by trial, with emphasis on the first-in-human in vivo CRISPR programme (NTLA-2001 / nexiguran ziclumeran), which achieved approximately 90% sustained serum TTR reduction after a single infusion. Finally, it examines the central tension now facing the field: in 2026 the phase 3 CARDIO-TTRansform trial of eplontersen missed its primary clinical endpoint despite an embedded cardiac magnetic resonance substudy demonstrating substantial myocardial amyloid regression in the same trial. Reconciling profound biochemical and imaging responses with heterogeneous clinical benefit is the defining open question for the next generation of ATTR therapeutics, and it carries directly into the ongoing phase 3 readouts for gene editing and amyloid depletion.

**Keywords:** transthyretin · ATTR amyloidosis · protein misfolding · cryo-EM · CRISPR-Cas9 · lipid nanoparticle · gene silencing · cardiac amyloidosis · amyloid depletion

## 1. Introduction

Amyloidoses are diseases of protein conformation: a soluble precursor that normally performs an unremarkable physiological job adopts an alternative β-sheet-rich fold, assembles into fibrils, and accumulates extracellularly until organ architecture and function fail. Transthyretin amyloidosis is the most common systemic amyloidosis caused by a non-immunoglobulin precursor, and it exists in two forms that share a final common pathway. Hereditary or variant ATTR (ATTRv) arises from pathogenic variants in the TTR gene and affects an estimated 50,000 people worldwide; wild-type ATTR (ATTRwt), in which structurally normal TTR misfolds as a function of age, may affect as many as 500,000 \[1\]. Untreated, median survival from diagnosis is approximately two to six years \[1\], and the disease spans cardiology, neurology, orthopaedics and ophthalmology — amyloid cardiomyopathy and progressive sensorimotor and autonomic neuropathy dominate the clinical picture, but carpal tunnel syndrome, lumbar spinal stenosis, vitreous opacities and leptomeningeal involvement also occur \[2\].

What makes ATTR scientifically compelling is the directness of the line from biophysics to therapy. The rate-limiting step in amyloidogenesis — dissociation of the TTR tetramer into monomers — is a measurable kinetic quantity, and that measurement supported the rational design of small molecules that occupy the thyroxine-binding sites and raise the activation barrier for dissociation \[4,5\]. A second strategy reasoned one level upstream: if the precursor itself can be removed from the circulation, the cascade cannot begin. Liver transplantation was the crude original implementation of that logic \[4\]; RNA interference and antisense oligonucleotides implemented it pharmacologically; and CRISPR-Cas9 delivered to hepatocytes by lipid nanoparticle now implements it permanently, in a single intravenous dose \[20,21\]. A third strategy, newest of all, concedes that preventing further deposition may not be enough for patients who already carry a heavy amyloid burden, and uses monoclonal antibodies to opsonise existing deposits for phagocytic removal \[22\].

This review examines each of these strategies against the molecular biology that motivates them, and closes on the question the field must now answer: whether lowering or clearing transthyretin reliably translates into longer, better lives for patients with established cardiomyopathy.

## 2. Transthyretin: structure, synthesis and physiological function

Transthyretin is a 127-amino-acid, β-sheet-rich polypeptide that assembles into a homotetramer, effectively a dimer of dimers, with two funnel-shaped thyroxine-binding sites at the dimer–dimer interface \[4,7\]. It is synthesised principally by the liver, with smaller contributions from the choroid plexus (the source of TTR in cerebrospinal fluid) and the retinal pigment epithelium (the source in the eye) \[3\]. Its canonical physiological role is transport: TTR is a minor carrier of thyroxine in plasma and the principal carrier of retinol-binding protein, and therefore of vitamin A \[3,4\].

That division of synthetic labour has direct therapeutic consequences. Hepatic TTR is the substrate for systemic amyloid deposition in heart and nerve, and liver-directed therapies — RNA silencers, antisense oligonucleotides and lipid-nanoparticle-delivered gene editors — reach it efficiently. TTR produced behind the blood–brain and blood–retinal barriers is not meaningfully reduced by these agents, which is why ocular and leptomeningeal amyloidosis can progress in patients whose circulating TTR has been almost abolished \[2,3\]. Vitamin A handling is the second consequence: because retinol transport depends on TTR, the phase 3 MAGNITUDE gene-editing trial excludes patients unable or unwilling to take vitamin A supplementation for the duration of the study \[31\].

Beyond transport, evidence from knockout animals suggests TTR may have additional roles in neuroprotection and in promoting neurite outgrowth after injury \[3\]. These functions are incompletely characterised, and their relevance to patients rendered essentially TTR-null by a single irreversible gene-editing dose remains an open and non-trivial question.

## 3. Molecular pathogenesis

### 3.1 Kinetic stability and the rate-limiting dissociation step

TTR amyloidogenesis requires tetramer dissociation; the assembled tetramer is not amyloidogenic, and the liberated monomer is \[4\]. Crucially, the governing parameter is kinetic rather than thermodynamic: what matters is how fast the tetramer comes apart, not merely how favourable the assembled state is at equilibrium. Amyloidogenic TTR variants generally destabilise the tetramer, accelerate its dissociation, or both \[4\]. This framing produced a concrete drug-design hypothesis — a ligand that binds the native tetramer more tightly than it binds the dissociative transition state will raise the activation barrier and kinetically trap the protein in its harmless assembled form — and tafamidis was designed on exactly that principle \[4\].

The hypothesis is testable in patients. Subunit-exchange assays, in which tagged TTR homotetramers are spiked into plasma and the rate of subunit mixing is followed over time, allow kinetic stability of endogenous TTR to be quantified directly in human plasma rather than inferred from recombinant protein in buffer \[5\]. Such assays make pharmacodynamic stabilisation a measurable endpoint, and they underpin the claim that acoramidis achieves greater than 90% stabilisation across the dosing interval ex vivo \[15\].

### 3.2 Proteolysis, shear stress and the mechano-enzymatic pathway

Unfolding alone does not explain the composition of amyloid found in patients. Ex vivo cardiac deposits are enriched for a C-terminal fragment spanning residues 49–127, which implicates proteolytic cleavage as a mechanistic step rather than an incidental postscript \[6\]. Work on the S52P variant showed that cleavage and subsequent fibrillogenesis require biomechanical force of the kind supplied by physiological fluid shear stress — a mechano-enzymatic mechanism that would be most pronounced where shear is greatest, which is to say in the heart, and which fits the observed abundance of the 49–127 fragment in cardiac amyloid \[6\]. Two further findings from that work bear on therapy: the protective, non-amyloidogenic T119M variant was neither cleaved nor fibrillogenic under the same conditions, and existing stabilisers inhibited proteolysis-mediated fibrillogenesis completely only when both thyroxine-binding sites were occupied \[6\].

### 3.3 What patient-derived fibril structures revealed

Cryo-electron microscopy of fibrils extracted from patient tissue, rather than grown in vitro, substantially revised the mechanistic picture. A 2.97 Å reconstruction of fibrils from the hearts of two patients with Val30Met ATTR amyloidosis showed a single protofilament built from two non-homologous TTR segments — an N-terminal fragment (Pro11–Lys35) and a C-terminal fragment (Gly57–Thr123) — arranged in a compact, spearhead-shaped cross-section around a water-filled internal cavity, with thirteen β-strands in exclusively parallel strand–strand register \[7\]. The fibril fold bears little resemblance to the native TTR protomer, which contains nine β-strands in largely antiparallel arrangement plus a single α-helix; the two conformations are not related by local rearrangement \[7\].

Two inferences follow. First, because the fibril fold is so far from the native fold, the precursor must be substantially unfolded before it assembles — consistent with the kinetic-stability model \[7\]. Second, and less expected, the spatial arrangement of the N- and C-terminal segments within a single fibril layer leaves room for the intervening residues 36–56, and full-length TTR is detectable in the extracts. The most parsimonious reading is therefore that fibril formation precedes proteolysis: unfolded full-length chains assemble into an early fibril state, and cleavage within the solvent-exposed, low-amyloidogenicity segment at residues 36–56 subsequently converts that state into the mature, N-terminally truncated fibril \[7\]. A later 2.78 Å structure of ATTRwt fibrils from a non-hereditary cardiac case showed a strikingly similar spearhead cross-section formed by the same N- and C-terminal fragments, indicating that wild-type and variant disease converge on a common fibril architecture despite their different genetics and clinical patterns \[8\].

## 4. Clinical phenotypes, epidemiology and diagnosis

### 4.1 Who has ATTR

ATTRwt is a disease of ageing hearts and is substantially under-recognised. In a prospective study of 120 consecutive patients aged 60 years or older admitted with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and left ventricular hypertrophy, 13.3% had moderate-to-severe myocardial uptake on 99mTc-DPD scintigraphy; all tested negative for TTR variants, and endomyocardial biopsy confirmed ATTRwt in all four patients biopsied \[9\]. Clinical overlap with other causes of HFpEF was high, although ATTRwt patients had higher NT-proBNP and troponin I, greater maximal wall thickness and more pacemakers \[9\].

ATTRv epidemiology is dominated in some populations by a small number of founder variants. The V122I variant (p.Val142Ile) is carried by roughly 3.1% of individuals of African ancestry in US biobank cohorts and is associated with heart failure (adjusted odds ratio 1.7–1.8) \[10\]. The diagnostic gap is stark: among V122I carriers with heart failure, only 11% carried a clinical diagnosis of hereditary ATTR cardiomyopathy, with a median of three years from symptom onset to diagnosis \[10\]. V122I remains the most common variant genotype in contemporary ATTR-CM trial populations \[24\].

### 4.2 Diagnosis and staging

The diagnostic pathway for cardiac ATTR is unusual in modern medicine in that it has largely dispensed with biopsy. In a multicentre study of 1,217 patients with suspected cardiac amyloidosis, myocardial radiotracer uptake on bone scintigraphy was greater than 99% sensitive and 86% specific for cardiac ATTR amyloid, with false positives arising almost exclusively from patients with cardiac light-chain amyloidosis \[11\]. Combining grade 2–3 cardiac uptake with the absence of a monoclonal protein therefore supports a non-biopsy diagnosis, and histological confirmation remains the cornerstone for other amyloid types \[11,12\].

Prognosis is captured by a two-biomarker staging system built on NT-proBNP and estimated glomerular filtration rate. In 869 patients at the UK National Amyloidosis Centre, with external validation in 318 French patients, median survival was 69.2 months in stage I, 46.7 months in stage II and 24.1 months in stage III \[13\] (Figure 2).

### 5.1 Kinetic stabilisers

ATTR-ACT established the principle that slowing tetramer dissociation changes outcomes. Among 441 patients randomised to tafamidis 80 mg, tafamidis 20 mg or placebo for 30 months, all-cause mortality was 29.5% with tafamidis versus 42.9% with placebo (hazard ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.51–0.96), with a relative risk ratio of 0.68 for cardiovascular hospitalisation and slower decline in six-minute walk distance and quality of life \[14\].

Acoramidis, a higher-affinity stabiliser achieving greater than 90% stabilisation across the dosing interval ex vivo, was tested in 632 patients in ATTRibute-CM using a four-step hierarchical analysis of death, cardiovascular hospitalisation, NT-proBNP and six-minute walk distance. The analysis favoured acoramidis with a win ratio of 1.8 (95% CI 1.4–2.2); death and cardiovascular hospitalisation together contributed 58% of pairwise comparisons, and NT-proBNP yielded the highest ratio of wins to losses (23.3% versus 7.0%) \[15\].

### 5.2 RNA interference and antisense knockdown

Silencing hepatic TTR synthesis removes the precursor rather than stabilising it. In HELIOS-B, 655 patients with ATTR-CM were randomised to vutrisiran 25 mg subcutaneously every 12 weeks or placebo for up to 36 months. Vutrisiran reduced the composite of all-cause death and recurrent cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.72, 95% CI 0.56–0.93; 0.67 in the monotherapy population not receiving background tafamidis) and all-cause death through 42 months (hazard ratio 0.65, 95% CI 0.46–0.90), while preserving six-minute walk distance (least-squares mean difference 26.5 m) and KCCQ-OS score (5.8 points) \[17\].

APOLLO-B, testing patisiran in 360 patients over 12 months, met its primary endpoint — the decline in six-minute walk distance was smaller with patisiran (median difference 14.69 m, 95% CI 0.69–28.69) — and showed a 3.7-point KCCQ-OS advantage, but did not demonstrate significant benefit on the composite endpoint of death, cardiovascular events and functional decline \[16\]. In ATTRv polyneuropathy, eplontersen reduced serum TTR by 81.7% at week 65 versus 11.2% in a historical placebo group, with large benefits on the mNIS+7 neuropathy score (difference −24.8) and Norfolk quality-of-life score (difference −19.7) \[18\]; in the cardiomyopathy subgroup of that trial, left ventricular ejection fraction and stroke volume improved relative to historical placebo \[19\].

### 5.3 In vivo CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of TTR

Gene editing pushes the knockdown logic to its endpoint: instead of repeatedly degrading TTR mRNA, disable the gene once. NTLA-2001 (nexiguran ziclumeran, nex-z) is a lipid nanoparticle encapsulating messenger RNA encoding Cas9 together with a single guide RNA targeting TTR; the nanoparticle is taken up by hepatocytes, where transient Cas9 expression introduces a double-strand break whose error-prone repair disrupts the coding sequence \[20\]. Because hepatocytes are long-lived and the edit is heritable within the cell lineage, a single intravenous infusion is intended to produce lifelong TTR reduction.

The first-in-human study enrolled six patients with ATTRv polyneuropathy at 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg. Adverse events were few and mild, and mean serum TTR fell by 52% (range 47–56) and 87% (range 80–96) respectively by day 28 \[20\]. A cardiomyopathy cohort followed: twelve patients dosed at 0.7 or 1.0 mg/kg achieved mean reductions exceeding 90% at day 28, sustained through four to six months, with two transient infusion reactions, one of grade 3 \[1\]. The mature phase 1 dataset, reported in 36 patients with ATTR-CM followed for at least 12 months — half in NYHA class III and 31% with variant disease — showed a mean serum TTR reduction of 89% (95% CI −92 to −87) at day 28 and 90% (95% CI −93 to −87) at 12 months \[21\]. Five patients had transient infusion-related reactions and two had transient liver-enzyme elevations judged treatment-related; serious adverse events, mostly consistent with the underlying cardiomyopathy, occurred in 14 patients \[21\].

Exploratory clinical signals were encouraging but preliminary: the geometric mean factor change from baseline at 12 months was 1.02 for NT-proBNP and 0.95 for high-sensitivity troponin T, median six-minute walk distance changed by 5 m (IQR −33 to 49), and 92% of patients had stable or improved NYHA class \[21\]. In a disease whose untreated natural history is relentless progression, stability is itself notable — but a single-arm phase 1 study cannot distinguish stability from regression to the mean, and the definitive test is underway.

### 5.4 Amyloid depleters: removing what is already there

Every strategy above acts on the precursor. None removes amyloid that has already been deposited, and in a patient presenting with stage III cardiomyopathy the deposited burden is the problem. NI006 (now ALXN2220) is a recombinant human anti-ATTR antibody designed to flag existing deposits for phagocytic immune cells \[22\]. In a phase 1 double-blind trial, 40 patients with wild-type or variant ATTR cardiomyopathy and chronic heart failure received ascending doses from 0.3 to 60 mg/kg every four weeks. No apparent drug-related serious adverse events occurred and no anti-drug antibodies were detected; at doses of at least 10 mg/kg, cardiac tracer uptake on scintigraphy and extracellular volume on cardiac magnetic resonance — both imaging surrogates of amyloid load — appeared to fall over 12 months, as did median NT-proBNP and troponin T \[22\]. A mechanistic pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic model, built before the trial, had predicted 10–60 mg/kg monthly as the efficacious range and proved consistent with the observed imaging results, a useful demonstration of quantitative translational pharmacology in a mechanism without precedent in cardiology \[23\].

Depletion is now in large-scale testing: ALXN2220 in a 1,182-patient phase 3 trial, in combination with eplontersen in a phase 2b study, and a competing anti-ATTR antibody programme in the 1,280-patient CLEOPATTRA trial \[35,36,37\]. The combination design is the conceptually interesting one, since it pairs precursor removal with deposit clearance — in principle the first regimen that could both stop new deposition and reverse the existing burden.

## 6. The depletion–outcome discordance

The field's assumption has been that deeper and earlier TTR reduction will produce proportionally greater clinical benefit. In 2026 that assumption met a serious challenge. CARDIO-TTRansform randomised 1,432 patients with ATTR-CM to eplontersen 45 mg or placebo every four weeks for 140 weeks — the largest ATTR-CM trial conducted to date \[27\], enrolling a contemporary population with a wide range of disease severity in which 57.2% were already receiving a TTR stabiliser at baseline \[24\]. The primary endpoint, a cumulative composite of cardiovascular death and recurrent cardiovascular events, was not met: 381 events in 210 eplontersen patients (29.4%) versus 392 events in 231 placebo patients (32.2%), rate ratio 0.89 (95% CI 0.73–1.09; P = 0.28) \[25\]. The composite of all-cause death and cardiovascular events gave a rate ratio of 0.86 (95% CI 0.71–1.04) \[25\].

What makes this result genuinely puzzling, rather than simply disappointing, is the imaging substudy embedded in the same trial. In 120 participants undergoing serial multiparametric cardiac magnetic resonance, eplontersen produced lower extracellular volume at week 140 than placebo by 6.7 percentage points (95% CI −8.7 to −4.7), lower native T1 by 25.9 ms, lower left ventricular mass by 19.1 g, and higher left and right ventricular ejection fractions (+5.1% and +4.6%) with improved strain in multiple directions \[26\]. Myocardial amyloid regression, defined as a fall in extracellular volume of at least 5 percentage points, occurred in 12.5% of eplontersen recipients and 0% of placebo recipients; progression occurred in 12.5% versus 76% \[26\]. By every imaging measure, sustained TTR suppression modified the myocardial disease.

Several explanations deserve consideration, and they are not mutually exclusive. The CMR substudy population differed from the main trial: only 12.5% were on a background stabiliser at baseline, compared with 57.2% overall \[24,26\], so the imaging cohort had more room to demonstrate an untreated-disease contrast. High background stabiliser use in the main trial would compress any incremental effect of adding TTR suppression. Second, the event-driven primary endpoint in a population with mean age 76.4 years may be substantially driven by competing non-amyloid cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular morbidity that no TTR-directed therapy can influence \[24\]. Third, and most important mechanistically, imaging regression of amyloid may take longer to convert into functional and clinical benefit than a 140-week trial can capture — myocardial fibrosis, conduction-system damage and extracardiac deposition accumulated over years are not necessarily reversible merely because new deposition has stopped. Fourth, the discordance may partly reflect endpoint selection: HELIOS-B, which showed benefit with vutrisiran, and CARDIO-TTRansform used different composite definitions and follow-up windows \[17,25\].

Whatever the resolution, the practical consequence is immediate. Serum TTR reduction is a pharmacodynamic marker, not a validated surrogate endpoint, and the degree of TTR lowering achieved by an agent cannot be used to predict its clinical effect. The ~90% reduction achieved by a single dose of nex-z \[21\] is a striking biochemical result whose clinical meaning will be settled only by MAGNITUDE \[31\].

## 7. Open questions

- Durability and genomic safety of one-shot editing. A permanent edit cannot be titrated, paused or withdrawn if an unanticipated consequence of lifelong TTR deficiency emerges. Durability at 12 months is established \[21\]; durability and safety at 20 years is the question that the long-term follow-up protocol running to 2038 is designed to answer \[33\].

- Extrahepatic transthyretin. Liver-directed therapies do not reduce TTR produced by the choroid plexus or retinal pigment epithelium \[3\], so ocular and leptomeningeal amyloidosis remain potential late phenotypes in long-lived patients whose systemic disease is controlled \[2\]. As systemic therapy improves survival, these compartments may become more clinically prominent, not less.

- Consequences of near-total TTR deficiency. Vitamin A supplementation addresses the known transport deficit \[31\], but the putative neuroprotective and neurite-outgrowth functions of TTR \[3\] have not been systematically assessed in patients maintained at ~10% of normal TTR for years.

- Reversibility of established organ damage. The CMR data show amyloid can regress \[26\]; whether a heart with years of infiltration, interstitial expansion and conduction disease recovers function in proportion to that regression is unresolved, and is the strongest argument for treating patients earlier in the course.

- Combination strategy and sequencing. If precursor removal and deposit clearance address different components of the disease, the rational regimen may be both — the hypothesis now under test in the eplontersen-plus-ALXN2220 phase 2b study \[36\]. How stabilisers, silencers, editors and depleters should be sequenced or combined, and in whom, is entirely unsettled.

- Trial endpoints and the surrogate problem. The discordance between imaging response and clinical outcome \[25,26\] indicates that neither serum TTR nor extracellular volume is yet a validated surrogate. Establishing which measurable response predicts survival would materially accelerate the field.

- Diagnosis and equity. Only 11% of V122I carriers with heart failure had a recorded diagnosis of hereditary ATTR cardiomyopathy, with a three-year median delay \[10\], while contemporary trial populations remain 88.8% White and 90.6% male \[24,25\]. Therapies that work only for patients who are identified in time will not reach the populations with the highest variant burden.

## 8. Conclusion

Transthyretin amyloidosis has become the clearest demonstration available that a quantitative understanding of protein biophysics can be converted into medicine. The rate-limiting dissociation of a tetramer, measured in plasma by subunit exchange, yielded stabilisers that reduce mortality; the recognition that the liver is the source of the amyloidogenic precursor yielded silencers and then, with lipid-nanoparticle-delivered CRISPR-Cas9, the first in vivo gene-editing therapeutic to achieve near-complete and apparently durable knockout of a disease-causing gene after a single infusion. Cryo-EM structures of fibrils taken from patients, rather than made in a test tube, revised the misfolding mechanism itself and showed wild-type and variant disease converging on a common architecture.

The next phase of the field will be defined less by how deeply transthyretin can be lowered than by what that lowering achieves. The 2026 CARDIO-TTRansform result — a failed clinical primary endpoint alongside unambiguous imaging evidence of myocardial amyloid regression in the same trial — is a caution against treating pharmacodynamic depth as a proxy for benefit, and a reminder that late-stage infiltrative disease may have an irreversible component. The phase 3 readouts due from 2027 for gene editing and amyloid depletion will determine whether permanent precursor elimination, active clearance of existing deposits, or their combination can convert a biochemical triumph into the clinical one that patients need.
