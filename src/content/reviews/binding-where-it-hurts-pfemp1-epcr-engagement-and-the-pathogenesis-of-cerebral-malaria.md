*A review of the parasite adhesion phenotype that links Plasmodium falciparum cytoadhesion to brain endothelial injury*

Prepared 9 November 2026 · Literature retrieved from PubMed/PubMed Central, bioRxiv and ClinicalTrials.gov

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Abstract

Cerebral malaria (CM) is the most lethal syndrome caused by *Plasmodium falciparum*, killing 15–25% of affected children even where parenteral artesunate and modern supportive care are available. Its defining lesion is the sequestration of infected erythrocytes (IEs) in the cerebral microvasculature, mediated by the variant surface antigen *P. falciparum* erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1). Over the past fifteen years a specific adhesive phenotype has emerged from this heterogeneous family: PfEMP1 variants carrying cysteine-rich interdomain region α1 (CIDRα1) domains that bind the endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR). This review synthesises the molecular, clinical and histopathological evidence tying EPCR-binding PfEMP1 to cerebral disease. EPCR is not a passive docking site but the cofactor for protein C activation and for activated protein C (APC) signalling through protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1); CIDRα1 binding is therefore simultaneously an adhesion event and a targeted sabotage of the brain endothelium’s principal anticoagulant and barrier-protective pathway. Parasites expressing these variants dominate the *var* transcriptome of children with severe malaria, their transcript abundance scales with disease severity, and a subfamily that binds intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and EPCR simultaneously is further enriched in CM and drives parasite uptake into, and swelling of, brain endothelial cells. Downstream, loss of EPCR and thrombomodulin, fibrin deposition and blood–brain barrier failure converge on the brain swelling that is the strongest imaging predictor of death. The axis is attractive therapeutically — through binding-inhibitory CIDRα1 antibodies, engineered soluble EPCR decoys, or restoration of cytoprotective signalling — but every candidate must contend with CIDRα1 sequence diversity, the short therapeutic window of an acute encephalopathy, and the fact that the lethal event may already have occurred by the time a child reaches hospital. We outline where the evidence is strong, where it conflicts, and which questions most need answering.

**Keywords:** *Plasmodium falciparum*; cerebral malaria; PfEMP1; endothelial protein C receptor; CIDRα1; cytoadhesion; blood–brain barrier; brain swelling; adjunctive therapy.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The clinical problem

Cerebral malaria is defined clinically as an unrousable coma (Blantyre or Glasgow coma score below the age-appropriate threshold) in a patient with *P. falciparum* parasitaemia in whom other causes of encephalopathy have been excluded. Despite the replacement of quinine by parenteral artesunate — which reduced in-hospital mortality in African children with severe malaria from 10.9% to 8.5% in the AQUAMAT trial \[17\] — case fatality in CM specifically has remained stubbornly in the 15–25% range, and a substantial minority of survivors are left with epilepsy, motor deficits or cognitive impairment \[14,22,23\]. This gap between effective parasite killing and unchanged neurological outcome is the central motivation for mechanistic work on CM: by the time a child presents, the damage is being done by host–parasite interactions in the cerebral microvasculature that an antimalarial drug does not directly reverse.

The pathological hallmark, recognised since the earliest autopsy series, is the packing of cerebral capillaries and venules with parasitised erythrocytes. What has changed is our understanding of why the brain, among all the organs in which IEs sequester, is so vulnerable, and which of the roughly sixty antigenically distinct adhesion molecules a single parasite genome encodes is responsible.

### 1.2 Scope

This review focuses on one adhesion phenotype — PfEMP1 binding to EPCR — and follows it from gene to bedside: the genetics and architecture of the *var* multigene family; the structural basis of EPCR engagement; the clinical epidemiology linking EPCR-binding variants to CM; the cellular consequences for the blood–brain barrier (BBB); and the therapeutic strategies the axis suggests. Host genetic modifiers, the contribution of rosetting and platelet-mediated clumping, and the extensive literature on the *P. berghei* ANKA experimental CM model are touched on only where they bear directly on the EPCR axis. All factual claims are referenced to primary literature retrieved from PubMed and PubMed Central; registry data are from ClinicalTrials.gov.

## 2. Biological background

### 2.1 Cytoadhesion and the intraerythrocytic cycle

*P. falciparum* merozoites invade erythrocytes and develop over roughly 48 hours through ring, trophozoite and schizont stages. From about 16 hours post-invasion the parasite exports proteins that remodel the host cell: knob-associated histidine-rich protein and other components build electron-dense knobs on the erythrocyte surface, and PfEMP1 is trafficked to and anchored within them. Mature-stage IEs therefore become adhesive and disappear from the peripheral circulation, binding to microvascular endothelium (sequestration), to uninfected erythrocytes (rosetting) and to platelets. The immediate evolutionary pay-off is avoidance of splenic clearance, since the spleen efficiently removes rigid, deformed mature IEs from the circulation; the cost, borne by the host, is microvascular obstruction and endothelial activation in whichever vascular bed the expressed variant happens to recognise \[18\].

### 2.2 The var gene family and PfEMP1 domain architecture

Each haploid *P. falciparum* genome carries approximately 50–60 *var* genes encoding PfEMP1 \[7\]. The extracellular region of each PfEMP1 is built from a variable number of Duffy-binding-like (DBL) domains and cysteine-rich interdomain regions (CIDR), followed by a transmembrane segment and a conserved cytoplasmic acidic terminal segment that tethers the molecule to the knob. Although sequence diversity across the family is extreme — a consequence of recombination and of immune selection — domain composition is highly ordered. *var* genes are classified by upstream sequence and chromosomal context into groups A, B and C. Group A genes encode PfEMP1 whose N-terminal head structure couples a DBLα1 domain to either a CIDRα1 domain or to one of the more diverse CIDRβ/γ/δ domains; groups B and C encode CD36-binding CIDRα2–6 domains \[7\]. Certain tandem domain arrangements recur across genomes and are designated domain cassettes (DCs): DC8 (DBLα2–CIDRα1.1–DBLβ12–DBLγ4) is a group A-like cassette recombined into a group B chromosomal location, while DC13 (DBLα1.7–CIDRα1.4) and DC4 (DBLα1.1/1.4–CIDRα1.6–DBLβ3) are group A cassettes \[2,5\]. ### 2.3 Mutually exclusive expression and antigenic variation

A single parasite transcribes essentially one *var* gene at a time, with the remainder held in a repressed state marked by histone H3 lysine 9 trimethylation and sequestered in perinuclear heterochromatic clusters. Switching between variants at a low per-generation rate produces the antigenic variation that sustains chronic infection and frustrates variant-specific immunity. The three-dimensional organisation of the genome is integral to this control: high-interacting regions enriched for repressive H3K36me3 and H3K9me3 marks form a transcriptionally repressive centre at telomeres and *var* loci, and deletion of the methyltransferase PfSET2 markedly reduces both intra- and inter-chromosomal interactions at these regions and reorganises *var* expression \[29\]. For the purposes of this review, the important consequence is that the adhesion phenotype of a given infection is a dynamic property of the parasite population, set by which *var* genes are being transcribed at that moment — which is precisely what transcript-profiling studies of clinical isolates measure.

### 2.4 Endothelial receptors

Three host receptors dominate the cytoadhesion literature. CD36 is bound by group B and C PfEMP1, is expressed widely on microvascular endothelium but poorly on brain endothelium, and is associated with uncomplicated infection. ICAM-1 is an inducible immunoglobulin-superfamily adhesion molecule bound by particular DBLβ domains. EPCR, the subject of this review, is a type 1 transmembrane protein of the CD1/MHC superfamily that binds the Gla domain of protein C and of APC. Early panning experiments showed that parasites selected for binding to primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells converged on a restricted set of *var* genes, including two DC8 genes that did not bind CD36, and that recombinant CIDR1 from DC8 inhibited binding of IEs to brain endothelium by about 50% — strong evidence that an unidentified brain-endothelial receptor existed and that the CIDR domain engaged it \[3\]. That receptor was identified in 2013 as EPCR \[1\].

### 2.5 The protein C–EPCR–PAR1 axis in normal physiology

EPCR’s physiological role explains why its subversion is so damaging. Thrombin generated at a site of vascular injury is captured by endothelial thrombomodulin; the thrombin–thrombomodulin complex then cleaves protein C, a reaction accelerated roughly twentyfold when protein C is presented by EPCR. The resulting APC is both anticoagulant — it proteolytically inactivates factors Va and VIIIa — and cytoprotective: while still bound to EPCR, APC cleaves PAR1 at a non-canonical site, generating a biased signal that stabilises endothelial junctions, suppresses nuclear factor κB-driven inflammatory gene expression and inhibits apoptosis. The same receptor, PAR1, cleaved instead by thrombin at its canonical site, produces the opposite, barrier-disrupting response. EPCR occupancy therefore acts as a switch that determines whether PAR1 signalling protects or damages the endothelium. Constitutive EPCR and thrombomodulin expression is comparatively low in the brain microvasculature, which may be one reason the brain is disproportionately vulnerable when this system is perturbed \[9\].

## 3. Evidence linking EPCR-binding PfEMP1 to cerebral malaria

### 3.1 Discovery of EPCR as the DC8/DC13 receptor

Turner and colleagues identified EPCR as the endothelial receptor for DC8- and DC13-expressing parasites, mapped binding to the N-terminal CIDRα1 domain, and showed that CIDRα1 interferes with protein C binding to EPCR \[1\]. The finding immediately reframed severe malaria cytoadhesion: the parasite was not merely sticking to a convenient surface protein, but to the hub of the endothelium’s anticoagulant and cytoprotective machinery. Structural work subsequently showed that although CIDRα1 domains are highly diverse in sequence, they preserve the architecture and the small set of residues required for EPCR engagement — a conserved functional core beneath a variable surface, which is both the reason immune escape is possible and the reason a cross-reactive intervention might be feasible \[18,19\].

### 3.2 var transcript epidemiology in clinical isolates

The clinical case rests principally on *var* transcript profiling of parasites taken from children at presentation. Lavstsen and colleagues compared transcripts from 88 children with severe malaria and 40 with uncomplicated malaria and found that severe syndromes — both severe malarial anaemia (SMA) and CM — were associated with high transcript levels of DC8-encoding *var* genes, with group A genes including DC13 also significantly elevated \[2\]. Jespersen and colleagues then sequenced the near-full-length transcripts dominating the *var* transcriptome in children with severe malaria and found that the only feature common to the encoded proteins was a CIDRα1 domain \[4\]. In other words, severe disease does not select a single variant or even a single cassette; it selects a binding function.

Shabani and colleagues extended this to a large Ugandan cohort (98 CM, 47 SMA, 14 asymptomatic parasitaemia) using primer sets designed from 226 sequenced field isolates \[7\]. EPCR-binding transcript levels increased monotonically with clinical severity — asymptomatic parasitaemia \< SMA \< retinopathy-negative CM \< retinopathy-positive CM — and each log<sup>10</sup> increase in transcript level was independently associated with CM rather than SMA after adjustment for parasite biomass, age, sex and nutritional status (Figure 2). Children with both CM and SMA carried the highest group A EPCR-binder transcript levels of all (Figure 3). Consistent with this, Tuikue Ndam and colleagues showed in 94 hospitalised children that IEs collected on admission from CM cases were significantly more likely to bind both EPCR and ICAM-1 than those from uncomplicated cases (P = 0.007), with higher transcript levels of CIDRα1.4 and CIDRα1.5 subclasses \[8\].

### 3.3 Dual EPCR and ICAM-1 binding

Lennartz and colleagues used structural biology to determine how a group of PfEMP1 DBLβ domains engages ICAM-1 and derived a sequence motif that predicts ICAM-1 binders from sequence alone. Searching multiple genomes with that motif revealed that ICAM-1-binding PfEMP1 almost always also carry an EPCR-binding CIDRα1, so that a single molecule can engage both receptors synergistically; expression of these predicted dual binders was associated with increased risk of CM \[5\]. This is mechanistically appealing because ICAM-1 is upregulated on inflamed brain endothelium, so dual binding would concentrate parasites precisely where the endothelium is already activated.

The association is not universal. In a Beninese cohort, Joste and colleagues found that A-type ICAM-1-binding domains were *not* associated with CM \[25\], and an earlier Beninese study found that children with CM did not develop humoral immunity against the DC8 variant IT4-VAR19 despite its EPCR and brain-endothelial binding \[24\]. Transmission intensity, age structure, case definition and the primer sets used all differ between cohorts, and some of the apparent inconsistency is methodological. The honest summary is that EPCR binding is a robust, reproducible correlate of severe disease across sites, whereas the additional contribution of ICAM-1 co-binding is well supported mechanistically but variably detected epidemiologically.

## 4. From adhesion to brain injury

### 4.1 Mechanical obstruction and parasite biomass

The crudest mechanism is physical. Sequestered IEs are rigid and adherent; packed capillaries impede flow, and local hypoxia and ischaemia follow. Sequestered parasite biomass, estimated from plasma *P. falciparum* histidine-rich protein 2 (PfHRP-2), is higher in CM than in SMA and higher still in retinopathy-positive than retinopathy-negative CM \[7\]. Yet biomass alone does not explain the syndrome: in the Ugandan cohort, *var* transcript levels remained associated with CM after adjustment for PfHRP-2, and no correlation was found between EPCR-binder transcript abundance and total or sequestered parasite biomass \[7\]. Something about the *quality* of adhesion, not merely its quantity, determines the clinical syndrome.

### 4.2 Subversion of the protein C pathway

That quality is, in large part, the loss of EPCR function. Petersen and colleagues showed that binding of the CIDRα1.1 domain to EPCR obstructs APC binding, severely impairs endothelial protein C activation, and blocks APC-mediated PAR1 cleavage and the barrier-protective effects that follow \[10\]. Critically, they also showed that an engineered soluble EPCR variant (E86A-sEPCR) binds CIDRα1.1 with high affinity, suggesting that a decoy receptor could in principle mop up the parasite ligand while leaving the host protein C pathway intact.

The in vivo counterpart came from autopsy and biopsy material. Moxon and colleagues examined brains of Malawian children who died of CM and found cerebral fibrin clots together with loss of EPCR, colocalising with sequestered IEs; using an ex vivo assay on subcutaneous microvessels they showed that the endothelial phenotype in CM is one of reduced thrombomodulin and EPCR expression \[9\]. Low constitutive expression of these regulatory molecules in brain microvasculature plausibly makes the cerebral bed uniquely intolerant of further depletion. Kessler and colleagues closed part of the loop by expressing a recombinant CIDRα1.7 domain cloned from a paediatric CM brain autopsy and showing that it inhibited the barrier-protective properties of EPCR in human brain endothelial cells in vitro \[6\].

### 4.3 Endothelial activation, parasite uptake and barrier failure

PfEMP1 binding does more than block a receptor. Using a three-dimensional spheroid model of the BBB, Adams and colleagues found that IEs expressing dual ICAM-1/EPCR-binding PfEMP1 are taken up by brain endothelial cells in an ICAM-1-dependent manner, causing endothelial cell swelling and breakdown of the barrier; parasites were subsequently identified within brain endothelium in post-mortem tissue from CM patients, indicating that this is not a culture artefact \[11\]. Endothelial ingress of parasites is a comparatively recent addition to the CM mechanism and suggests that barrier failure is partly a consequence of injury to the endothelial cell itself rather than only of junctional loosening.

Attempts to pin barrier loss on a single signalling pathway have been instructive precisely because they failed. Storm and colleagues measured transendothelial electrical resistance in human brain microvascular endothelial cells and found that PAR1 inhibitors reversed thrombin-induced barrier disruption but did *not* rescue the barrier dysfunction caused by IE lysate, implicating a PAR1-independent mechanism in the parasite-driven effect \[26\]. Any adjunctive therapy aimed at this step therefore cannot simply be a PAR1 antagonist. In vitro BBB models themselves differ substantially in the properties they reproduce — transwell monolayers, co-cultures with pericytes and astrocytes, spheroids and microfluidic chips each capture a different subset of the neurovascular unit — and the choice of model materially affects which mechanisms are detectable \[12\].

### 4.4 Brain swelling and raised intracranial pressure

The clinical endpoint of these processes is brain swelling. In the landmark Malawian MRI study, Seydel and colleagues imaged 168 children meeting a stringent retinopathy-confirmed definition of CM: 25 died, and 21 of those 25 (84%) had severe brain swelling on admission imaging, compared with 39 of 143 survivors (27%) (Figure 4) \[14\]. Serial imaging showed that swelling resolved in survivors, indicating that raised intracranial pressure in CM is transient — and therefore, in principle, survivable if the patient can be supported through it. Subsequent work refined non-invasive measurement of brain volume and showed that the amount of oedema correlates with neurological recovery as well as with mortality \[15,16\].

How swelling arises is not fully settled, and the mechanisms are probably not mutually exclusive. Candidates include vasogenic oedema from BBB breakdown, cytotoxic oedema from ischaemic injury, increased cerebral blood volume from vascular congestion, and increased interstitial fluid from impaired clearance. Experimental work in a murine model in which reversible brain swelling can be induced reliably — by a single immunisation with radiation-attenuated sporozoites before challenge — demonstrated by electron microscopy that the barrier is breached *transcellularly* rather than by tight-junction disassembly, and that this mode of disruption permits reversal while still leaving focal microhaemorrhages behind \[13\]. Whether the same holds in human disease is unknown, but it offers a coherent explanation for the clinical observation that swelling in survivors resolves without obvious infarction.

### 4.5 Contributions beyond PfEMP1

EPCR-binding PfEMP1 is a necessary part of the story but not the whole of it. Recent syntheses of paediatric CM pathogenesis place BBB impairment from local hypoxaemia, ischaemia and endothelial activation upstream, followed by oxidative stress from free haem released during haemolysis, and then by secondary injury in which tumour necrosis factor α, reactive oxygen species, CD8+ T cells and parasite toxins cross the compromised barrier and damage neurons directly; acute kidney injury and hyperuricaemia appear to worsen both barrier impairment and oedema \[22\]. Seizures, hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis and severe anaemia each contribute independently to brain injury and are the targets of most current supportive care \[23\].

## 5. The retinopathy question and case definition

Because the WHO clinical definition of CM is non-specific, a proportion of children who meet it are comatose for another reason while incidentally parasitaemic. Malarial retinopathy — retinal whitening, vessel changes and haemorrhages seen on dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy — identifies children whose coma is genuinely attributable to sequestration, and retinopathy-negative cases have historically been regarded as misclassified \[7\]. The *var* transcript data complicate that binary. In the Ugandan cohort, EPCR-binder transcript levels in retinopathy-negative CM sat consistently *between* those of retinopathy-positive CM and those of SMA, and far above those of asymptomatic parasitaemia; only CIDRα1.4/1.6a transcripts distinguished retinopathy-positive from retinopathy-negative cases \[7\]. Moreover, transcript levels did not differ between children above and below a proposed PfHRP-2 cut-off intended to identify “true” CM. The most parsimonious reading is that retinopathy-negative CM is largely a milder point on the same spectrum rather than a different disease — which matters for trial design, since excluding retinopathy-negative children removes a sizeable group in whom the parasite is probably still driving the coma.

## 6. Therapeutic implications

### 6.1 What current treatment achieves, and what it does not

Parenteral artesunate is the standard of care for severe falciparum malaria. In AQUAMAT, 5,425 African children were randomised to artesunate or quinine; in-hospital mortality was 8.5% versus 10.9% (stratified odds ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.63–0.90; p = 0.0022), with significantly less coma development, fewer convulsions, less coma-score deterioration and less post-treatment hypoglycaemia in the artesunate arm (Figure 5) \[17\]. Artemisinins act on young ring-stage parasites before they sequester, which is almost certainly why they outperform quinine. But they do not detach parasites already bound, and they do not repair an injured endothelium. Modern intensive-care management of CM accordingly concentrates on the consequences: control of seizures, glycaemic and electrolyte management, transfusion, and — where feasible — monitoring and treatment of raised intracranial pressure \[23\].

### 6.2 Targeting the PfEMP1–EPCR axis directly

Three broad strategies follow from the mechanism. The first is to block the interaction with antibodies. Because EPCR-binding CIDRα1 domains preserve a structurally conserved binding site beneath a variable surface, a vaccine or therapeutic antibody that recognises that site could in principle cover many variants. Turner and colleagues immunised with single or multiple recombinant CIDRα1 domains and found that binding-inhibitory antibodies against closely related variants were readily elicited, but antibodies against distant variants were sporadic and rarely inhibitory; sequence similarity predicted cross-inhibition poorly, implying that the cross-reactive epitopes are predominantly conformational \[19\]. Immunogen engineering to focus responses on those epitopes, or rational selection of a multivalent panel, is the obvious next step.

Natural immunity suggests the target is reachable. Peptide-microarray profiling of Malian sera showed that both adults and children preferentially recognise peptides within EPCR-binding regions over non-binding regions, with adults showing broader and more intense responses, and paediatric responses rising through a transmission season and waning afterwards \[27\]. A systems-serology analysis of Malawian children found that a combination of seven antibody targets and Fc features distinguished CM from uncomplicated malaria with 87% accuracy, and that the ability of antibodies to engage C1q and FcγRIIIb — rather than IgG titre — correlated with protection; antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis of ICAM-1/EPCR co-binding IEs was higher in uncomplicated than in cerebral malaria \[20\]. Protective humoral immunity against CM thus appears to depend on effector function as much as on binding, a consideration that should shape any PfEMP1-based vaccine design \[21\].

The second strategy is a decoy. The engineered soluble EPCR variant E86A-sEPCR binds CIDRα1.1 with high affinity and was proposed on that basis as a means of overcoming parasite-inflicted protein C system defects \[10\]. The attraction is that a decoy neutralises the ligand regardless of which CIDRα1 subtype is expressed; the difficulties are pharmacokinetic (sustaining sufficient circulating concentration against an enormous sequestered parasite biomass) and, potentially, immunological.

The third is to restore downstream cytoprotection rather than prevent binding — for example with signalling-selective APC variants that retain PAR1-biased cytoprotective activity without anticoagulant activity, thereby avoiding bleeding risk in children who may already have thrombocytopenia and microhaemorrhages. The negative result with PAR1 inhibitors \[26\] is a caution here: the parasite-driven barrier loss observed in vitro was PAR1-independent, so restoring APC–PAR1 signalling may address only part of the injury.

It is worth being explicit that the two licensed malaria vaccines act upstream of everything discussed here. R21/Matrix-M targets the circumsporozoite protein of the pre-erythrocytic stage; in a phase 3 trial across four African countries it showed 75% efficacy against first clinical malaria episode at seasonal sites and 68% at standard sites over 12 months \[28\]. Preventing blood-stage infection prevents sequestration, and at population scale this is the most important near-term intervention against CM. It is not, however, a treatment for the child already comatose, which is the gap a PfEMP1-directed therapeutic would fill.

## 7. Open questions

**Why do some children with high EPCR-binder expression develop coma and others severe anaemia?**

Transcript levels overlap substantially between CM and SMA, and children with both syndromes have the highest group A EPCR-binder levels of all \[7\]. Host factors — endothelial baseline phenotype, inflammatory set point, age, co-infection — must shape which syndrome results, and are poorly characterised.

**What explains the paradoxically low group A and CIDRα1 transcript levels in children who died?**

Shabani and colleagues found lower, not higher, group A and CIDRα1 transcripts in fatal CM despite higher parasite biomass, a pattern seen in one earlier study but not another \[7\]. Whether this reflects terminal host physiology, co-infection, sampling at a different point in the cycle, or an unmeasured variant class is unresolved.

**How much of the ICAM-1 co-binding effect is real at the population level?**

Strong mechanistic support \[5,11\] contrasts with a null association in Benin \[25\]. Harmonised primer sets, case definitions and ex vivo binding assays across sites would settle this.

**What drives barrier failure if not PAR1?**

IE-lysate-induced loss of barrier function was PAR1-independent in vitro \[26\], and parasite uptake into endothelium is ICAM-1-dependent \[11\]. The effector pathway between adhesion and junctional failure is the key gap for adjunctive drug design.

**Is brain swelling a cause of death or a marker of it?**

Swelling is strongly associated with mortality and resolves in survivors \[14–16\]; whether lowering intracranial pressure changes outcome is being tested clinically rather than assumed (Table 3).

**Can conformational cross-inhibitory epitopes on CIDRα1 be engineered into an immunogen?**

This is the pivotal question for a PfEMP1-based vaccine, and current data say it will not happen by simply mixing recombinant domains \[19\].

## 8. Conclusions

The identification of EPCR as the receptor for DC8 and DC13 PfEMP1 gave cerebral malaria research something it had lacked: a specific, testable molecular lesion that unites parasite adhesion with host pathophysiology. The evidence that EPCR-binding CIDRα1 domains matter clinically is now broad — transcript dominance in severe disease across multiple African cohorts, a severity gradient, ex vivo binding phenotypes, loss of EPCR and thrombomodulin colocalising with sequestered parasites in autopsy brain, and in vitro reproduction of barrier failure with domains cloned from fatal cases. The weight of this evidence does not make the axis the sole driver of coma: haem-driven oxidative stress, cytokine and T-cell-mediated injury, seizures, acidosis and renal failure all contribute, and the observation that PAR1 blockade fails to rescue parasite-induced barrier loss is a reminder that the pathway from adhesion to oedema has unmapped segments.

For therapy, the practical implication is sobering but clarifying. An intervention that prevents EPCR engagement is best deployed before coma develops — which points to vaccination or prophylaxis rather than rescue therapy — while an intervention for the child already in coma must act on the downstream endothelial injury, where the mechanism is least well defined. Both routes depend on work that is currently preclinical: engineered immunogens or monoclonals that inhibit binding across the CIDRα1 repertoire, and a clearer account of how a bound parasite breaks a brain capillary. Meanwhile, the most reliable gains against cerebral malaria will continue to come from preventing the infections that cause it and from supporting the child through the days in which the brain is swollen.
