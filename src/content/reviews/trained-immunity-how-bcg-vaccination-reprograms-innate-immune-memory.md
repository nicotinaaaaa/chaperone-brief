*Mechanisms, Clinical Evidence, and the Case for Trained-Immunity-Based Vaccines*

A Scientific Review — August 2026

## Abstract

For most of the twentieth century, immunological memory was considered the exclusive province of B and T lymphocytes. This view has been overturned by two decades of work showing that innate immune cells — monocytes, macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, and neutrophils, together with their bone-marrow progenitors — can also “remember” a prior microbial encounter and respond more vigorously to a second, even unrelated, challenge. This phenomenon, termed trained immunity, is best characterized in the context of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) tuberculosis vaccine, whose non-specific, disease-reducing effects on child mortality were noted as early as the 1920s but only mechanistically explained beginning in 2011. This review synthesizes the historical evidence for BCG's heterologous effects, the epigenetic (histone H3K4/H3K27 modification) and metabolic (glycolysis-favoring Akt–mTOR–HIF-1α signaling) reprogramming events that underlie training at the level of mature monocytes and bone-marrow hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, and the clinical trial evidence for BCG-induced protection against heterologous viral infection, including a randomized yellow-fever challenge study and multiple COVID-19 trials. We also discuss the double-edged nature of trained immunity, including its exploitation in bladder cancer immunotherapy versus its potential contribution to chronic inflammatory disease and altered pregnancy outcomes in animal models, and close with open questions guiding the design of next-generation trained-immunity-based vaccines (TIbV).

## 1. Introduction: A Second Kind of Immunological Memory

Vertebrate immunity is classically divided into two arms. Adaptive immunity, carried out by B and T lymphocytes bearing somatically rearranged antigen receptors, is slow to develop on first encounter but generates long-lived, antigen-specific memory cells that mount a faster and larger response upon re-exposure to the same pathogen — the principle underlying essentially all conventional vaccines. Innate immunity, by contrast, has long been viewed as a rapid but non-specific first line of defense mediated by germline-encoded pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors, with no capacity to “learn” from past infections \[1\].

This dichotomy began to erode when epidemiologists observed that some live-attenuated vaccines — most notably BCG, but also measles and oral polio vaccines — reduce all-cause childhood mortality by more than can be explained by protection against their target pathogens alone \[10\]. As early as 1928, Pearl reported a lower incidence of cancer among tuberculosis patients at autopsy, and by the 1960s multiple groups had shown that BCG-infected animals resisted unrelated pathogens through mechanisms independent of T and B lymphocytes \[1\]. These “non-specific effects” (NSEs) resisted mechanistic explanation for decades. In a landmark 2012 study, Kleinnijenhuis and colleagues demonstrated that BCG vaccination in healthy human volunteers durably increased monocyte cytokine responses to unrelated bacterial and fungal stimuli via NOD2 receptor engagement and histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3), coining the modern concept of “trained immunity” \[7\]. This review traces the biological mechanisms that have since been elucidated, the clinical evidence for and against BCG's heterologous protective effects, and the translational effort now underway to design vaccines that deliberately harness this innate memory.

## 3. Molecular Mechanisms of BCG-Induced Trained Immunity

### 3.1 Receptor Engagement and Initial Signaling

Trained immunity is initiated when muramyl dipeptide, a peptidoglycan fragment of the mycobacterial cell wall, engages the intracellular pattern-recognition receptor NOD2 on monocytes; β-glucan from Candida albicans acts analogously through the C-type lectin receptor Dectin-1 \[7,10\]. Receptor engagement triggers a signaling cascade that converges on two interlinked reprogramming processes — epigenetic and metabolic — detailed below and summarized schematically in Figure 1.

### 3.2 Epigenetic Reprogramming

BCG exposure deposits activating histone marks, chiefly H3K4me3 and H3K27 acetylation, at the promoters and enhancers of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes including TNF, IL6, and IL1B in monocytes \[7\]. These chromatin marks persist after the initiating stimulus has cleared and after cytokine transcription has returned to baseline, so that a subsequent, unrelated stimulus produces a faster and larger transcriptional response than in an untrained cell. Genome-wide epigenetic reprogramming of this kind has been documented directly in human volunteers following BCG vaccination, including durable changes in circulating monocytes and, separately, in neutrophils, where enhanced antimicrobial function and H3K4me3 marks persist for at least three months post-vaccination \[5,6\]. Trained macrophages additionally upregulate surface pattern-recognition and costimulatory molecules (TLR4, CD11b, CD14, CD40), consistent with a heightened state of activation-readiness \[1\].

### 3.3 Metabolic Reprogramming

Epigenetic remodeling does not occur in isolation: it is tightly coupled to a shift in central cellular metabolism. Arts et al. showed that BCG-trained monocytes shift from oxidative phosphorylation toward aerobic glycolysis — a Warburg-like effect — through activation of the Akt–mTOR–HIF-1α axis, and that glutamine catabolism and cholesterol synthesis pathways are concurrently upregulated \[1\]. This is not merely a bioenergetic side effect: glycolytic and tricarboxylic-acid-cycle intermediates (e.g., acetyl-CoA, succinate) serve as direct substrates and cofactors for the histone-modifying enzymes that install the epigenetic marks described above, creating a self-reinforcing metabolic–epigenetic loop. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of this metabolic shift (for example, with the mTOR-linked glycolysis inhibitor metformin) blunts both the histone modifications and the enhanced cytokine responses of trained monocytes, directly demonstrating the causal dependency of epigenetic training on metabolic rewiring \[1\].

### 3.4 Central Training at the Hematopoietic Stem and Progenitor Cell Level

A key unresolved puzzle was how training could persist for months when mature monocytes survive in circulation for only one to three days. Kaufmann et al. resolved this in a mouse model by showing that BCG reaches the bone marrow directly, where it alters the transcriptional landscape of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and multipotent progenitors (MPPs), biasing hematopoiesis toward myelopoiesis at the expense of lymphopoiesis; macrophages subsequently differentiated from these “educated” HSCs retained epigenetic reprogramming and provided superior protection against virulent Mycobacterium tuberculosis challenge compared with naïve macrophages \[2\]. Cirovic et al. confirmed an analogous process in human BCG vaccinees, identifying the transcription factors HNF1A and HNF1B as key regulators of a persistent myeloid transcriptional program within the HSPC compartment, corroborated by elevated granulocyte counts in BCG-vaccinated infants and by epigenetic remodeling detectable in peripheral CD14+ monocytes three months after vaccination \[4\]. Interestingly, training is not confined to the bone marrow–blood axis: Jeyanathan et al. demonstrated that parenteral (subcutaneous) BCG vaccination also generates long-lived, trained alveolar macrophages in the lung, but through an indirect route — mycobacterial dissemination alters the gut microbiome and circulating metabolites, which in turn reprogram tissue-resident macrophages at the distal mucosal surface (the “gut–lung axis”).

## 4. Cell Types That Exhibit Trained Immunity

Monocytes and macrophages remain the best-characterized trained cell type, but the phenomenon extends further. NK cells trained by BCG produce elevated IL-1β and IL-6 \[1\]. Neutrophils, traditionally viewed as short-lived and terminally differentiated, show functional reprogramming lasting at least three months after BCG vaccination, with increased expression of activation markers and antimicrobial capacity linked to genome-wide H3K4me3 changes \[5\]. Tissue-resident macrophage populations, including alveolar macrophages in the lung, can also be trained despite never directly encountering the vaccine at that site, through the indirect gut–lung signaling route described above. Because training ultimately resides at the level of self-renewing bone-marrow progenitors, all of these mature cell types are thought to derive at least part of their trained phenotype from a common upstream HSPC reprogramming event \[2,4\].

## 5. Clinical and Translational Evidence

### 5.1 Heterologous Protection Against Infection

The clinical case for trained immunity rests heavily on observational and randomized data showing that BCG reduces mortality and morbidity from infections unrelated to tuberculosis. West African cohort studies found that BCG-vaccinated infants had lower all-cause mortality than unvaccinated peers, an effect partly explained by protection against neonatal sepsis and respiratory infections \[1\]. The strongest mechanistic human evidence comes from a randomized, placebo-controlled experimental challenge study by Arts et al., in which healthy volunteers were vaccinated with BCG or placebo and then challenged with an attenuated yellow fever vaccine virus: BCG recipients showed significantly reduced viremia, an effect that correlated tightly with BCG-induced upregulation of IL-1β, a signature cytokine of trained immunity, rather than with the antigen-specific interferon-γ response \[6\].

### 5.2 COVID-19 Trials

The proposed heterologous antiviral effect of BCG motivated a wave of randomized controlled trials during the COVID-19 pandemic, several of which are summarized in Table 3. These included the BCG-CORONA trial in Dutch health-care workers (testing reduction in absenteeism and clinical severity) and the large Guinea-Bissau BCG-strain comparison trial, alongside additional trials in the elderly and in health-care workers in the United States and Brazil. Results across this trial program have been mixed, and a rigorous meta-analytic consensus on efficacy specifically against COVID-19 has not been established; Chen et al. note explicitly that “the protective effect of BCG was not found in another analysis,” underscoring that population characteristics, BCG strain, and prior mycobacterial exposure likely all modulate outcomes \[1\].

### 5.3 Cancer Immunotherapy

The oldest and most firmly established clinical application of BCG-induced innate training is oncological rather than infectious: intravesical BCG, introduced by Morales and colleagues in 1976, remains the standard adjuvant therapy for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer nearly fifty years later \[1\]. Its mechanism is now understood to substantially overlap with trained immunity — local instillation recruits and trains innate immune cells within the bladder wall, enhancing anti-tumor cytotoxicity. Efforts to engineer recombinant BCG strains expressing higher levels of key pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) molecules aim to potentiate this trained-immunity-dependent anti-tumor effect further \[1\].

### 5.4 Trained-Immunity-Based Vaccines (TIbV)

The translational goal now emerging from this literature is the deliberate design of “trained-immunity-based vaccines” that pair a PRR-engaging adjuvant with conventional antigen-specific components to elicit both arms of memory simultaneously \[3,11\]. Baydemir et al. argue that incorporating TIbV principles into existing platforms could be particularly valuable for populations with blunted adaptive responses, such as the elderly and immunocompromised, and could extend the breadth of protection conferred by a single vaccination against antigenically unrelated pathogens \[11\]. Moorlag et al.'s 2024 multi-omics study adds an important nuance: BCG vaccination enhanced innate responses specifically in individuals who began in a relatively “dormant” immune state, rather than uniformly boosting immunity across all vaccinees, implying that TIbV efficacy may need to be personalized based on an individual's baseline epigenetic and immune profile \[15\].

## 6. Potential Harms and Context-Dependence

Trained immunity is not uniformly beneficial. Because the trained phenotype is broadly pro-inflammatory rather than pathogen-specific, chronic or repeated activation could, in principle, exacerbate inflammatory disease; Chen et al. note evidence linking trained immunity to the progression of chronic inflammatory conditions such as atherosclerosis \[1\]. In a mouse model of pregnancy, Dang et al. found that pre-conceptional BCG training altered the natural killer cell and macrophage composition of the maternal–fetal interface and caused fetal growth restriction, despite not worsening fetal resorption rates — a cautionary finding for the timing of BCG-based interventions relative to pregnancy \[12\]. These findings collectively argue that trained immunity should be viewed as a tunable, context-dependent state rather than an unconditionally protective one, reinforcing the need for careful trial design (dose, timing, and population selection) in any therapeutic application.

## 7. Discussion and Open Questions

Three open questions dominate the current trained-immunity research agenda. First, durability and reversibility: while functional training has been documented for at least three months in circulating monocytes and neutrophils, and considerably longer via HSPC-level reprogramming in animal models, the precise kinetics of decay in humans, and whether periodic re-exposure is required to sustain protection, remain incompletely defined \[4,5\]. Second, inter-individual variability: the 2024 multi-omics study by Moorlag et al. profiling 323 BCG vaccinees over a 90-day time course found that training magnitude depended on each individual's baseline chromatin accessibility and immune “dormancy,” meaning trained immunity is not a uniform boost but a state-dependent phenomenon — a finding with direct implications for who is likely to benefit from BCG-based interventions and by how much \[15\]. Third, translating mechanism into policy: the mixed results of the COVID-19 BCG trial program illustrate the gap between compelling mechanistic and small-cohort challenge data (such as the yellow fever experimental infection study \[6\]) and reproducible, adequately powered clinical benefit at the population level. Bibliometric analysis of the field indicates that trained-immunity-based vaccine development is now the dominant research focus, alongside continued mechanistic work on metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk \[8\].

## 8. Conclusion

Trained immunity overturns a century-old assumption that innate immune cells cannot “learn” from experience. Beginning with century-old epidemiological hints from BCG vaccination campaigns and culminating in a detailed molecular picture of NOD2/Dectin-1 receptor engagement, H3K4me3/H3K27ac histone remodeling, Akt–mTOR–HIF-1α-driven metabolic reprogramming, and durable reprogramming of bone-marrow hematopoietic progenitors, this field has matured from an epidemiological curiosity into a mechanistically grounded and clinically active area of immunology. Its applications already span oncology (bladder cancer immunotherapy), infectious disease prevention, and next-generation vaccine design, while its double-edged potential to worsen chronic inflammatory disease or affect pregnancy outcomes demands continued caution. As trained-immunity-based vaccine platforms move toward the clinic, resolving the durability, inter-individual variability, and population-level efficacy of BCG-induced training will determine whether this century-old vaccine's second act lives up to its considerable promise.
